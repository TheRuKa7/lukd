## Luck, population and the Remainder

Luck biases physically possible uncertain outcomes within an active channel. It cannot make an impossible outcome possible, invent knowledge, reverse completed death or eliminate the need for materials and labour. The Civic allocation is a standing share of access to the system's protection, not a literal percentage chance of surviving each day. There is no universal conversion from a Civic point to probability. A specified event's alternatives, hazards, competing claims and channel determine the bias. A larger allocation gives more leverage among comparable claims but never guarantees success.

Repeated risky events exhaust a channel's contracted reserve and may require a documented replenishment. An arbitrary happy coincidence is not evidence of an invisible new power. The author must be able to identify whether an outcome arose from skill, physical cause, a declared channel or ordinary chance. Readers need the decisive distinction in a scene, not an actuarial equation before every action.

Externalities are part of a channel's claim. A road contract cannot create safety by invisibly passing a crash to a named innocent bystander; any such transfer is an illicit redirection with a traceable debit. Thale's protected districts divert reserve access and maintenance budgets from poorer districts, increasing their exposure to ordinary hazards. The narrative does not depict every fortunate event as a magical assault on a stranger.

The conserved pre-finale total includes registered allocations, public and private reserves, unallocated capacity and the hidden Remainder. Birth establishes a dependent allocation drawn from a household or district pool; the Naming at seven records a chosen practice digit, not a first soul or first entitlement to safety. Death releases a registered share through witnessed probate; dependents have a legally defined claim before the balance returns to the pool. Fraud and exclusion can obstruct that process. No birth creates luck; no death deletes it. Animal and ecosystem channels are registered collectively in watershed and habitat reserves, not as numbered human citizens. Omens do not certify that only registered human life matters.

People without paperwork still exist and retain dependent or unclaimed ordinary allocations. Their missing documents do not produce Nil's NaN. Nil alone is an unlisted self-aware account for which the merged Ledger has no ordinary identity entry. The state can misdescribe many undocumented people without making them supernatural anomalies.

The Basilica routes Hap-mediated possibilities through regional accounts. Each Dawn Tally truncates incompatible fractional claims into an unlisted holding account. Over three centuries this accumulation becomes Nil. The fractions carry traces of many lives; the underlying possibility passes through captive Hap. Nil is therefore neither Hap reincarnated nor a detached child of a god. The midpoint establishes the many-life accumulation; the finale reveals the captive source and extraction process. Divine connection confers no moral rank, unique right to rule or ownership by contributors.

Equalizing displayed allocations would not repair broken infrastructure or release captured channels. More importantly, hoarders and protected institutions control the reserves and restrict lawful transfers. The central scarcity is worsened by policy, monopoly and inaccessible Remainder capacity. The novel must not portray destitution as a natural moral necessity of mathematics.

## Political world and regional consequences

The Basilica originally won support by coordinating storm protection, trade and disaster reserves. Its present supporters include patients, transport workers and households whose predictable coverage is real. Its opposition includes municipal reformers, local keepers, reserve mutuals and separatists who disagree about how much common coordination to retain. Neither group is culturally or morally uniform.

Regional Seals are monopoly credentials. Breaking one reopens a locally accountable channel; it does not crown the winner, transfer a culture's ownership to Nil or magically settle public policy. Original records remain in the Basilica until the return. Keepers can deny access on procedural grounds and be persuaded by evidence or a bounded trial; reverence is not obtained through bodily defeat.

Hyakki's dispute divides restoration workers who need stable vows, younger spirits who want permission to change and a court terrified of further fading. In the River Empire, dock cooperatives and small lenders dispute Jinling's selective relief as well as her father's predatory claims. At the observatory, instrument crews require wages and safe weather access while scholars debate publication. Kroton has Synod adherents who maintain real shelters alongside residents excluded by its ranking. Crossroads employees rely on House wages while organizing against exclusions. These positions recur through letters, representatives and consequences after the party departs.

Jinling cancels debts under eight fen from the Exchange owner's distributable surplus, not depositor funds or workers' wages. She transfers reserve oversight to a provisional board including workers, depositors and municipal clerks. Her protected ownership stake and reputation remain contested. Relief is a funded first action, not an instant cure of class power.

Thale considered decentralized emergency allocation after discovering Hap in TR999 and rejected a Board proposal because it would dilute executive control and might fail during a storm. His concealment is a deliberate choice, not lack of an available alternative. Ivo retains the rejected proposal; the party obtains it alongside dated release records. Thale's argument remains serious because transition risk is real, but he cannot claim no other course was offered.

## The finale's mechanism and legitimacy

Positive-Sum ends the Basilica's compulsory single-format rounding and monopoly on channels. It does not promise an infinite reservoir, perfect safety, equal outcomes or the abolition of uncertainty. The equation demonstrates bounded translation between independently meaningful records, allowing possibilities the old normalization discarded to circulate without erasing their differences. Freed Hap participates but cannot command the new institutions or unilaterally seize allocations.

The final eight contributors authorize use of their protected records, not disposal of their persons or national sovereignty. Before the final journey, regional provisional councils and reserve stewards grant witnessed, revocable mandates for a bounded transition. The mandate is contested and limited; the companions do not claim universal assent. Essential local coverage continues from named emergency pools while the central channel switches over. Maeve's fitting holds the route open; Ivo's rejected decentralization proposal supplies an institutional starting point; the proofs make translation testable.

The first field test in Śūnyapura fails to preserve an event's timing despite balancing quantities. The Kroton test succeeds for a small consenting rescue team but cannot scale through a still-locked monopoly channel. By the finale the reader has seen a failure, a bounded success, a remaining barrier and the cost of transition. The final mechanism pays off this work rather than inventing a new spell.

Post-finale oversight belongs to regional reserve councils with elected resident representatives, technical stewards and independent witness records. Emergency seizures require review and expire; permanent forced Civic settlement is prohibited. A joint appeals panel compares counter-copies in public. People may refuse private contracts; essential civic protection cannot depend on purchasing a personal oath. Nil holds no hereditary office, remains a repair apprentice by choice and can refuse further examination. Thale relinquishes executive control, preserves records and faces public proceedings; tending pigeons is an image of diminished power, not acquittal. Some injuries and deaths remain irreparable. Hina's birthday occurs a year later amid continuing work.

## Meridian: a useful system that can refuse a person

Independent-looking authority is therefore vulnerable to the same excluded entry. This is a specific institutional dependency, not proof that every clerk is wicked.

Ward mechanics, clinicians and households organize mutual aid but cannot freely draw from another district's reserve. A lawful seizure requires a named claim, amount, source, warrant and appeal route. The appeal is formally real but can be inaccessible in time to a person facing Civic00. Ama's case shows that gap; her skill and testimony exist before the protagonist helps. Baker's stock, the resident's cart and Nought's limited rooms make assistance costly and finite.

The opening's three relationships demonstrate different kinds of power: a registrar can deny a credential; an employer can refuse sponsorship; a Collector can enforce a contested debit. ## Chapter 8: The Ferryman's Seam

The ferryman would not take their money until he had finished inspecting the rope.

Vesper stood on the landing with three fares in her hand. Behind them, the Wards were beginning to make the sounds of another working morning: shutters, cart wheels, a distant complaint about a delivery. Nothing in those sounds admitted that Nil had become someone worth hunting.

“We were told you left at this bell,” Vesper said.

The man bent the rope between his hands. A few pale fibres lifted from the bend.

“When the rope's ready.”

“Is it ready?”

He showed her the fibres.

“You're welcome to depart without it.”

Maeve put down her tool bundle. Nil watched her mouth twitch. Vesper closed her hand around the money and waited.

The boat was low in the water, with a little cargo awning and benches worn smooth at the middle. Its name had been repainted often enough that the letters stood higher than the wood around them. Tetsu, according to Gus's note. The man and the boat appeared equally unwilling to be hurried.

He replaced a short section of line, testing the splice with his whole weight before beckoning them forward.

“Receipt.”

Nil offered the closed review in its oilcloth. Tetsu dried his hands before touching it. He read Varro's office number, checked the route release and held the paper beside a slate nailed under the awning.

“Closed claim gets you through the drain toll,” he said. “It doesn't pay the crossing.”

“We've brought the fares.”

“And this is the slower crossing. Weather holds, Moon Pier before the night barrier. Weather doesn't, you sleep on the boat.”

Nil had slept on a chair and a roof in the last two nights. A boat sounded almost like choosing a different trade.

“Can the office stop us?” they asked.

Tetsu turned the paper back toward them. “This claim's closed. If a new order reaches the landing before I leave, I read the new order. I don't pretend a piece of paper means more than it says.”

He did not ask why the figure above Nil's head would not settle. He had seen it; his gaze had paused, then returned to the receipt.

Vesper paid. Tetsu wrote three entries, tore off the fare counterfoil and handed it to Maeve, who already had a place for paper in her folio. Nil took back the original review. Seal 1 remained separately wrapped inside their coat, where a loose hand could not mistake it for a coin.

“Tools under the bench,” Tetsu said. “Weight low. No standing when we turn out of the canal.”

Nil stepped aboard first and held the bundle while Maeve followed. They tried to take its heavier end with their right hand, forgot the scrape and hissed.

“Left,” Maeve said.

“I know.”

“Your hand seems to have missed the meeting.”

They shifted their grip. She accepted the help once it was actually help.

The boat moved before Nil felt ready for Meridian to leave them. The landing drifted sideways; the stair behind it narrowed to a grey crease. They searched the rooftops for the one on which they had slept. Every roof looked like another roof. Somewhere under them Nought was answering questions, Baker was setting out bowls, and Ama had a receipt that ought to be enough.

Nil touched the notebook beneath their coat. They had written an address for a woman in a window. They had no address of their own to give her.

“Gus knows the route,” Vesper said.

Nil looked up.

“You keep checking the paper as if it'll tell you she's followed us.”

“I said we'd remember.”

“Then remember. You don't have to carry the whole stair.”

She sat facing the canal behind them. Her hand rested near her holster without touching it. The small cut beneath her lip scar had dried to a dark line. Five live cartridges; Nil knew the count because she had made no effort to turn the gun into a mystery.

For a while they travelled between high stone walls. Water stains marked floods above the height of Nil's head. Workers on a maintenance shelf were hauling up a bent grate. One raised a hand to Tetsu; he answered without releasing the tiller.

Beyond the last lock, the canal widened into a reach of moving grey water. Nil felt the boat change its mind beneath them.

“Stay seated,” Tetsu said.

They sat more firmly, an action they would not previously have believed possible.

Maeve untied a packet of yesterday's bread. Gus had put it beside the fare receipt; Vesper had paid for it with the breakfast. Maeve broke it into three pieces and offered Tetsu a fourth from her own share.

He shook his head, then pointed with his chin to a cloth packet beneath his seat.

“You eat. Mine's there.”

They ate slowly. The bread was hard enough to require an intention. Nil watched a bird land on a half-submerged post, wobble and recover. No number hovered over it. The current did not appear to care.

“Do the river pools cover the birds?” they asked.

Tetsu glanced at the post. “Watershed reserve covers the habitat. Doesn't teach them where to perch.”

The bird abandoned the post and flew away, supplying its own opinion.

By midday the far bank had broken into low islands. Pine roots gripped stone walls. Narrow roofs appeared between trees, their ridges lifting at the ends. At one small landing a shrine bell hung above a rack of fishing floats. The bell did not ring when the wind reached it; a woman climbed onto a stool and freed the cord by hand.

“Hyakki?” Nil asked.

“Outer villages.”

“Are we nearly there?”

“Near enough to know we aren't.”

Vesper had fallen asleep with her hat tipped down, one boot braced against a bench leg. Maeve was checking the small iron pin she had saved from the forge lane. She had replaced the porter's temporary fitting before departure; this rejected piece was bent at its tip.

“Keeping it?” Nil asked.

“So I remember why it failed.”

Nil took out their washer, then reconsidered and put it away. Not every useful object required an exhibition.

Tetsu shifted the tiller. A stronger wind pushed beneath the cargo awning, and the water ahead darkened in a long uneven stripe.

“Canvas down.”

Vesper was awake at once. She rose too quickly.

“Seated,” Tetsu said, with exactly the same voice.

She sat, reached up and worked at the awning tie. The wet knot held. Maeve slid along the bench toward her.

“Not that end. It's loaded.”

“I have untied a knot before.”

“Then leave this one the pleasure of being your next.”

Maeve took the loose tail, eased it back through the loop and lowered the canvas in a controlled fold. Nil caught the corner with their left hand. Water ran off it onto their knees.

Tetsu turned the boat toward a sheltered cut between islands. A wave struck the side before they reached it. The bow rose, came down hard, and something knocked twice beneath the forward bench.

Maeve looked toward the sound. Tetsu was already looking.

“Brace pin.”

“I heard.” She lifted the bench flap. The brace beneath the small mast had worked loose at one end; each roll drove the iron against an enlarged hole. “Can't just hammer it back. It'll tear the soft edge.”

Vesper had the pin halfway into her hand before Maeve caught her wrist.

“Leave the load on it until we support the brace.”

For a breath neither moved. Nil saw the sharp reply Vesper almost chose. Then she opened her fingers.

“Tell me where.”

Maeve pointed to a short spar stowed along the side. “Under that crosspiece. Nil, fold the spare cloth twice. Not your dressing.”

They had already reached toward their right hand. They stopped and found the spare rag.

The repair took three people and most of the little space beneath the bench. Vesper held the spar where Maeve put it. Nil fed the folded cloth around the brace so the new fitting would bear against a wider surface. Maeve worked the damaged pin free, measured it against the spare stock Tetsu passed back, and chose a piece just long enough.

“Can I let go?” Vesper asked.

“Not yet.”

A wave struck. Nil's shoulder hit the bench edge. Vesper's arm trembled beneath the spar, but she kept it in place.

Maeve set the new pin, secured its retaining loop and checked the load before nodding.

“Now.”

Vesper let out a breath and lowered the spar. The brace held. Nothing brightened above anyone's head. Wood, iron and the right order of work had been sufficient.

They spent the next hour behind a stone breakwater, waiting for the gusts to ease. Tetsu bailed the water that had come aboard. Nil offered to help and was given a smaller scoop that could be used with the left hand.

When Tetsu leaned forward, the coat at his shoulder pulled across an old line of stitching. A rectangular patch had been removed there. Under its cleaner outline survived the little angled seam Nil had seen on the Collector's uniform.

They looked too long.

“Yes,” Tetsu said.

Nil set the scoop down. “Were you—”

“Used to be.”

Vesper watched the narrow opening beyond the breakwater. Maeve continued checking the brace, giving the question somewhere private to land.

“Why did you stop?” Nil asked.

Tetsu emptied his bucket over the side. “A long answer. Not a fare I sold you.”

Nil flushed. “I didn't mean—”

“I know. And you can still ask. I can still say no.”

He crouched beside the replaced line and beckoned Nil closer.

“This I can show you.”

The knot looked simple until Nil tried to copy it. Their loop came loose under a pull. Tetsu undid it without comment, put the end back in their left hand and showed where the standing part needed to lie.

“You want it to hold under weight and release when the weight comes off. Two different jobs.”

Nil tried again. This time the pull tightened the right part. Tetsu handed it back for a third attempt.

Across from them Vesper was rubbing the arm she had held under the spar.

“You were right,” she told Maeve.

“About the pin?”

“Let's start there.”

Maeve nodded, accepting exactly that much.

They reached Moon Pier after the lamps had been lit. Red lanterns hung above dark water, their reflections torn into long ribbons by the wake. Nil could hear drums somewhere inland. A procession of lights moved behind the roofs, rising and falling as if the whole street were breathing.

At the pier's upper end, a barrier lay across the shrine road. Its keeper was winding the day's chain into a locked housing.

Tetsu tied the boat to the landing and checked the knot Nil had helped make.

“Night barrier's down,” he said. “You can sleep aboard. Tide's slack here.”

Vesper looked toward the lights. “How long's the way around?”

“Longer in the dark. You don't know the water steps.”

Nil had already begun to gather the tool bundle. They set it back beneath the bench. The island would not vanish if they waited until morning. For the first time since leaving Nought's chair, they were tired enough to accept a safe place when it was offered.

Tetsu spread the spare canvas over the benches and gave them the dry side. Maeve checked the brace once more before lying down. Vesper placed her hat over her eyes.

“Nobody say first light,” she said.

Nil looked at the lantern procession beyond the roofs. One light lagged behind the others, hurried, then stopped beside a doorway to wait for something they could not see.

They thought of a woman climbing a stool to free a bell.

Under the canvas, with water making small sounds against the hull, Nil practiced Tetsu's knot until their fingers could remember it without looking.

Nil sat between Vesper and Hina. The private challenge had ended yesterday. Today their opinions were those of witnesses and travellers, not a government won by stopping three wheels correctly.

The first argument concerned the winter shelters. Ao wanted their scheduled renewals protected before the local gates opened. A restoration worker wanted the carrier's household moved ahead of the palace cabinet that had held its place in the queue for two months.

“The cabinet contains three unstable manifestations,” Ao said.

The worker looked at the list. “Then say that. Don't write cabinet work and ask me to guess who is inside.”

Ao crossed out the entry and wrote the actual care requirement, leaving the original legible beneath it.

Nil thought of the corrected spelling at Enma's desk.

The village clerk wanted to know who would pay the night dispatch worker. The households wanted to know whether accepting a local route would give the court access to their private vows. The court wanted limits on emergency withdrawals. A young spirit with a cracked lacquer wrist wanted a right to change keepers without losing the work already done.

Hina leaned forward at that.

“Write that one.”

The clerk had already begun.

A household keeper at the window shook her head. “All these new routes. How much more comes into the pool?”

“No more from opening the lock,” the village clerk said.

“Then who's waiting longer?”

Nil looked at the growing agreement. It had been easier to watch a control gauge reach zero than to answer that question.

The keeper laid out her own renewal slips. She had paid for two evening visits. The proposed dispatch rota gave her household one longer visit and a shared night call. She wanted to know what happened if two households needed that call at once.

Ofuku read the distances, then moved a delivery from late afternoon to midday. A route worker said the move would cost an extra carrying shift. The clerk found an unspent escort fee on the old road schedule; it could fund that shift if the households approved its reassignment. It could not fund a second night worker as well.

“Then write the limit,” the keeper said. “I won't sign a promise with the missing half left out.”

They wrote it. Emergency calls would be triaged by the duty keeper, with the unserved request logged and a second contact at the winter shelter. Nobody claimed that made simultaneous need harmless. The keeper kept her old renewal route for the first cycle and agreed to review the new one after seeing the dispatch record.

Hina watched her leave the signature space empty. The woman had not become an enemy by wanting a better answer.

By noon the paper held more conditions than declarations. The two existing winter shelters kept their named reserve schedules. Local restoration could use the reopening road channel under a household keeper and an independent route witness; neither could clear a transfer alone. Emergency lengths had to be logged after use and replenished from the designated mutual reserve, not silently taken from the next waiting household.

The dispatch work had a paid rota. Copies of refusals would stay at Ofuku's shrine and at the village office. Households could dispute an entry without surrendering the original tag. The agreement would be reviewed after the first service cycle, with failed renewals left visible in the record.

It was a small arrangement. It covered named roads and participating households, not every island or every vow. Several keepers refused to join until they had seen it work. Their ordinary care would continue under the existing schedule; refusal was not a reason to cut them off.

Tamamo read that line for a long time before signing.

“I will not call this permanence,” she said.

“Good,” Ofuku replied. “Someone would ask you to explain it.”

Hina made a startled sound that might have become a laugh in a room with fewer exhausted people.

The actual channel lock stood in a cupboard beside the road window. It was smaller than Nil had imagined. A single credential marked 9 occupied the central receiver; all the local requests had been routed through its exclusive clearance.

Maeve laid her grandmother's supplier rubbing beside the maintenance drawing. She did not claim it proved how the whole system ought to work. It showed which older service path remained physically present beneath the newer lock.

“This joint,” she told the route worker. “If you verify the isolation first, the local receiver can be serviced without opening a household line.”

The worker checked it himself. Maeve waited instead of reaching past him.

Ao isolated the control receiver while the existing care schedules continued through their separate contracted lines. The local keeper and village witness fitted their paired authorization plates. Each read the other's source before either turned a key.

The road gauge rose. Somewhere outside, a dispatch bell rang once.

The woman with the broken emergency seal closed her eyes.

Nothing in the room announced that history had changed. A courier outside swore because the bell had caught him carrying a sack. He set it down, took the new route slip and went out to do the work the open channel now allowed.

Only after the local receiver had completed its witnessed test did Tamamo release the exclusive office credential. Seal 9 came free into Ao's hand. The new agreement ended its monopoly use on these routes; it remained a credential recognized by the central access system, not a title to Hyakki.

Ao checked the release mark and passed it to Tamamo.

She held it toward Hina.

“For the journey you asked to make.”

Hina did not take it immediately. “Not because I've become something else?”

“No.” Tamamo's voice was very quiet. “I should not have made that the question.”

Hina accepted the metal. It was small enough to vanish inside her fist.

“Nil's carrying the other one,” she said, and turned to them. “Will you keep this with it?”

Nil wrapped the credential separately from Seal 1 and wrote its receiving mark in the notebook. The equal care they gave both objects seemed to satisfy Hina more than a solemn speech would have.

***

The carrier was able to stand in its doorway by the time they reached the market lane.

Its fingers were solid, but one ankle remained thin enough for the light to show through. The woman beside it pointed to the ankle before anyone could pronounce the repair finished.

“The route's open,” she said. “That isn't the same as the whole job being done.”

The restoration worker nodded and wrote the next visit on her household slip. Hina helped the carrier sit where the draught would not reach its damaged side.

At Ofuku's workshop the queue had become an actual row of objects and people along the courtyard wall. A woven screen needed a support frame before its vow threads could be touched. The cracked-wrist spirit wanted a different shade of lacquer from the one Ofuku had prepared. Two human keepers disputed whether a travelling repairer should be paid for the delayed day.

Ofuku turned toward Maeve. “Can you—”

“Ask her first,” Hina said, pointing at the spirit with the lacquer wrist.

Ofuku shut her mouth, looked at the spirit and began again.

“Which part do you want us to work on today?”

Maeve put the tools where the spirit could inspect them. Hina held the colour samples beside the repaired joint. Nil kept the delivery records flat and dry while Vesper took the disputed wage slips to the village clerk.

No one called it saving the world. By the end of the afternoon there were six fewer things on the urgent list and four new ones. Ofuku rubbed the side of her hand before writing the next date.

She paid Maeve for the completed stool and packing hinges, from the workshop's posted maintenance purse. Nil and Vesper received the agreed dispatch and sorting wages for that day's work. It was not much. Nil counted their coins twice before understanding that nobody was waiting to ask for them back.

“You can spend them,” Vesper said.

“I know.”

“You're holding them like they'll object.”

Nil loosened their grip. Tobi would have approved of the lesson being repeated with a different object.

Hina brought a little tin from her shelf. Inside were several coins wrapped in paper labelled with a very large moon cake.

Ofuku looked at the tin, then at the travel sheet.

“There is a household passage fund.”

“That's for deliveries.”

“And people.”

“I want to pay mine.” Hina counted the fare from the cake money and left the remainder in the tin. “Hina can have a smaller Tuesday.”

Ofuku turned away to put a bowl in the wrong cupboard. She noticed, took it out and found the right one.

The departure had become real enough that neither wanted to discuss it directly.

Nil sat at the cleared table with the Hyakki toll release. It carried the same route family mark as the drain review and Maeve's supplier attachment. For a moment the three papers seemed to fit together with the satisfying click of a repaired clasp.

“The same charge,” Nil said.

Ofuku leaned over the page. “The same route mark. Check the individual claims before you call them the same charge.”

Nil looked again. The subsidiary references differed.

“Right.”

The release did establish one thing. The Hyakki road's old clearance obligation had been pledged through the Luck Exchange at Bāfā. Opening the local route changed the collateral schedule; the Exchange had to reconcile the remaining claim. Its harbour office was named on the page, with a contact desk and a required receiving date.

They had somewhere specific to take the discrepancy. The journey was no longer simply a way to stay ahead of the next machine.

Vesper read the Exchange name and looked away before Nil could ask what she thought of it.

“A busy harbour,” she said. “We'll need to keep copies.”

Ofuku laid Enma's finding beside the toll release. His courier had brought the sealed receiving copy that morning. Ofuku read both versions against each other, then signed the second copy into the shrine's protected drawer.

The original finding remained in Enma's archive. Hina's counter-copy now had the witnessed travel seal, with the archive reference and a route for requesting a replacement. It was evidence of a specific absence, with all its limits intact.

Hina showed Nil the inner pocket again before putting it away. “Dry side. Flat. No cake crumbs.”

“An exacting standard,” Vesper said.

“For a document, yes.”

Maeve had made a simple outer guard for the sleeve, but waited until Hina asked before sliding it into place.

At the gate, Tamamo's two retainers were speaking to the dispatch worker. Ao checked the next care delivery. Aka corrected a message that had still called local clearance temporary indulgence. He crossed out the phrase where the worker could see, then wrote the agreement's actual term beneath it.

Hina stopped beside them.

“Tell her the carrier's fingers held.”

Ao nodded. “And the ankle?”

“Still needs work.”

“I will tell her both.”

Ofuku walked with them to the road bend. There she straightened Hina's sleeve, stopped herself from straightening the patch and asked whether she had packed the dry cover.

“Twice,” Hina said.

“Then take one out. You need room for your things.”

Hina threw her arms around her.

The umbrella bumped Ofuku's shoulder. She caught its edge automatically, making room rather than pushing it away. For a while neither said anything.

“You can write that you want to come home,” Ofuku said at last. “You don't have to invent a delivery.”

“You can write that you miss me.”

“I will be far too busy.”

“Then write badly.”

Ofuku's mouth moved against Hina's hair. Nil could not hear the answer. They gave the two of them a little more road.

Tetsu was waiting at the landing with a cargo bound for the outer channel and the River coast beyond it. He checked the new route release, read the separate archive dispatch address and took the fares from four passengers.

“Another one,” he said to Vesper.

“An improvement to the business.”

Hina stepped aboard, looked at the low benches and declared that the vessel met the minimum dignity required by her office. Tetsu pointed beneath the nearest seat.

“Bundle there. Dignity low in the boat.”

Nil laughed. It came easily enough to startle them.

They used the knot Tetsu had taught them to secure the tool bundle. This time they did not need to watch the whole movement. Maeve tested it and nodded.

When the landing drew away, Hina kept waving long after Ofuku had become a small white-haired figure at the road bend. The umbrella's eye stayed fixed on the shore. Nil sat beside her without asking whether she was excited.

Night reached them in the outer channel. They ate the rice packets Ofuku had packed and put on the dry covers before the air grew cold. Vesper took the first watch beside Tetsu; Maeve slept with her folio under her arm. Hina's umbrella lay open beneath the cargo awning so its eye could see the stars.

Nil put the two separately wrapped Seals beside the notebook and checked the oilcloth. They wrote down the Exchange receiving desk, the route reference and the fact that the carrier's ankle still needed work.

Then the boat's slow motion drew the words out of focus.

***

A paper lantern hung in darkness.

It had been repaired many times. Some patches were almost transparent; others carried ink from whatever page had been available. Two little dice turned behind the paper where eyes might have been. Around the lantern drifted separate digits, too slowly to form a count.

“What makes a person real?” it asked.

Nil tried to see what held it up. The darkness gave them nothing to decide from.

“A name?” they said.

The lantern waited.

Nil thought of the hairpin's unfinished book, of Ama's receipt, of Hina choosing which patch should face the door. None had begun to matter only after someone counted it correctly.

“I don't think the answer belongs to the person asking for proof,” they said.

The light moved behind the paper. It might have been a laugh. It might have been a draught.

When Nil woke, Hina was asleep against the rolled cargo cover. Her hand lay outside her sleeve. All five fingers were there.

They did not wake her to say so.

Beyond the bow, beneath a paling sky, the first River signal lamps were beginning to separate from the stars.
