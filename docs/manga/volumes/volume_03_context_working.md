## Luck, population and the Remainder

Luck biases physically possible uncertain outcomes within an active channel. It cannot make an impossible outcome possible, invent knowledge, reverse completed death or eliminate the need for materials and labour. The Civic allocation is a standing share of access to the system's protection, not a literal percentage chance of surviving each day. There is no universal conversion from a Civic point to probability. A specified event's alternatives, hazards, competing claims and channel determine the bias. A larger allocation gives more leverage among comparable claims but never guarantees success.

Repeated risky events exhaust a channel's contracted reserve and may require a documented replenishment. An arbitrary happy coincidence is not evidence of an invisible new power. The author must be able to identify whether an outcome arose from skill, physical cause, a declared channel or ordinary chance. Readers need the decisive distinction in a scene, not an actuarial equation before every action.

Externalities are part of a channel's claim. A road contract cannot create safety by invisibly passing a crash to a named innocent bystander; any such transfer is an illicit redirection with a traceable debit. Thale's protected districts divert reserve access and maintenance budgets from poorer districts, increasing their exposure to ordinary hazards. The narrative does not depict every fortunate event as a magical assault on a stranger.

The conserved pre-finale total includes registered allocations, public and private reserves, unallocated capacity and the hidden Remainder. Birth establishes a dependent allocation drawn from a household or district pool; the Naming at seven records a chosen practice digit, not a first soul or first entitlement to safety. Death releases a registered share through witnessed probate; dependents have a legally defined claim before the balance returns to the pool. Fraud and exclusion can obstruct that process. No birth creates luck; no death deletes it. Animal and ecosystem channels are registered collectively in watershed and habitat reserves, not as numbered human citizens. Omens do not certify that only registered human life matters.

People without paperwork still exist and retain dependent or unclaimed ordinary allocations. Their missing documents do not produce Nil's NaN. Nil alone is an unlisted self-aware account for which the merged Ledger has no ordinary identity entry. The state can misdescribe many undocumented people without making them supernatural anomalies.

The Basilica routes Hap-mediated possibilities through regional accounts. Each Dawn Tally truncates incompatible fractional claims into an unlisted holding account. Over three centuries this accumulation becomes Nil. The fractions carry traces of many lives; the underlying possibility passes through captive Hap. Nil is therefore neither Hap reincarnated nor a detached child of a god. The midpoint establishes the many-life accumulation; the finale reveals the captive source and extraction process. Divine connection confers no moral rank, unique right to rule or ownership by contributors.

Equalizing displayed allocations would not repair broken infrastructure or release captured channels. More importantly, hoarders and protected institutions control the reserves and restrict lawful transfers. The central scarcity is worsened by policy, monopoly and inaccessible Remainder capacity. The novel must not portray destitution as a natural moral necessity of mathematics.

## Political world and regional consequences

The Basilica originally won support by coordinating storm protection, trade and disaster reserves. Its present supporters include patients, transport workers and households whose predictable coverage is real. Its opposition includes municipal reformers, local keepers, reserve mutuals and separatists who disagree about how much common coordination to retain. Neither group is culturally or morally uniform.

Regional Seals are monopoly credentials. Breaking one reopens a locally accountable channel; it does not crown the winner, transfer a culture's ownership to Nil or magically settle public policy. Original records remain in the Basilica until the return. Keepers can deny access on procedural grounds and be persuaded by evidence or a bounded trial; reverence is not obtained through bodily defeat.

Hyakki's dispute divides restoration workers who need stable vows, younger spirits who want permission to change and a court terrified of further fading. In the River Empire, dock cooperatives and small lenders dispute Jinling's selective relief as well as her father's predatory claims. At the observatory, instrument crews require wages and safe weather access while scholars debate publication. Kroton has Synod adherents who maintain real shelters alongside residents excluded by its ranking. Crossroads employees rely on House wages while organizing against exclusions. These positions recur through letters, representatives and consequences after the party departs.

Jinling cancels debts under eight fen from the Exchange owner's distributable surplus, not depositor funds or workers' wages. She transfers reserve oversight to a provisional board including workers, depositors and municipal clerks. Her protected ownership stake and reputation remain contested. Relief is a funded first action, not an instant cure of class power.

Thale considered decentralized emergency allocation after discovering Hap in TR999 and rejected a Board proposal because it would dilute executive control and might fail during a storm. His concealment is a deliberate choice, not lack of an available alternative. Ivo retains the rejected proposal; the party obtains it alongside dated release records. Thale's argument remains serious because transition risk is real, but he cannot claim no other course was offered.

## The finale's mechanism and legitimacy

Positive-Sum ends the Basilica's compulsory single-format rounding and monopoly on channels. It does not promise an infinite reservoir, perfect safety, equal outcomes or the abolition of uncertainty. The equation demonstrates bounded translation between independently meaningful records, allowing possibilities the old normalization discarded to circulate without erasing their differences. Freed Hap participates but cannot command the new institutions or unilaterally seize allocations.

The final eight contributors authorize use of their protected records, not disposal of their persons or national sovereignty. Before the final journey, regional provisional councils and reserve stewards grant witnessed, revocable mandates for a bounded transition. The mandate is contested and limited; the companions do not claim universal assent. Essential local coverage continues from named emergency pools while the central channel switches over. Maeve's fitting holds the route open; Ivo's rejected decentralization proposal supplies an institutional starting point; the proofs make translation testable.

The first field test in Śūnyapura fails to preserve an event's timing despite balancing quantities. The Kroton test succeeds for a small consenting rescue team but cannot scale through a still-locked monopoly channel. By the finale the reader has seen a failure, a bounded success, a remaining barrier and the cost of transition. The final mechanism pays off this work rather than inventing a new spell.

Post-finale oversight belongs to regional reserve councils with elected resident representatives, technical stewards and independent witness records. Emergency seizures require review and expire; permanent forced Civic settlement is prohibited. A joint appeals panel compares counter-copies in public. People may refuse private contracts; essential civic protection cannot depend on purchasing a personal oath. Nil holds no hereditary office, remains a repair apprentice by choice and can refuse further examination. Thale relinquishes executive control, preserves records and faces public proceedings; tending pigeons is an image of diminished power, not acquittal. Some injuries and deaths remain irreparable. Hina's birthday occurs a year later amid continuing work.

## Meridian: a useful system that can refuse a person

Independent-looking authority is therefore vulnerable to the same excluded entry. This is a specific institutional dependency, not proof that every clerk is wicked.

Ward mechanics, clinicians and households organize mutual aid but cannot freely draw from another district's reserve. A lawful seizure requires a named claim, amount, source, warrant and appeal route. The appeal is formally real but can be inaccessible in time to a person facing Civic00. Ama's case shows that gap; her skill and testimony exist before the protagonist helps. Baker's stock, the resident's cart and Nought's limited rooms make assistance costly and finite.

The opening's three relationships demonstrate different kinds of power: a registrar can deny a credential; an employer can refuse sponsorship; a Collector can enforce a contested debit. # Volume Three: The Price of Shelter

## Chapter 15: Purchased Guests

The boy returned the horseshoe with a bill attached.

Nil stared at it, then at the empty fitting on their brace. They had been ashore in Bāfā for less than ten minutes.

“That was mine.”

“Still is.” The boy held out his other hand. “Message delivery. One fen.”

Vesper caught his wrist before he could retreat into the crowd. He stopped pulling as soon as he saw the gun at her hip.

“Start again,” she said.

He was small, wearing a red festival jacket that had belonged to someone with longer arms. A paper lantern on a stick bobbed over one shoulder. Its little painted windows had been patched with parcel labels.

“You're hurting.”

Vesper loosened her grip. He could have run. Instead he rubbed his wrist and waited.

“I took it,” he told Nil. “I shouldn't have. She said return it before I delivered the message.”

“Who?”

He turned the attached paper over. Beneath a pawnshop address was a neat signature and eight little bell marks.

**Ba Jinling. Your claim has changed hands. Please ask what that means before allowing anyone to escort you anywhere.**

Nil read it twice. Behind the boy the harbour spread in layers of masts, market roofs and gold-lettered account boards. A crane swung a crate above the landing. Beneath it, workers argued with a clerk who was stamping each load before the rope had stopped moving.

They had come with a receiving reference. They had been received first.

“Little Lantern,” the boy said. “That's what people call me. The delivery fee is real. The stealing wasn't part of the job.”

Maeve took the horseshoe and checked the repaired join before passing it to Nil. “Anything else missing?”

Nil checked the oilcloth beneath their coat. Seals 1 and 9 were still separately wrapped. Hina put a protective hand against the inner pocket holding Enma's finding.

“No,” Nil said.

“Good. Now put the little catch all the way down.”

They fitted the horseshoe back into its frame. It had been easy to mistake the first click for the last.

Vesper read the message. Something in the name made her thumb pause against the paper.

“I know the shop,” she said. “I don't know what she's bought.”

Little Lantern held out the delivery ledger. “She pays if you don't. I have to say that too.”

“Then she can,” Vesper said.

He nodded, writing the fee into a different column from the little deposits owed to other customers. Nil noticed the distinction because he took such care over it.

Tetsu was unloading the last of the cargo behind them. Nil went back to thank him and ask where a return message could be left.

“Harbour dispatch,” he said. “Same boat route, different clerk. Address the keeper, not the building.”

He checked the knot on the tool bundle when Maeve lifted it. Nil had tied it correctly. Tetsu did not compliment them; he simply left it alone.

They followed Little Lantern through the lower market. He kept to the broad lanes, although Nil could see quicker ways between the stalls. At the first crossing he stopped outside a public claim board.

Their description hung near the middle. Someone had copied the orange streak in their white hair as a bright slash above the account field.

Across the notice a new strip read: **ENFORCEMENT SUSPENDED PENDING OWNER'S INSTRUCTION.**

Nil stopped walking.

“Owner of what?”

Little Lantern looked toward Vesper. She did not improve the answer for him.

“The claim,” he said. “I think. Ask her.”

The pawnshop occupied the ground floor of a narrow building with a bell above the door and three different measures beside the counter. The shelves held tools, bowls, folded cloth and one magnificent hat that Vesper looked at with professional suspicion.

A woman in jade green was arguing with a supplier about a damaged hinge.

“You charged me for sound iron,” she said. “I have arranged my prejudices around receiving it.”

The man offered a discount. She asked whether the discount would hold the cupboard door up. Eventually he took the hinge away and promised another by afternoon.

Only then did she turn to the visitors.

Eight small golden bells sounded when she moved: some in her hair ornaments, some at her ankles. She had a folding fan with abacus rods for ribs and the composed expression of someone who expected a conversation to have several prices before it found an honest one.

“Jinling,” she said. “Thank you for coming before believing my paperwork.”

“It says owner,” Nil replied.

“Yes. It is an ugly word for what I have purchased.”

“What have you purchased?”

She laid three documents on the counter: the transferable bounty claim, its assignment receipt, and a dated instruction suspending enforcement. The amount had come from her private account, identified separately from the shop's customer deposits.

“The right to direct this particular claim,” she said. “Not your body, your work, your records or your decision to stay.”

Vesper read the assignment first. Maeve read the instruction. Hina peered over the counter until Jinling moved the lamp so she could see.

“Can you sell it again?” Nil asked.

Jinling took a breath. “Under its original terms, yes. Which is why I have also written this.”

The fourth document forbade transfer without Nil's consent while the claim was under her control. It did not pretend to cancel an order from another jurisdiction or guarantee that an official would obey the suspension.

“Why not cancel it entirely?”

“The issuer can repost a cancelled bounty. While I hold this one, the harbour registry must show that its enforcement is suspended. That buys an argument at the gate. I would prefer to sell you certainty, but I haven't found an honest supplier.”

Nil looked at Vesper.

“The distinction's real,” she said. “So's the risk.”

Jinling nodded toward Little Lantern. “Did you return it?”

He pointed to Nil's brace.

“And tell them?”

“Yes.”

She counted his delivery fee into his hand. “The theft goes in a different conversation. You can eat first. There's rice behind the screen.”

He hesitated, waiting for the instruction to become a demand.

“You can leave with it,” she added.

He went behind the screen. After a moment he came back with the bowl and sat where he could still see the door.

“You knew we were coming,” Nil said.

Jinling touched the Hyakki route reference Nil had placed beside the claim. “I knew this release was coming. The road obligation is pledged through the Exchange. If local clearance stays open, the old collateral schedule needs a review. My father would prefer that review take place after the next collection.”

“Your father?”

“Chancellor Jin Chan. The Exchange.”

Vesper's gaze moved to the windows.

Jinling noticed. “The shutters close from inside. Two exits, neither through my office. You may inspect them before deciding whether you want rooms.”

She offered three upstairs sleeping spaces, with a screen for the fourth person if Hina wanted her own corner. Food and lodging for three nights would come from Jinling's private hospitality budget. There was no work obligation in the guest terms. Separate paid copying work was available if they wanted it, and she would not require Nil's account signature to earn the fee.

It sounded dangerously close to everything Nil had wanted from an employer, delivered by a person who had just bought the right to say where the hunt stopped.

From behind the screen came a wooden clatter. Little Lantern had tried to stack his empty bowl without standing up. He caught two others before they fell, missed the third and stared at the crack it made against the floor.

Jinling turned at once. “Cut?”

He shook his head.

“Stay clear of the pieces.”

“I can pay.”

“I asked about your hands.”

The boy hid them inside his sleeves. Nil knew the movement: stop the other person seeing the part that might make you expensive.

Maeve fetched a little broom. Hina crouched beside the boy and inspected the floor beneath his shoe before letting him move. The umbrella's tongue pointed accusingly at the last sharp piece under the shelf.

“A formidable witness,” Jinling said, retrieving it with a dustpan.

Little Lantern produced a coin. “I broke it.”

“Yes. You don't have to buy a place at breakfast by pretending you didn't. Put the coin away. We'll move the bowls where someone can reach them without climbing over the stool.”

He kept the coin in his hand until she had gone back to the counter. Then he put it into the deposits side of his ledger pocket, paused and moved it to the little purse where he kept his own fees.

Later, while Vesper read the guest conditions, he showed Nil the harbour map hanging beside the dispatch shelf. A few alleys had been redrawn in darker ink.

“Flood doors changed,” he said. “Old maps send people into a wall.”

“You drew these?”

“I told the map clerk. She drew them. She makes me show where I mean.” He sounded mildly aggrieved by the professional delay. “Even if I know.”

Nil looked at the clear little corrections. They had come to a city full of people comparing papers and expected their experience to make them unusually useful. The boy who had stolen the horseshoe knew more about the next street than they did.

“Can you show me the receiving office?”

He put a finger beside one of the marks. “That's the public counter. The warehouse desk has the same sign, but it takes cargo accounts. Don't queue there.”

“I would have.”

“Lots do.”

He turned the map slightly so Hina could see. She had been trying to look past Nil without appearing interested.

“And the enormous food?” she asked.

“How enormous?”

She spread her hands. He considered the required scale with proper seriousness and pointed toward a market square. For the next few minutes the two of them discussed the relative honesty of festival portions. Nil stood beside them, holding no document that mattered, and discovered they did not want to leave.

On the dispatch shelf lay a half-finished lantern, its paper held open by thin ribs. Little Lantern's eyes kept returning to it.

The cooperative's witness brought its still-open loss complaint. Nil's correction remained attached. Jinling's private payment covered the immediate wage shortfall; the hearing did not call it a waiver of further claims. The worker with the healed knuckle was back at the yard. His name appeared on the injury record without requiring him to come and perform the pain for an audience.

The provisional board was argued over for most of the morning. Workers selected two temporary representatives from the people who would actually operate the draw stations. Depositors chose their own receiving witnesses. Municipal clerks held the public counter-copies. Jinling remained an owner with access and legal obligations, not an elected voice for everyone who had accepted her help.

Its first term ended after a stated service cycle unless renewed through the public process. Emergency debits needed two named approvals, a recorded purpose and an expiry. Essential wage and deposit accounts stayed outside private review stakes. The board could correct a local schedule; it could not seize the reserves of other regions or promise protection it did not fund.

A warehouse lender refused to endorse the relief release. He wanted an appeal against the source classification of some claimed surplus. The board accepted the appeal and kept the disputed portion out of the immediate distribution rather than paying it away before the question could be heard.

The remaining verified surplus funded the listed under-eight-fen discharges. Not deposits. Not wages. Not luck newly created because somebody had made a generous speech.

Jinling signed the loss of her father's sole control and her own claim to inherit it unchanged. Her pen paused at the ownership disclosure. Nil saw her look toward the quiet side door, where she could have left the room and continued buying a few debts in secret.

She kept writing.

After the board's actual service test, the exclusive office credential came out of the central receiver. Seal 8 was released under the completed review terms. The provisional local authority replaced its monopoly clearance on the participating routes; the credential remained a separate central-access key, not a crown.

The receiving clerk offered it to Jinling.

She turned to Nil. “Will you keep it with the others?”

They wrapped it separately, recorded the receiving mark and checked the three packets in order:1,9,8. The repetition was useful now. It was not an answer to what should govern the Exchange tomorrow.

The true-account extract received a protected travelling seal. Jinling would carry it; the board kept the certified archive copy and the right to challenge any use that exceeded the release. Bai Si retained the dead-count extract and Cui's independent reference. Hina's Enma finding remained in its dry sleeve, query attachment restricted until Nil chose to authorize another comparison.

Someone asked whether the witnesses intended to remain and run the new arrangement.

Jinling closed her fan.

“The records need independent measurement. I want to accompany the inquiry. The board has its own paid clerks and its own route stewards. It cannot depend on my standing in this room.”

“And your private claim purchase?” Vesper asked from the side.

Jinling brought out the bounty assignment. The suspension remained registered; its administration would pass to the named local custodian under the no-transfer condition Nil had accepted. Neither Jinling nor the board could sell it onward without Nil's permission. The national issuer could still attempt a new order. Everyone read that limit before signing.

Nil did not mistake a safer harbour for a country that could no longer hunt them.

***

Little Lantern waited outside the shop with his delivery ledger under his jacket.

He had heard Jinling was leaving. When she came through the door he asked whether that meant the rice behind the screen belonged to someone else now.

Jinling sat on the front step, making herself low enough that he did not have to look up.

“The shop will stay open. The household keeper and I have spoken about a room and regular meals, if you want them. You wouldn't pay with deliveries.”

“What would I pay with?”

“Nothing. It's a care arrangement.”

He looked down at the ledger.

“People say that before they count.”

Jinling nodded. “Then keep your own copy. Choose someone outside the shop to read it with you. The school clerk can be the second contact, if you like her.”

“She's very slow.”

“That may be a professional advantage in this conversation.”

He considered the doorway. “Can I keep the ledger?”

“Yes.”

“Do I have to keep delivering?”

“No. If you choose any work later, it needs its own safe hours and fee. Food and a bed don't depend on it.”

“What if I go out?”

“Tell the keeper where you're going. We make a safe return plan. Nobody sends you away for disliking a room.”

He sat beside her. The lantern on its stick leaned against the step.

“I want to see it first.”

Jinling stood. “Then let's look.”

Nil watched them go inside with the household keeper, who introduced herself before asking whether the boy wanted the door open. An arrangement had begun. It was not a child purchased along with a bounty, and it would need work after the visitors left.

At the back table lay a courier packet addressed through Gus's Exchange contact. Nil recognized the Wards dispatch mark. They had sent a short message on arrival; this reply had been written on Day 10 and carried by the direct cargo post. Its dates mattered. Nought knew they had reached the harbour. He knew nothing yet about the cooperative hold or the court descent.

His letter did not mysteriously answer the questions they had not sent.

He was still at the shrine. Baker had complained about the Collector standing where customers queued. Ama had delivered the repaired coat and kept both receipt copies. The laundry cart needed a new wheel. Gus had added the price and an address for contributing when Nil could afford it.

Nil read that part twice, feeling a peculiar relief at being given a concrete debt instead of a moral one with no end.

Nought had included a short statement about the release order. The Wards office acknowledged receiving it but claimed the treatment transfer still required a named receiving clinic, the patient's agreement and separately guaranteed travel coverage. A stale authorization alone had not opened Livia's stair.

Jinling compared its reference with the page bearing I.M. The order in the Exchange archive matched. She obtained a certified dated copy and sent it to Gus with a request to ask Livia what care and route she wanted. They did not write a rescue as if consent and medicine were pieces of a missing sum.

Below the order, another archive slip carried the full name behind the initials: Ivo Marr. It identified a rejected emergency-allocation proposal from TR999. Copies would be released under the existing correspondence request; the author had authorized transmission of the administrative proposal, not every classified appendix held by the Basilica.

The local clerk fetched the received copy from the public dispatch file. Ivo had not travelled to Bāfā. His handwriting had.

The proposal described regional emergency pools with shared oversight and an appeals route outside the Comptroller's sole office. It listed risks of delay and inconsistent local records. In the margin, Thale had rejected the transfer of executive control pending a level of certainty the proposal itself said no emergency system could guarantee.

Nil touched the date. “He had another option.”

“An option with costs,” Jinling said. “And people he would have had to share the decision with.”

Bai Si read the retained limits. “We should compare this with the original if we reach it.”

They copied the reference, not the conclusion that a document had solved the problem by existing.

Nought's second sheet was less official. He wrote that questions about the route papers might grow into a larger inquiry. He supplied the addresses of Open Hand contacts: a clinic, a meal room, a carrier who accepted sealed counter-copies without demanding the private account inside.

The older tradition behind the name called chance Hap. The Basilica prohibited some of its independent pledges. Nought described the tradition as people making room for an outcome they did not own, not proof of what a hidden god presently wanted.

**You do not owe the network an examination because someone gave you a chair,** he wrote. **If you need a door opened, ask for a door. We can disagree about the story above it.**

Nil looked at the notebook he had given them. They had not written the dreams into the letters. They could not tell whether his old tradition and their shifting figures belonged together, and the page did not ask them to decide it yet.

Jinling brought an invitation from the observatory's trade liaison. Her earlier request for an independent comparison had received a named instrument slot, subject to arrival and an agreement on what personal data could be used. Śūnyapura kept measurements not maintained by the Exchange or the Ten Courts. That was a reason to go, not a promise that scholars would know what Nil was.

They booked a paid place on the plateau caravan leaving before the following dawn. The board's inquiry budget covered the declared comparison journey and provisions, with personal purchases excluded. Jinling logged her own contribution separately. Nil paid the first small contribution toward the laundry cart from earned sorting wages and kept the acknowledgment.

They sent Nought a new letter with the observatory address, the public comparison references and the reason for going. Hina helped keep the restricted query attachment out of the dispatch copy. The carrier stamped it Day 13. Any answer would have to find them farther along the road; none was owed to them tonight.

Vesper stood at the room's threshold while they checked the itinerary.

“Do you still want me on the road?” she asked.

Maeve looked up. “I want you to tell us when the road goes through your past.”

“I will.”

Hina closed the sleeve around Enma's finding. “I'm not promising to be comfortable about it.”

“I know.”

Nil did not offer a verdict for Mara. “You can come. We don't decide what she owes you.”

Vesper nodded. The answer hurt; it was also more than she had been entitled to demand.

That evening Jinling went back to the hearing desk to sign her departure disclosure. Her father was still there, arguing over a warehouse assessment. When she came out, she carried the untouched little dish of preserved fruit from breakfast in a wrapped bowl.

No one asked whether she had forgiven him. She put the bowl with the road provisions.

Nil slept in the bed one last night, with three separately wrapped credentials and the paid caravan receipt beneath the notebook.

***

A little money toad sat on a counter in darkness.

It had three legs, a coin beneath its mouth and two turning dice where its eyes should have been. Separate digits drifted around it like dust caught in lamplight. The counter stretched no farther than Nil could see.

“What are you owed?” it asked.

Nil thought first of a record that would recognize them, then of the woman with the eight-fen debt and the man pinched by the capstan lever. Wanting an answer had not made their answer true.

“I don't know how to count that,” they said.

The toad waited.

“I know some things I owe. That doesn't mean everybody I help has to give me a place.”

The coin turned beneath its mouth. Nil could not tell whether it had landed on a face or merely rolled beyond the light.

When they woke, the caravan driver was knocking on the shop shutter. Maeve had already tied the tool bundle. Hina was arguing that a road breakfast could still deserve a plate.

Jinling counted five companions at the stair, then looked at Bai Si's lanterns and changed the carrying order so nobody had to force them through a narrow doorway.

Nil took the last bag without pretending it was too light to share.
