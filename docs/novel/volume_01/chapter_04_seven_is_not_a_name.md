# Volume One: A Name on Loan

## Chapter 4: Seven Is Not a Name

Nought put the empty cups in a basin and left the terminal untouched.

That helped. NIL had expected him to wipe his hands, pull a lever and begin before the courage to object could arrive. Instead he examined the front shutter, listened to the street and said, “Would you like the chair closer?”

“Does it need to be?”

“Only if you want to sit.”

NIL carried it over. The cord around its repaired leg creaked.

The terminal was smaller than the Collector's machine. Its brass had gone dull except around the handle, where years of hands had kept a patch bright. A red ribbon hung from a screw. The bow had been tied with more care than symmetry.

“Someone left that?”

“Someone wanted me to keep it.”

“Did their Naming work?”

“Yes.”

NIL waited for the comforting explanation: therefore this one would work too. Nought lifted the ribbon clear of the handle and laid it safely beside the machine.

“There are two records,” he said. “The name you offer a person, and what the city agrees to put in its book. People sometimes confuse them.”

“The city seems quite certain.”

“The city has very good paper.”

He brought out a small unused notebook. No wire connected it to the wall.

NIL looked from it to the machine. “That one doesn't connect?”

“No. I keep a few for people who need one. You can take it with you.”

“Then why write it down?”

Nought considered the question. “So I remember what you asked me to call you.”

The sound outside changed. Someone wheeled a load of empty jars past the door; each uneven paving stone rang through the glass. NIL listened until the cart had gone.

“There's something written on the decoder,” they said.

They turned their wrist. The cloth over their palm had begun to stain at its edge. Nought did not take the hand to inspect it. NIL drew back the cuff with their left fingers.

Inside the brass circle, beneath a fracture too narrow for a nail, three letters had survived the wear.

NIL.

“The clerk crossed it out.”

“In an account field?”

They nodded.

“It isn't an account number.”

“I know.” The reply came sharper than intended. “Everybody keeps telling me what it isn't.”

Nought lowered the notebook a little. “What is it to you?”

NIL had no quick answer. They had used the word while thinking: *NIL should get there before the bell. NIL should keep the washer. NIL should not look at the Hound.* An instruction could have a subject even if a form could not.

“It's been with me,” they said. “I could choose something else. Something that doesn't mean a missing box.”

“You could.”

“Would that be better?”

“I don't get to decide that.”

NIL looked down at the letters. The clerk's line was only on the paper in their pocket. It had not reached the brass. They had imagined it had.

“Nil,” they said. “For me. Not for the box.”

Nought wrote it. He used a small i and a small l.

“Nil,” he repeated, and turned the notebook so they could see.

They had not expected the difference to be so slight. Four strokes of ink. The room remained small; the pan still needed emptying. Their hand hurt.

But Nought had asked, and they had answered, and he had used the answer.

“You can change it,” he said. “You can also keep it.”

Nil reached out with their left hand and touched the page beside the ink.

“Keep it.”

Nought let the notebook stay open while he prepared the terminal.

He checked the wall connection first. Then the small reserve gauge, then a sleeve of old forms. Nil watched the order and tried to remember it. A habit acquired in a registration queue was not likely to vanish because one man had been kind.

“This records a resonance,” Nought said. “A practice digit. The city uses it to recognize the channel you favor. It isn't the amount of protection assigned to your account.”

“Ama had one.”

“That was her Civic allocation.”

“She has seven now.”

“And choosing seven here wouldn't take it from her.”

Nil studied the separate windows. One was narrow, made for a single digit. The other was wider. Above the terminal the empty bracket looked as though someone had once hung a little card there and then thought better of it.

“Why seven?” Nil asked.

“For the ceremony's age? It's when the public record usually asks a child to choose. Children already have names. And protection.” Nought tapped the old ribbon. “This doesn't make them begin to exist.”

“I meant, why would I choose seven?”

“Would you?”

Nil almost answered yes just to end the examination. Nought caught their glance at the forms and slid the sleeve away.

“I'm asking you, not looking for the answer printed underneath.”

There were numbers they disliked. The office's closing bell. The steps they had counted instead of taking. The 01 above Ama when nobody could give her a time for the appeal. But disliking something was not the same as choosing something for oneself.

Seven was a narrow crooked shape. It appeared in ordinary places: the service stair, a mark on Tobi's shirt. None of that was a reason strong enough to justify a life.

Perhaps it did not need to be.

“Seven,” Nil said.

Nought nodded as if another answer would have received the same attention. “All right. The terminal speaks to the city. If it cannot find your record, I can't promise what it will request. We can stop before you place your wrist.”

Nil looked toward the back door. There was still an exit. There was also a notebook on the table that already held their name.

“I want to try.”

Nought showed them where to rest the uninjured part of the wrist and how to lift clear if the connection grew hot. His hand hovered beside the disconnect. Nil placed their right wrist on the cradle.

The brass detents settled with a sound like a lock being tested gently. A line of light crossed the narrow window. It stopped at 7.

Nil leaned forward.

Nought smiled. It was cautious, almost surprised. Nil felt their own face answering before they thought to protect it.

The wider window remained dark.

One second. Two.

Then the familiar broken characters appeared.

NaN.

Nil waited for another motion inside the mechanism. They could see the wheel. It was still.

“Did I choose wrong?”

“No.” Nought said it immediately. Then he looked at the connection instead of at the seven. “No. That's not what it's asking.”

Something clicked inside the wall.

He disconnected the cradle. The seven remained in its separate little frame. Above Nil's head, the glitch continued.

“What did it ask?”

Nought was listening.

The click came again, farther away, followed by another beyond the window. Not a wheel turning. Relays answering one another.

He closed the notebook and put it in Nil's left hand.

“Under your coat.”

“I broke it.”

“Not you.” His voice had hardened, but not toward them. He took the lamp off the table. “Leave the chair. This way.”

The terminal's wide window cleared. Letters struck it one at a time.

REMAINDER DETECTED.

Nil had never seen those words together. They tried to turn them into a sensible question. Behind the shutter, a public board rattled. A bell stopped halfway through a note.

Nought opened the back door.

Above the roofs, high in the white walls of the Basilica, a great amber aperture began to open. Its light found the rain, then the pipes, then the small wet yard behind the shrine.

Nil had wanted the city to notice them.

They stepped back from the light.

***

In the upper offices, Ivo Marr had brought the Comptroller a sheaf of routine corrections and a dinner already past its best.

Thale was reading the corrections. He had set a thumb beside an amended route total, holding his place while Ivo explained why the posted copy differed.

The relay struck.

Ivo turned. REMAINDER DETECTED occupied the little brass panel beside the door. He knew the words separately. He did not know what they meant here.

Thale's thumb lifted from the page.

For a moment the only sound was the kitchen clock in the next room. Then the Comptroller drew the district map toward him. The dinner plate caught against its wooden frame; he moved it aside, carefully, so the gravy would not spill.

“Which relay?” he asked.

Ivo bent to the panel. “Lower Wards. Open Hand lane. The registration connection has already been cut.”

Thale followed the printed line with one finger.

His Civic plate read 50, steel against the white cloth. It did not change.

“There you are,” he said.

Ivo waited for an instruction. Thale had spoken so quietly that it might not have been meant for him.

On the table, beside the cooling plate, the spoon rested where Thale had set it down. He had not taken a mouthful.
