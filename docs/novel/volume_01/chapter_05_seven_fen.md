# Volume One: A Name on Loan

## Chapter 5: Seven Fen

The back stair was dry. That made the sound of Nil's boots seem unreasonably loud.

Nought went first, checking the landing before he motioned them down. Through a little window Nil could see the yard they had just left. Amber light crossed it slowly, finding every puddle.

“Gus,” Nought said. “At the Bazaar. Tell him you need a strap repaired.”

“Baker told me about him.”

“Good. He'll believe Baker before he'll believe me.”

The attempt at lightness did not quite survive the sound below them: a metal foot finding stone.

Nought stopped at the lower door. He took a small witness plate from a pocket, scratched a mark across its paper seal and fitted it into the empty slots on Nil's decoder brace. He asked before fastening it. Nil nodded.

“If an enforcement machine offers a review stake, this identifies a witness and a defence deposit,” he said. “The shrine mutual keeps a little for emergencies. It doesn't buy you protection from every outcome.”

“What happens if I lose?”

“The deposit is exhausted and the machine's claim stands. Don't let anyone tell you that's the same thing as your life becoming its property.”

“It might take me anyway.”

Nought looked at them. “It might.”

That answer frightened Nil more than a promise would have. It also made the plate feel more solid in their hand.

Nought showed them the release catch. “A wheel can be stopped. A bad agreement can still be refused. If you have a way out, take it.”

“Come with me.”

“Someone has to be at the front when they ask who used the station.”

“You can tell them.”

“I will tell them what I witnessed.” He touched the notebook beneath Nil's coat. “Keep that dry.”

He opened the door, and Nil stepped into a service yard filled with empty cages for Nixie tubes. When they turned back, the door was already closing.

The Hound stood at the yard's far end.

Not the same machine, Nil thought. This one's muzzle was clear of laundry. Then they saw a strip of white cloth caught in the hinge beneath its lens.

The same machine. It had learned the way around.

A notice unfolded across its chest. Nil made themself read it. The pursuit warrant had a claim number. Beneath it, smaller words offered a witnessed review of the detention stake.

The plate on Nil's wrist clicked against the brace.

“I want the review,” they said.

The Hound lifted a foot. A strip of brass in the yard paving rose to meet it, closing a narrow boundary around the two of them. The machine's reserve vessel filled a second scale behind its chest. Nil's own little display acquired a separate frame beside the persistent NaN.

Nought's deposit had become a declared stake. It had not become money in Nil's pocket, or a number above their head.

The Hound waited while three marked positions lit on the brace.

Nil understood a lever. They understood a path with a source and a destination. They did not yet understand why a turning wheel should care what happened to a person standing in a yard.

The first face came past too quickly. They pressed the catch. A 5 struck into place.

The second stopped at 8. The third at 3.

Nothing matched. The brace gave a brief unpleasant knock, and the Hound advanced.

Nil ducked behind a cage rack. The hooked muzzle tore two empty frames free and threw them across the yard. One struck the wall where their head had been.

The defence wheel was smaller than the first. A zero passed it just as Nil panicked and pressed. They caught the next face instead.

The strike did not vanish. It glanced through the rack and hit their shoulder. The separate stake frame dropped. Pain made the edges of the yard bright.

The machine's public plate changed: **REVIEW CONTINUES**.

It was not impressed that this was Nil's first attempt.

Nil crouched until their breath stopped fighting them. The wheels had marks that returned. There was a pause after the heavy segment, a fraction of slack in the spring. They could feel it through the brace if they stopped trying to watch every face at once.

They took a second set. A 3, another 3, then a 7.

The two matched marks locked together. A narrow channel opened between the mutual deposit and the machine's detention reserve; the Hound's temporary scale fell. Its foot skidded, not because its body had been cut but because the claim supporting the advance had lost purchase.

Nil stared for half a beat too long.

The muzzle came back.

They waited for the spring's little pause. Zero.

The catch went down. The channel closed before the strike could complete. The Hound's foot hit harmlessly outside the boundary, and Nil stumbled away with their arm still attached to them.

They laughed once. It sounded much too close to crying.

On the roof above, someone said, “Do the laughing after the last round, Kid.”

Nil looked up.

A woman in a broad dark hat sat on the fire-escape rail. Two playing cards were tucked into its band. One boot was hooked around the rung below, and the revolver in her right hand was pointed at the yard gate's ratchet, just beyond the Hound's back.

“I thought you couldn't interfere,” Nil said.

“Not inside the stake. Look where I'm aiming.”

She fired.

The shot broke the latch controlling the yard gate, outside the marked stake boundary. The gate swung open. She had not won the review for Nil. She had made somewhere to go if they survived it.

“Eyes down,” she said.

Nil stopped three more faces. The first two matched; the third did not. The Hound's scale fell again, but the review stayed open. The woman opened the cylinder, caught the one spent cartridge in her palm and left that chamber empty. Six loaded rounds remained when she snapped it shut.

“The heavy face has a tell,” she said. “Listen.”

“I am.”

“Then stop listening to me.”

The next attack came low. Nil caught zero again. Their shoulder hurt where the first strike had landed; the pain did not alter the wheel's rhythm.

On the next set they missed the digit they wanted, then stopped another of it, and another. Three sixes locked into the brace. The detention stake emptied. The brass boundary dropped flat.

The Hound's legs settled under it. It had lost the current review claim, not forgotten every command in its body.

**LOCAL REVIEW CLOSED**, its plate read. A new notice began printing behind the first.

The woman dropped from the lower stair.

“Run now.”

They ran.

She took the gate she had opened, turned behind a pipe housing and pushed Nil into the narrow space beside a maintenance door. Her left braid caught against the collar of her coat; she freed it impatiently. From here the Hound could not see them without coming all the way around the housing.

“Name?” she asked.

Nil almost gave the answer they had offered Ama. It had become out of date.

“Nil.”

She nodded. “Vesper.”

“Why did you help?”

Vesper glanced toward the gate. “Seven fen.”

Nil waited.

“Standard rescue rate.”

“I don't have seven fen.”

“That's a serious weakness in your negotiating position.”

“You helped before asking.”

Her expression shifted, just a little. “And now I'm asking. Terrible business practice.”

Nil had begun to apologize when footsteps struck the passage on the other side of the pipe housing. Two pairs: one human, one too regular. Vesper raised a finger, listening.

The human passed. The mechanical steps stopped.

Nil could see a narrow stair beyond Vesper's shoulder. She had seen it too. She could reach the roof alone before the machine came around.

Instead she took the witness plate from Nil's brace, read its edge and put it back without breaking the seal.

“Gus's mark,” she said. “Old repair. Who sent you?”

“Nought. And Baker.”

“Both? You collect references quickly.”

The footsteps resumed. Vesper took a coin from her pocket, turned it once between finger and thumb, then closed her hand over it.

“There's another way into the Bazaar,” she said. “It isn't the shortest.”

“I can pay later. If someone lets me work.”

“Let's try keeping you in one piece first.”

She held the maintenance door open. Nil stepped through, then stopped it with their left boot so she could follow without turning her back on the passage.

Vesper looked at the boot, then at Nil.

“Good,” she said, and came inside.
