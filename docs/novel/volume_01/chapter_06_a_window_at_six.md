# Volume One: A Name on Loan

## Chapter 6: A Window at Six

Vesper knew which doors opened inward.

Nil discovered this after pushing the third one in the wrong direction and finding her already on the other side, waiting with a look of exaggerated patience. She had led them through a store of old tubes, across a room where someone was sleeping beneath a workbench, and into a passage that smelled of hot iron.

“Do you live here?” Nil whispered.

“Not if anyone asks.”

“That wasn't an answer.”

“You're going to find a lot of those.”

At the end of the passage she stopped joking.

A grate overlooked the Bazaar's lower service court. Beyond it, a porter was trying to guide a handcart around a brass machine standing in the middle of the lane. The machine lifted one stamp foot. The porter reversed so abruptly that a wheel came off the cart.

Nil recognized the Hound's shape. This one's muzzle carried no laundry.

“Different machine,” Vesper said. “Same order.”

The porter's load tilted. Nil reached for the grate before thinking. Vesper caught its edge too.

“Together,” she said.

They lifted it clear of its bottom hooks and leaned it against the wall. The porter hauled his cart into the gap. Vesper put her shoulder beneath the load while Nil retrieved the wheel before it rolled into the gutter.

“Pin's gone,” Nil said.

The porter swore softly. A woman in a forge apron appeared at the head of the lane and tossed him a short piece of iron.

“Temporary,” she called. “Don't take the slope.”

She had three red braids, a burn on the edge of her apron and a hammer stuck through a loop at her hip. Nil caught the load while the porter fitted the iron through the axle. The movement pulled at the scrape beneath their bandage.

The Hound turned.

“Inside,” Vesper told the porter.

The forge woman was already pulling open her door. The porter steered for it. Vesper let go of the cart only when its weight was safely over the threshold.

“Yard ahead's clear,” she told Nil. “But it can reach the forge door before they shut it.”

Nil looked at the machine's foot, then at the new paper claim spreading over its chest.

“I can ask for review.”

“If you can hold the channel long enough for them to get that cart inside.”

“You could go.”

Vesper set her jaw. “I could do all sorts of things.”

She placed a narrow dealer's seal against the plate on Nil's brace and showed them the printed stake before closing the clasp. Her own defence deposit supplemented what remained on Nought's witness plate. Neither of their Civic displays changed.

“Three stops,” she said. “You, me, you. Don't chase what I've already missed.”

“What am I trying to make?”

She answered the way a dealer reads out the house rules, fast and without looking at the cards. “Two alike, Pair. Three alike, Jackpot. Three in a row, up or down, is a Cascade; it buys a round before the other side answers. Make the three add to ten and it loses its next move. Zero-Sum. Nothing matches, you've Bust.” She paused. “Three of the number you chose at the shrine is the one people tell stories about. Don't plan your evening around it.”

“You've explained this before.”

“I used to deal it.” She did not say where.

The machine's lane boundary rose. A bell at the forge door rang as the porter finally cleared the threshold.

Nil caught 5. Vesper caught 6 so neatly that Nil almost forgot to take the third stop. They recovered late, pressed, and caught 8 instead of 7.

Nothing locked into a pattern.

The Hound advanced. Nil turned to get behind Vesper, and she turned at the same moment. Their shoulders struck. Its muzzle tore the corner from a crate between them.

Vesper caught the defence wheel on zero, but a splinter cut the cheek below her old lip scar. She wiped it with the back of her wrist.

“Same side,” she said.

“You moved.”

“So did you.”

The machine's lens adjusted, waiting for the next exchange. Nil could still see the place where a 7 would have followed her 6. It was difficult not to keep trying to repair the moment after it had passed.

Vesper touched the stone beside Nil's left boot.

“Stay on this side of the post. I'll cross behind. If I say down, it means down, not come and rescue me from a plan I haven't finished.”

Nil nodded.

“Say it.”

“This side. You cross behind.”

The next time Nil stopped a 3. Vesper looked at it before taking her turn and stopped another 3. Nil missed the third match and caught 0.

The Pair held. The machine's detention stake fell, and its next advance left a little more space between them and the forge.

Vesper moved behind the post. Nil stayed.

“Down.”

They went down. Her shot passed above the Hound's braced shoulder and struck the metal stop on a hanging service ladder. The ladder dropped behind it, forcing the machine to turn instead of coming directly at the crouching person.

The defence wheel turned. Nil found the spring's pause and caught zero. They did not have to scramble into someone else's path.

“Better,” Vesper said.

The next sequence began. Nil saw 5, waited, stopped it. Vesper took 6. This time Nil looked at the wheel instead of imagining the face they had missed before.

Seven.

The ordered marks locked. A stepwise channel ran through all three, taking a larger bite from the detention reserve and holding its next advance closed. Nil felt the brace open for another sequence before the Hound could act.

Vesper's grin was brief and delighted. “Again. It gave you room.”

Nil took the first stop without looking up. Another Pair completed the claim. The machine lowered itself into the service groove and began printing a local-review notice.

The forge woman came out holding a bucket.

“If that's oil,” Vesper said, “I wouldn't.”

“It's water. You both look flammable.”

She offered it to Nil first.

They took a mouthful and passed it on. Vesper drank, then poured a little onto a cloth and pressed it to her cheek.

“How long does the review keep it there?” Nil asked.

“Until someone posts another lawful claim or a less patient officer breaks the rules.” Vesper looked at the new public notice. “We didn't beat the city. We bought time in this lane.”

“A very expensive cart pin,” the woman said.

She lifted the broken crate out of the forge doorway. Inside, the porter was tightening the replacement pin while another worker held the load. The forge's light fell on useful crowded things: clamps, shaped bars, a set of repaired hinges waiting beside a labelled slate. Nil could have stood there for an hour reading what each object had become after somebody refused to discard it.

The woman noticed.

“Looking for work?”

“Yes.” Nil answered too quickly.

“Then you've chosen a bad evening.” She turned the slate around. A closure notice had been pasted beneath the hinge list. “Supplier's account has been attached. They're coming to inventory the tools next.”

Vesper read it. “You could be gone before that.”

“With which tools? The ones bolted to the floor?”

“Maeve,” the porter said from inside. “We've got the small bench clear.”

She thanked him without turning. “Maeve,” she told Nil. “Triskel. And I'm not leaving the place open for someone to carry off three generations of jigs as loose iron.”

Nil looked at the hinge list. One entry had been crossed out and rewritten with a different delivery address.

“Can the guild hold them?”

Maeve followed their finger. “Maybe. If we get the receiving marks before the inventory.”

“We need Gus,” Vesper said.

“So do I. Come on.”

Maeve handed the door key to the porter. She named the two clamps he was not to use and the slope he was not to take, then walked with them through the lower market.

The Bazaar was not one room. It was a series of spaces people had made beside other people's spaces: awnings tied to a tube shop, steps passing through a cooper's yard, a café whose tables had to be moved whenever a long wheel assembly came through. A woman with Civic 03 was adjusting the brake on a chair for a customer whose 81 shone against a clean collar. The customer waited until she was finished and tried it where she told him to.

Nil watched her make him test it twice.

“Good trade,” Maeve said.

“Does the number make it harder?” Nil asked.

She looked at the customer's collar, then at the brake. “Not if he listens.”

Gus's stall was a bench with a roof and more drawers than seemed possible beneath it. He was checking a glass tube against a little lamp. When Maeve set the closure notice on his work surface, he covered the lamp before looking up.

“If you put another wet warrant on my dry parts, I'm charging the office for a new towel.”

“Not a warrant,” she said.

“An ambitious distinction.”

His eyes moved to Nil, paused at the bandage, then to Vesper's cheek.

“Nought sent me,” Nil said. “For a strap.”

“And Baker,” Vesper added. “Excellent references. Awful timing.”

Gus put a small screen across the open side of the stall. “Show me the strap.”

He asked before touching it. Nil loosened the brace themself and let him examine the fastening that had caught their sleeve. He did not try to pry the fused ring away from skin.

“This bit I can improve,” he said, pointing. “That bit I leave alone.”

Nil nodded.

While he worked, Maeve laid out her tool list. The guild could hold equipment, Gus said, if someone signed for each piece before the inventory arrived. Not ownership transferred to a hidden friend; witnessed storage with receipts. Maeve wanted the signs now. He wanted the actual list of what she could move.

“Two benches, the small anvil, four jigs.”

“You have five jigs.”

“The fifth belongs to my aunt. She'll argue her own case.”

Gus grunted approval and brought out the receipt book.

Nil touched the washer in their pocket. The room above a repair shop was beginning to look less like an escape from the rest of the world.

Gus finished the strap and set three little trays beside it. A narrow horseshoe, visibly repaired across one end; a black cat token; a paper ward in a protective sleeve.

“Nought's frame takes three,” he said. “These aren't three things you owe me for. Pick one.”

“What does it do?” Nil pointed to the horseshoe.

“Gives your chosen face a little more room on a wheel that already carries it. Doesn't add a seven where there isn't one. Doesn't promise you'll stop it.”

“The cat?”

“One repair of a bad pattern. The ward catches the first strike. Different help. Same size hole in the frame.”

“Why give me one?”

Gus glanced toward the service court. “Because I watched a porter bring a month's tube orders through a lane you helped keep open. Because Nought sent you. Because I can spare one, not the whole drawer.”

Nil chose the horseshoe. They liked that someone had repaired it without hiding the join.

Vesper turned it over, inspecting the weld. “Open end down? All the luck falls out.”

Maeve took it from her and pointed to the fitting. “Open end into the slot, or you break it.”

Gus hid a smile in the receipt book.

He fastened the charm where Nil could reach the release. Then Vesper offered to replace the wet bandage. Nil let her. She worked less neatly than Nought but checked that the fingers could move.

“What about work?” Nil asked Gus.

“Right now?”

“Later.”

“If there's a later with no officers at my screen, we'll discuss it.”

It was not a promise. Nil folded the possibility carefully beside the rejected form.

They left by a service balcony while Maeve stayed long enough to finish signing. Across the lane, above a locked stair, a young woman sat beside a high window. Her Civic read 06. There was a cup on the sill and a folded paper beneath her hand.

She looked down when someone tried the stair door. A Collector checked its lock and walked away without going in.

Nil moved toward the balcony steps.

Vesper caught their sleeve, above the bandage. “Where are you going?”

“She can't come down.”

“Then don't bring the whole hunt to her door.”

Nil pulled their arm free. “So we leave her there.”

Vesper took that without smiling. “We find out what she wants. Who's treating her. Who can move her after we go. Running upstairs doesn't answer any of those.”

The woman at the window had seen them. She lifted the folded paper as though considering whether to drop it. The Collector turned at the corner, and she lowered her hand.

Gus came out with a receipt. Nil pointed.

“Livia Kade,” he said quietly. “I've tried the release desk.”

“Is she asking to leave?”

“She's asking for the treatment she was promised. Not every promise includes the road to it.”

Nil held out the back of the receipt. “Write the window down.”

Gus looked at them for a moment, then did. He added a contact mark below the address.

“No promise you can't keep,” he said.

“We remember,” Nil answered. It felt smaller than a rescue. They kept it anyway.

Gus pointed out the covered junction beyond the drain on the back of the same paper. “First ferry's just before dawn. If you miss it, wait here. I'll bring the dispatch cart through at daybreak.”

Maeve joined them at the end of the balcony carrying a wrapped tool bundle and a satchel full of signed storage copies.

“My aunt has the forge key. Guild has the tools. I'm coming as far as the shrine road.” She showed them a supplier slip with the same toll mark as Gus's notice. “Someone is charging us for a channel my grandmother used to keep with a kettle and a promise. I'd like to ask them what changed.”

They waited in Gus's back room until the cordon on the canal road thinned, well past midnight. Then they took the drain steps toward the covered night route.

Below, a ratchet clicked. Vesper stopped turning her coin.

A large man stood at the gate. Rust-coloured coat, heavy braced shoulders, three glass jars secured against his belt. A hound-skull mask hid his face. His human hand checked a little wrapped token beneath the coat before moving to the latch of a jar.

His Civic read 61.

“Vesper Sette,” he said. “I should have charged more for this shift.”

“Varro,” she answered. Her voice had gone bright enough to sound false. “Always a pleasure to inspect the rates.”

He pointed past her at Nil.

“The unregistered account comes with me.”
