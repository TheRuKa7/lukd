# Volume Two: The Unfinished Hundred

## Chapter 8: The Ferryman's Seam

The ferryman would not take their money until he had finished inspecting the rope.

Vesper stood on the landing with three fares in her hand. Behind them, the Wards were beginning to make the sounds of another working morning: shutters, cart wheels, a distant complaint about a delivery. Nothing in those sounds admitted that Nil had become someone worth hunting.

“We were told you left at this bell,” Vesper said.

The man bent the rope between his hands. A few pale fibres lifted from the bend.

“When the rope's ready.”

“Is it ready?”

He showed her the fibres.

“You're welcome to depart without it.”

Maeve put down her tool bundle. Nil watched her mouth twitch. Vesper closed her hand around the money and waited.

The boat was low in the water, with a little cargo awning and benches worn smooth at the middle. Its name had been repainted often enough that the letters stood higher than the wood around them. Tetsu, according to Gus's note. The man and the boat appeared equally unwilling to be hurried.

He replaced a short section of line, testing the splice with his whole weight before beckoning them forward.

“Receipt.”

Nil offered the closed review in its oilcloth. Tetsu dried his hands before touching it. He read Varro's office number, checked the route release and held the paper beside a slate nailed under the awning.

“Closed claim gets you through the drain toll,” he said. “It doesn't pay the crossing.”

“We've brought the fares.”

“And this is the slower crossing. Weather holds, Moon Pier before the night barrier. Weather doesn't, you sleep on the boat.”

Nil had slept on a chair and a roof in the last two nights. A boat sounded almost like choosing a different trade.

“Can the office stop us?” they asked.

Tetsu turned the paper back toward them. “This claim's closed. If a new order reaches the landing before I leave, I read the new order. I don't pretend a piece of paper means more than it says.”

He did not ask why the figure above Nil's head would not settle. He had seen it; his gaze had paused, then returned to the receipt.

Vesper paid. Tetsu wrote three entries, tore off the fare counterfoil and handed it to Maeve, who already had a place for paper in her folio. Nil took back the original review. Seal 1 remained separately wrapped inside their coat, where a loose hand could not mistake it for a coin.

“Tools under the bench,” Tetsu said. “Weight low. No standing when we turn out of the canal.”

Nil stepped aboard first and held the bundle while Maeve followed. They tried to take its heavier end with their right hand, forgot the scrape and hissed.

“Left,” Maeve said.

“I know.”

“Your hand seems to have missed the meeting.”

They shifted their grip. She accepted the help once it was actually help.

The boat moved before Nil felt ready for Meridian to leave them. The landing drifted sideways; the stair behind it narrowed to a grey crease. They searched the rooftops for the one on which they had slept. Every roof looked like another roof. Somewhere under them Nought was answering questions, Baker was setting out bowls, and Ama had a receipt that ought to be enough.

Nil touched the notebook beneath their coat. They had written an address for a woman in a window. They had no address of their own to give her.

“Gus knows the route,” Vesper said.

Nil looked up.

“You keep checking the paper as if it'll tell you she's followed us.”

“I said we'd remember.”

“Then remember. You don't have to carry the whole stair.”

She sat facing the canal behind them. Her hand rested near her holster without touching it. The small cut beneath her lip scar had dried to a dark line. Five live cartridges; Nil knew the count because she had made no effort to turn the gun into a mystery.

For a while they travelled between high stone walls. Water stains marked floods above the height of Nil's head. Workers on a maintenance shelf were hauling up a bent grate. One raised a hand to Tetsu; he answered without releasing the tiller.

Beyond the last lock, the canal widened into a reach of moving grey water. Nil felt the boat change its mind beneath them.

“Stay seated,” Tetsu said.

They sat more firmly, an action they would not previously have believed possible.

Maeve untied a packet of yesterday's bread. Gus had put it beside the fare receipt; Vesper had paid for it with the breakfast. Maeve broke it into three pieces and offered Tetsu a fourth from her own share.

He shook his head, then pointed with his chin to a cloth packet beneath his seat.

“You eat. Mine's there.”

They ate slowly. The bread was hard enough to require an intention. Nil watched a bird land on a half-submerged post, wobble and recover. No number hovered over it. The current did not appear to care.

“Do the river pools cover the birds?” they asked.

Tetsu glanced at the post. “Watershed reserve covers the habitat. Doesn't teach them where to perch.”

The bird abandoned the post and flew away, supplying its own opinion.

By midday the far bank had broken into low islands. Pine roots gripped stone walls. Narrow roofs appeared between trees, their ridges lifting at the ends. At one small landing a shrine bell hung above a rack of fishing floats. The bell did not ring when the wind reached it; a woman climbed onto a stool and freed the cord by hand.

“Hyakki?” Nil asked.

“Outer villages.”

“Are we nearly there?”

“Near enough to know we aren't.”

Vesper had fallen asleep with her hat tipped down, one boot braced against a bench leg. Maeve was checking the small iron pin she had saved from the forge lane. She had replaced the porter's temporary fitting before departure; this rejected piece was bent at its tip.

“Keeping it?” Nil asked.

“So I remember why it failed.”

Nil took out their washer, then reconsidered and put it away. Not every useful object required an exhibition.

Tetsu shifted the tiller. A stronger wind pushed beneath the cargo awning, and the water ahead darkened in a long uneven stripe.

“Canvas down.”

Vesper was awake at once. She rose too quickly.

“Seated,” Tetsu said, with exactly the same voice.

She sat, reached up and worked at the awning tie. The wet knot held. Maeve slid along the bench toward her.

“Not that end. It's loaded.”

“I have untied a knot before.”

“Then leave this one the pleasure of being your next.”

Maeve took the loose tail, eased it back through the loop and lowered the canvas in a controlled fold. Nil caught the corner with their left hand. Water ran off it onto their knees.

Tetsu turned the boat toward a sheltered cut between islands. A wave struck the side before they reached it. The bow rose, came down hard, and something knocked twice beneath the forward bench.

Maeve looked toward the sound. Tetsu was already looking.

“Brace pin.”

“I heard.” She lifted the bench flap. The brace beneath the small mast had worked loose at one end; each roll drove the iron against an enlarged hole. “Can't just hammer it back. It'll tear the soft edge.”

Vesper had the pin halfway into her hand before Maeve caught her wrist.

“Leave the load on it until we support the brace.”

For a breath neither moved. Nil saw the sharp reply Vesper almost chose. Then she opened her fingers.

“Tell me where.”

Maeve pointed to a short spar stowed along the side. “Under that crosspiece. Nil, fold the spare cloth twice. Not your dressing.”

They had already reached toward their right hand. They stopped and found the spare rag.

The repair took three people and most of the little space beneath the bench. Vesper held the spar where Maeve put it. Nil fed the folded cloth around the brace so the new fitting would bear against a wider surface. Maeve worked the damaged pin free, measured it against the spare stock Tetsu passed back, and chose a piece just long enough.

“Can I let go?” Vesper asked.

“Not yet.”

A wave struck. Nil's shoulder hit the bench edge. Vesper's arm trembled beneath the spar, but she kept it in place.

Maeve set the new pin, secured its retaining loop and checked the load before nodding.

“Now.”

Vesper let out a breath and lowered the spar. The brace held. Nothing brightened above anyone's head. Wood, iron and the right order of work had been sufficient.

They spent the next hour behind a stone breakwater, waiting for the gusts to ease. Tetsu bailed the water that had come aboard. Nil offered to help and was given a smaller scoop that could be used with the left hand.

When Tetsu leaned forward, the coat at his shoulder pulled across an old line of stitching. A rectangular patch had been removed there. Under its cleaner outline survived the little angled seam Nil had seen on the Collector's uniform.

They looked too long.

“Yes,” Tetsu said.

Nil set the scoop down. “Were you—”

“Used to be.”

Vesper watched the narrow opening beyond the breakwater. Maeve continued checking the brace, giving the question somewhere private to land.

“Why did you stop?” Nil asked.

Tetsu emptied his bucket over the side. “A long answer. Not a fare I sold you.”

Nil flushed. “I didn't mean—”

“I know. And you can still ask. I can still say no.”

He crouched beside the replaced line and beckoned Nil closer.

“This I can show you.”

The knot looked simple until Nil tried to copy it. Their loop came loose under a pull. Tetsu undid it without comment, put the end back in their left hand and showed where the standing part needed to lie.

“You want it to hold under weight and release when the weight comes off. Two different jobs.”

Nil tried again. This time the pull tightened the right part. Tetsu handed it back for a third attempt.

Across from them Vesper was rubbing the arm she had held under the spar.

“You were right,” she told Maeve.

“About the pin?”

“Let's start there.”

Maeve nodded, accepting exactly that much.

They reached Moon Pier after the lamps had been lit. Red lanterns hung above dark water, their reflections torn into long ribbons by the wake. Nil could hear drums somewhere inland. A procession of lights moved behind the roofs, rising and falling as if the whole street were breathing.

At the pier's upper end, a barrier lay across the shrine road. Its keeper was winding the day's chain into a locked housing.

Tetsu tied the boat to the landing and checked the knot Nil had helped make.

“Night barrier's down,” he said. “You can sleep aboard. Tide's slack here.”

Vesper looked toward the lights. “How long's the way around?”

“Longer in the dark. You don't know the water steps.”

Nil had already begun to gather the tool bundle. They set it back beneath the bench. The island would not vanish if they waited until morning. For the first time since leaving Nought's chair, they were tired enough to accept a safe place when it was offered.

Tetsu spread the spare canvas over the benches and gave them the dry side. Maeve checked the brace once more before lying down. Vesper placed her hat over her eyes.

“Nobody say first light,” she said.

Nil looked at the lantern procession beyond the roofs. One light lagged behind the others, hurried, then stopped beside a doorway to wait for something they could not see.

They thought of a woman climbing a stool to free a bell.

Under the canvas, with water making small sounds against the hull, Nil practiced Tetsu's knot until their fingers could remember it without looking.
