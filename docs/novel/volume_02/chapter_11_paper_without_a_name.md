# Volume Two: The Unfinished Hundred

## Chapter 11: Paper Without a Name

The woman ahead of them wanted a spelling corrected.

She had brought two marriage records, a household tag and a letter from the dead man's brother. The junior clerk kept returning to the oldest copy, where the wrong stroke was very beautifully written.

“My father could write his own name,” she said. “He wrote it on the boat. You can still go and look.”

At the far end of the room a great brush stopped moving.

Enma rose from behind the archive desk. Nobody struck a gong to announce him. He came to the little table, read the letter and asked the woman whether the boat could be visited before the afternoon tide.

“My brother's there now.”

“Then let the witness go while he can.”

He marked the disputed entry without erasing the earlier hand. The clerk began to explain why the old copy had been preferred.

“Keep the explanation with the correction,” Enma said. “The next reader will need both.”

Nil had expected a gate tall enough to make them feel small. There was one outside, painted dark red, but the waiting room beyond it contained benches, an ink stain shaped like a fish and a cracked tile someone had covered with a woven mat. The people waiting were mostly occupied with paper. Grief had brought several of them; none looked grateful to have been made part of an impressive building.

Hina sat beside Nil with the umbrella across her knees. Its eye watched the disputed spelling until the woman left.

“He can correct it,” Nil whispered.

“When there's evidence.”

“What if there isn't?”

Hina rubbed the edge of her patch. “That's what we're asking.”

Enma called them after the last public petitioner. He wore his authority in the careful placement of his hands: nothing was moved until there was room for it, nothing set aside where it could be mistaken for finished. His face was grave without appearing hungry for anyone else's fear.

He read Ofuku's introduction, the bounty notice and the closed drain review. He asked Nil which part they wanted examined first.

“Whether I'm in your book.”

“You have chosen a name?”

“Nil.”

He wrote it on a request slip, exactly as Nought had. The small i and l looked ordinary in his large handwriting.

“And the displayed result has remained unresolved since before that choice?”

Nil nodded.

“Searching a personal entry is one request. Entering the protected comparison room is another. I can begin the search now. If you wish to witness the original comparison, you must show that you can close a loaded channel without redirecting it through the room.”

Vesper looked up. “What does that demonstration cost?”

Enma led them through an inner door. Beyond it stood a low practice frame between two stone basins. Three little wheels faced a bank of weighted shutters. No blade, jaw or human figure occupied the far side.

“An isolated training reserve,” he said. “Nothing drawn from household or restoration coverage. A loss leaves the shutters closed. We cancel the circuit and you may return with an authorized operator. I will still issue the search result. No person's body or Civic allocation is offered.”

He showed them a broad release bar on their side of the frame. Maeve pressed it before anything was loaded. All three wheels stopped, and a dry marker fell into the empty basin.

“Four completed rounds,” Enma continued. “No Parry. The trial concerns how a channel is carried forward; closing every return path would conceal the fault it is designed to expose. You can cancel at any point.”

Nil looked at zero on the defence wheel. Its little interval had become the safest place they knew how to find.

“What happens when the shutters act?”

“They close part of the training route. You can move the available load through a declared return. If you cannot, the gauge falls. The housing bears the force.”

Maeve examined the bolted housing before she nodded. Vesper read the reserve tag and its cancellation line. Hina put the umbrella on a dry support where its eye could watch both basins.

Nil could refuse the demonstration and still receive the answer. They stayed by the frame for a moment, feeling the difference between that and a door whose only alternatives were surrender and escape.

“I want to see the original,” they said.

Enma set the request slip where all of them could see it. “Then let us make the route safe.”

The first round began with a deep wooden click. Each strike had a declared base of fifty training units, printed beside the gauge. Not Nil's ordinary field strength: the instrument's calibration, for the instrument's bounded reserve. The actual digit would be added to it.

Nil stopped 5. Vesper, taking the second position, caught 3.

For an instant Vesper's 3 remained in both windows. Then the scoring window advanced to 4. Nil's final stop landed on 6 and stayed there in both windows.

Nothing useful matched. A shutter closed, and part of the practice gauge dropped into the lower basin.

Nil reached instinctively toward the defence catch.

“Not that path,” Vesper said.

Their hand stopped above it. The brass return line trembled, then settled against the housing with a hard knock. No one had been struck. Nil nevertheless felt their shoulder anticipate the Hound's muzzle.

“She stopped three,” Nil said.

“She did,” Enma said.

“Then why did it change?”

He opened a narrow cover over the route between the second and third scoring windows. A tiny pin stood upright.

“The first actual strike above fifty lifts this. Your first was fifty-five. The lifted pin advances the next scoring digit once. Operators call it Carry.”

“Not the actual wheel,” Maeve said, leaning close.

“Not the landing.”

Nil looked at Vesper's actual 3, still faithfully recorded beside the 4. The decoder had not invented a different stop. It had carried an operation to a place they had failed to read.

“Can it happen again from the changed number?”

“No. One carry in the round. A changed scoring digit is not a new actual strike.” Enma lowered the pin and showed them the one-use latch beneath it. “Nine advances to zero. If there is no next scoring position, it has nowhere to go and expires. It is not stored for another round.”

“That would have been useful to know first,” Nil said.

Enma rested a hand on the open cover. “It would. I should have shown the latch before loading the frame.”

He did not turn the correction into a lesson about Nil's impatience. He gave them the release bar again and waited while they decided whether to continue.

Nil glanced at Vesper. She had not mocked the hand that had reached for zero.

“You saw it move?” they asked.

“After your first. I thought I'd caught the wrong face.”

“It was.” Nil studied the pin. “I just didn't know what I'd done.”

For the second round they agreed on the actual stops first. Nil would take 5. If they caught it, Vesper would take another 5, allowing the carry to score it as 6. Nil would then seek 7.

“And if you miss?” Vesper asked.

“Read what happens. Don't pretend the plan happened instead.”

She smiled slightly. “I have been trying to put that into words.”

Nil caught 5. The actual strike read 55 and lifted the pin. Vesper waited through an entire turn before accepting 5. The scoring frame advanced it once to 6; the one-use latch lay flat. Nil's final 7 remained 7.

The Cascade opened three shutters in sequence. The next closing action stayed held long enough for another round.

Hina leaned forward, umbrella tilting with her. “It worked.”

“It worked once,” Maeve said, with the seriousness of someone who kept failed pins.

In the third round Nil tried to repeat too quickly. They stopped 7 instead of 5. Vesper caught 7; the carry made it 8. Nil caught 3. Seven, eight, three. The shutters did not mistake their determination for a pattern.

The bonus interval ended. Another part of the practice gauge fell.

One round remained.

Nil looked at the open shutters, then at the basin. There was enough declared reserve left to finish if they could close a useful pattern. There was also an entirely intact release bar within reach.

“We can come back,” Maeve said.

Hina nodded. Nil could see that she meant it, although the court's notice lay inside her sleeve.

They did not have to win to keep the company around them. That made it easier, rather than harder, to look at the wheel.

“Six,” Nil told Vesper. “Actual five for you, if I get six. Then six again.”

The wheel turned. Nil let one 6 pass while their hand steadied. The next came into reach and they stopped it.

The pin lifted.

Vesper took 5. Its actual window stayed 5. The scoring window became 6.

Nil listened for the spring's little pause, the same ordinary slack they had first heard in a yard full of broken cages.

Six.

The three scored sixes locked together. The remaining trial shutters opened. The gauge settled, and the unused training load returned to its labelled reserve.

Enma disconnected the frame before anyone put a hand inside it.

Hina let out a whoop so unceremonious that two clerks looked through the door. She shut her mouth, considered the situation and bowed to them with excessive dignity.

“An examination,” she said, “has concluded.”

Vesper laughed into her sleeve.

***

The comparison room was colder than the waiting room. Its shelves rose only a little above Enma's head, but they extended farther than Nil could see. At each junction a small plaque identified the source of the records: household declarations, shrine witnesses, probate returns, regional comparison indexes.

“Not every life in a single book,” Enma said when Nil stopped beneath the plaques. “Copies disagree. Some arrive late. Some never arrive.”

“You still look for them.”

“Yes.”

He searched the chosen name, then the unresolved account signature, then the possible cross-references provided by the decoder's registration field. The result was not a dramatic page tearing itself out. There was simply no link where the lookup should have returned one.

Enma repeated the query against an independently maintained index. He checked its last received date before setting down the brush.

Hina stood close enough that her sleeve touched Nil's coat. She did not look at their head.

“No entry?” Nil asked.

“No matching entry in these records.”

“Living or dead?”

“No matching cross-reference under either. That is the finding. It does not tell me why.”

Nil looked at the unmarked request slip. They had imagined a page waiting somewhere, a space made for them before they learned to ask. Even a bad page might have meant someone had known where to begin.

“Then what do you call someone who isn't in it?”

Enma looked at the name he had written.

“You asked me to call you Nil.”

Their eyes stung. They were annoyed by it, and more annoyed that annoyance did not help.

“The court wants me.”

“The court has an interest in a missing entry. Interest is not knowledge.”

He wrote the finding in plain language, listing the records searched and their dates. Beneath it he added that absence from this archive did not certify absence of life, personhood, history or claim to care. He left room for another keeper's comparison.

“Do you want this recorded?” he asked.

Nil read it twice. “Yes. With the limits.”

Enma set a narrow access sleeve beside the finding. “Who may receive the search copy?”

Nil looked at Hina. The court had offered to buy the missing entry before knowing what was missing. They did not want their answer hanging beside a bounty notice.

“Ofuku for safekeeping. Hina to carry it. The others can read it with me.”

“The palace?”

“We can show them the finding and its limits. Not the query signature.”

Enma marked the restricted attachment separately. “A future comparison will need your permission for that part.”

Hina read the names on the sleeve before accepting it. Nil had expected the blank place in a book to leave them with nothing. They had not expected to have to decide who could see it.

Enma pressed his witness mark beside the text. The original went into his archive. A counter-copy went into a dry sleeve, its temporary clasp to be sealed for travel after Ofuku had witnessed the receiving copy.

Nil reached for it, then stopped. Their coat already held the closed review, the Seal, Livia's address and Nought's notebook.

Hina held out both hands.

“I can carry it,” she said. “If you want.”

Nil gave it to her. She placed it inside the flat inner pocket beneath her obi, away from the umbrella's wet edge.

Outside, the woman with the disputed spelling was waiting for the returning witness. She had bought two steamed buns and kept one wrapped beside her. When she saw Nil, she moved her bag to make room on the bench.

They sat while Maeve and Vesper checked the road back.

The woman looked at the finding's sleeve in Hina's hands.

“Not the answer you wanted?”

“I don't know yet.”

She nodded as though that was an answer a person might reasonably have. When her witness came through the gate, she stood so quickly she nearly left the spare bun behind, then turned back and caught it.

Nil watched them go inside together.

Hina remained beside the bench, holding the copy flat.

“We should eat,” she said at last. “Before we argue with another palace.”

Nil wiped their face with the uninjured hand and stood.
