# Volume Two: The Unfinished Hundred

## Chapter 12: The Foxes' Bargain

The road to the palace divided at a tree that had grown around an old sign.

One arrow pointed through the trunk. Another pointed down into its roots. Hina passed both and took the narrow path to the left.

“The sign is older than the road,” she said.

“They could change the sign,” Nil replied.

“They ask it every year.”

Vesper looked at the lettering, then at the thick bark around it. “Does it answer?”

“It says the road could change back.”

Nobody could think of a useful reply to that.

They had left Ofuku's house after breakfast on the sixth morning since Nil had missed the repair trial. The scab on Nil's palm no longer needed a thick dressing. They kept a clean strip over it for the climb, mostly to stop them picking at it. Ofuku had insisted on sending rice packets, the court notice and a list of restoration requests that had waited longer than the court's own posted limit.

“I can't carry all those,” Hina had complained.

“Then ask a friend.”

Hina had looked at the four of them and seemed briefly unsure how to use the word. Maeve had held out the folio. That had settled the paper, if not the larger question.

The foxfire began below the palace steps. Small blue flames occupied stone cups along one side of the path; red ones burned along the other. They gave almost no heat. Nil put their hand above one and felt only the cold morning moving between their fingers.

“Don't touch the wick,” said a voice ahead.

“It will still burn you,” added another.

Two retainers stood under the outer gate. At first Nil thought the second was the first reflected in a dark panel. Then one turned to read the names on a message strip, while the other kept watching the path.

Ao wore blue; Aka wore red. Each had one fox tail, and their tied hair fell on opposite sides. Similar faces made the differences in expression easier to notice. Ao looked as though he had been waiting for a late delivery. Aka looked as though he had been waiting to hear why it was late.

“Hina of the keeper's house,” Ao said.

“Hina of Hina,” she answered.

The umbrella tipped its canopy toward them, showing the restored patch. Neither retainer laughed.

Ao read the court notice and Enma's receiving sleeve. Aka read the household requests on the back of Ofuku's list.

“These aren't audience credentials,” Ao said.

“They're requests nobody answered,” Hina replied.

“The road office answers those.”

“The road office says the palace has the lock.”

Aka looked at the oldest date. “This one's past the posted interval.”

“The carrier's fingers went away,” Hina said. “We opened the last emergency cord.”

Ao folded the message strip, carefully enough that Nil could see him taking time before speaking.

“The court is keeping three shelters open while the mainland withholds the next reserve clearance. Tamamo has asked for permanence because renewals keep arriving too late. That isn't an excuse for a late request. It is what happens to the requests after we receive them.”

“Then show us,” Hina said.

The two retainers looked at each other. Their paired speech had been a kind of door; for the first time it stood open long enough to see people behind it.

“We can grant a witnessed approach,” Ao said. “Not armed entry into the private rooms.”

Vesper unclipped her holster without drawing the revolver. “A locked rack, a receipt, and it stays in sight.”

Maeve showed her hammer. “This too?”

“The covered workroom,” Aka said. “No one touches the bundle without you.”

They agreed. Vesper and Maeve would remain at the outer court as the named witnesses, with their belongings sealed in a rack they could see. Nil and Hina would hear the offer in the receiving hall. If either wanted to leave, the approach channel had to return them to this gate.

“Put that last part in the copy,” Vesper said.

Ao did.

Before admitting them he led Nil and Hina to two little posts beside the first step. Each held a flame beneath a glass hood. A silver thread joined the posts to the gate's approach channel.

“The court uses images,” he said. “The receiving rooms are safe only if you can distinguish the marked path from the image of one. This demonstration is bounded to these steps. You can stop and ask us to escort you instead.”

“Is the escort price different?” Nil asked.

“No.”

“Then why test?”

Ao looked at the little flames. “Because people sometimes decide they know a room better than its keeper.”

Aka glanced at his brother. “And keepers sometimes decide an explanation is complete because they gave it once.”

The demonstration began. Across the first step a second stair appeared, pale and inviting, leading around the post toward a door that had not been there a moment before.

Nil looked at the silver thread. Its reflection followed the false stair for several inches, then ended in the empty space beneath it.

Hina lowered the umbrella until its eye was level with the real post. The flame's painted reflection seemed to lean toward the new door. Beneath the hood the actual wick had not moved.

“This way,” she said, taking the original stair.

Nil followed. The false door thinned behind them. There had never been a passage there, but the image had made it easy to forget where the wall ought to stand.

At the second post the path appeared to continue straight ahead. Hina took a step and stopped before her sandal came down.

Nil could see why. The approach thread turned right into a little metal guide. The image went on without it.

“It's like a wheel,” Nil said. “The picture can lie. The actual stop has a witness.”

“A comparison,” Aka said. “Not a universal cure for being deceived.”

“I didn't say it was.”

“Keep saying that.”

Ao lowered the demonstration hoods. Both false paths vanished. The gate behind them remained open, with Vesper leaning against its inner post and Maeve counting the rack seals.

Hina looked past the retainers toward the palace roofs.

“Why hasn't the household line been opened?”

“Clearance must come through the court key,” Ao said.

“Does it have to?”

He hesitated. Aka took the request list from him.

“The local route exists,” Aka said. “Its opening authority was withdrawn under the newer compact.”

“Because unreviewed local transfers emptied two winter shelters,” Ao said sharply. “If you tell half the story, tell it to someone who wasn't there.”

Aka lowered the list. For a moment his expression lost all its quickness.

“I was there.”

“Then help me keep the next shelter open.”

The silence after that was not meant for visitors. Nil looked down at the paper rather than pretending they had not heard.

Hina lifted her umbrella. “Ofuku's asking for named local witnesses. Not for a hole anyone can empty.”

Ao read the oldest request again.

“She'll need a reserve schedule,” he said.

“She sent one.” Maeve's voice came from the gate. “Back page. I helped add the delivery times.”

Aka turned the sheet over. Ao did not ask him to turn it back.

The receiving hall smelled of lamp oil, dry paper and medicines being boiled somewhere out of sight.

Nil had expected a throne. There was a raised seat at the far end, but Tamamo stood beside a long worktable, reviewing a care roster with an attendant. Her layered robes were violet and pale gold. Behind her, nine luminous tails moved with the slow deliberation of lights drifting beneath water. Digits shone at their tips, too bright to confuse with the Civic 99 above her.

She finished speaking to the attendant before turning to her visitors.

“Hina,” she said. “You have brought the keeper's objections.”

Hina swallowed. “Some of them are mine.”

Tamamo inclined her head. “Then I should hear them from you.”

Up close, her warmth did not feel false. That made Nil more uneasy than a threat would have. She had read Hina's restoration requests and knew which household had used its last cord. She asked whether the fingers had held through morning. Hina said they had, and Tamamo's shoulders eased almost imperceptibly.

“I am glad.”

She led them to a screened side room where three patients rested in supported manifestations. A lacquered instrument lay under a fine mesh of vow threads. Beside it, the outline of a woman came and went around a wooden hairpin. An attendant changed a basin of warm water without interrupting the woman's quiet account of a dream.

“The old channels can still hold them,” Tamamo said. “But every renewal asks us to prove again that they have not ceased to matter. I cannot promise them a quick repair.”

Nil looked at the hairpin. Its keeper had wrapped the damaged end in cloth so it would not catch.

“Would permanence repair that?”

“No. It would keep the present manifestation stable. Repairs could continue without risking another disappearance between them.”

Hina's hand tightened on the umbrella shaft.

“And then?”

“Then a person could remain.”

“As they are now?”

Tamamo met her eyes. “Within the attested form. Unbounded change would reopen the uncertainty the compact closes.”

The room was quiet enough for Nil to hear the attendant wringing out the cloth. Hina looked at the woman beside the hairpin. The woman had shut her eyes, perhaps tired, perhaps unwilling to be asked to stand for an argument.

Hina moved back from the screen.

In the receiving hall she opened her umbrella fully. The old patch lifted into place, crooked and unmistakable.

“What if I want a new patch?”

“Repairs that preserve the attested form are permitted.”

“What if I want a different colour?”

Tamamo paused. “That would require review.”

“What if I don't want to wait for review?”

“Hina.” The tenderness in Tamamo's voice was almost painful. “I am trying to give you time enough to be impatient.”

Hina looked down. Nil could see the small hand around the shaft shaking.

“I know.”

It was the first thing she had said without either ceremony or defiance since entering the palace.

Tamamo laid the custody notice on the table. “The mainland has offered the court a permanent allotment if we surrender the unresolved traveller. They believe the missing account can be settled. I cannot certify their belief. I can certify what the offered reserve would maintain.”

“They want to study me,” Nil said.

“They want custody. I will not soften the word.”

“And if I refuse?”

“Then there is no agreement.”

Nil looked toward the open route back. Ao stood beside it. He did not close the door.

“I refuse to be exchanged,” they said.

Tamamo accepted the sentence without pretending it made the care roster disappear. “Then we must discuss what else you are asking of this court.”

Hina turned to Nil. “If the reserve really holds—”

“You can want it,” Nil said. The answer surprised them by how much it hurt. “I don't get to tell you not to be frightened.”

“But I can't buy it with you.”

They shook their head.

Hina pressed her lips together. For a moment Nil thought she might cry. She did not. She looked at Tamamo's roster instead.

“Then let us see the local gate. The one that's still there. If the keeper can open a bounded route, you don't need to wait for the mainland to own somebody.”

“A broken local schedule could harm people already under care.”

“So test the lock separately,” Nil said. “A declared reserve. Like Enma's frame. Not the patients.”

Tamamo studied them for a long time.

“An Audit can test the control claim,” she said at last. “It cannot write the replacement agreement. If you close my isolated claim, I will bring the key to a witnessed negotiation tomorrow. No household reserves enter the stake. If you fail, the present controls remain and you leave under the approach protection.”

“No custody clause,” Hina said.

“None.”

“And nobody says the trial made the decision for the people outside.”

Tamamo's tails drew close behind her. “You have been listening to Ofuku.”

“Yes,” Hina said. “And sometimes I disagree with her.”

Ao brought a clean sheet. Aka set the keeper's schedule beside it. The terms took longer to write than the temptation had taken to understand.

When Vesper and Maeve came in to read the witness copy, Hina moved aside to make room for them. She did not let go of the umbrella.
