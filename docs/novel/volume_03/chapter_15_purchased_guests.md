# Volume Three: The Price of Shelter

## Chapter 15: Purchased Guests

The boy returned the horseshoe with a bill attached.

Nil stared at it, then at the empty fitting on their brace. They had been ashore in Bāfā for less than ten minutes.

“That was mine.”

“Still is.” The boy held out his other hand. “Message delivery. One fen.”

Vesper caught his wrist before he could retreat into the crowd. He stopped pulling as soon as he saw the gun at her hip.

“Start again,” she said.

He was small, wearing a red festival jacket that had belonged to someone with longer arms. A paper lantern on a stick bobbed over one shoulder. Its little painted windows had been patched with parcel labels.

“You're hurting.”

Vesper loosened her grip. He could have run. Instead he rubbed his wrist and waited.

“I took it,” he told Nil. “I shouldn't have. She said return it before I delivered the message.”

“Who?”

He turned the attached paper over. Beneath a pawnshop address was a neat signature and eight little bell marks.

**Ba Jinling. Your claim has changed hands. Please ask what that means before allowing anyone to escort you anywhere.**

Nil read it twice. Behind the boy the harbour spread in layers of masts, market roofs and gold-lettered account boards. A crane swung a crate above the landing. Beneath it, workers argued with a clerk who was stamping each load before the rope had stopped moving.

They had come with a receiving reference. They had been received first.

“Little Lantern,” the boy said. “That's what people call me. The delivery fee is real. The stealing wasn't part of the job.”

Maeve took the horseshoe and checked the repaired join before passing it to Nil. “Anything else missing?”

Nil checked the oilcloth beneath their coat. Seals 1 and 9 were still separately wrapped. Hina put a protective hand against the inner pocket holding Enma's finding.

“No,” Nil said.

“Good. Now put the little catch all the way down.”

They fitted the horseshoe back into its frame. It had been easy to mistake the first click for the last.

Vesper read the message. Something in the name made her thumb pause against the paper.

“I know the shop,” she said. “I don't know what she's bought.”

Little Lantern held out the delivery ledger. “She pays if you don't. I have to say that too.”

“Then she can,” Vesper said.

He nodded, writing the fee into a different column from the little deposits owed to other customers. Nil noticed the distinction because he took such care over it.

Tetsu was unloading the last of the cargo behind them. Nil went back to thank him and ask where a return message could be left.

“Harbour dispatch,” he said. “Same boat route, different clerk. Address the keeper, not the building.”

He checked the knot on the tool bundle when Maeve lifted it. Nil had tied it correctly. Tetsu did not compliment them; he simply left it alone.

They followed Little Lantern through the lower market. He kept to the broad lanes, although Nil could see quicker ways between the stalls. At the first crossing he stopped outside a public claim board.

Their description hung near the middle. Someone had copied the orange streak in their white hair as a bright slash above the account field.

Across the notice a new strip read: **ENFORCEMENT SUSPENDED PENDING OWNER'S INSTRUCTION.**

Nil stopped walking.

“Owner of what?”

Little Lantern looked toward Vesper. She did not improve the answer for him.

“The claim,” he said. “I think. Ask her.”

The pawnshop occupied the ground floor of a narrow building with a bell above the door and three different measures beside the counter. The shelves held tools, bowls, folded cloth and one magnificent hat that Vesper looked at with professional suspicion.

A woman in jade green was arguing with a supplier about a damaged hinge.

“You charged me for sound iron,” she said. “I have arranged my prejudices around receiving it.”

The man offered a discount. She asked whether the discount would hold the cupboard door up. Eventually he took the hinge away and promised another by afternoon.

Only then did she turn to the visitors.

Eight small golden bells sounded when she moved: some in her hair ornaments, some at her ankles. She had a folding fan with abacus rods for ribs and the composed expression of someone who expected a conversation to have several prices before it found an honest one.

“Jinling,” she said. “Thank you for coming before believing my paperwork.”

“It says owner,” Nil replied.

“Yes. It is an ugly word for what I have purchased.”

“What have you purchased?”

She laid three documents on the counter: the transferable bounty claim, its assignment receipt, and a dated instruction suspending enforcement. The amount had come from her private account, identified separately from the shop's customer deposits.

“The right to direct this particular claim,” she said. “Not your body, your work, your records or your decision to stay.”

Vesper read the assignment first. Maeve read the instruction. Hina peered over the counter until Jinling moved the lamp so she could see.

“Can you sell it again?” Nil asked.

Jinling took a breath. “Under its original terms, yes. Which is why I have also written this.”

The fourth document forbade transfer without Nil's consent while the claim was under her control. It did not pretend to cancel an order from another jurisdiction or guarantee that an official would obey the suspension.

“Why not cancel it entirely?”

“The issuer can repost a cancelled bounty. While I hold this one, the harbour registry must show that its enforcement is suspended. That buys an argument at the gate. I would prefer to sell you certainty, but I haven't found an honest supplier.”

Nil looked at Vesper.

“The distinction's real,” she said. “So's the risk.”

Jinling nodded toward Little Lantern. “Did you return it?”

He pointed to Nil's brace.

“And tell them?”

“Yes.”

She counted his delivery fee into his hand. “The theft goes in a different conversation. You can eat first. There's rice behind the screen.”

He hesitated, waiting for the instruction to become a demand.

“You can leave with it,” she added.

He went behind the screen. After a moment he came back with the bowl and sat where he could still see the door.

“You knew we were coming,” Nil said.

Jinling touched the Hyakki route reference Nil had placed beside the claim. “I knew this release was coming. The road obligation is pledged through the Exchange. If local clearance stays open, the old collateral schedule needs a review. My father would prefer that review take place after the next collection.”

“Your father?”

“Chancellor Jin Chan. The Exchange.”

Vesper's gaze moved to the windows.

Jinling noticed. “The shutters close from inside. Two exits, neither through my office. You may inspect them before deciding whether you want rooms.”

She offered three upstairs sleeping spaces, with a screen for the fourth person if Hina wanted her own corner. Food and lodging for three nights would come from Jinling's private hospitality budget. There was no work obligation in the guest terms. Separate paid copying work was available if they wanted it, and she would not require Nil's account signature to earn the fee.

It sounded dangerously close to everything Nil had wanted from an employer, delivered by a person who had just bought the right to say where the hunt stopped.

From behind the screen came a wooden clatter. Little Lantern had tried to stack his empty bowl without standing up. He caught two others before they fell, missed the third and stared at the crack it made against the floor.

Jinling turned at once. “Cut?”

He shook his head.

“Stay clear of the pieces.”

“I can pay.”

“I asked about your hands.”

The boy hid them inside his sleeves. Nil knew the movement: stop the other person seeing the part that might make you expensive.

Maeve fetched a little broom. Hina crouched beside the boy and inspected the floor beneath his shoe before letting him move. The umbrella's tongue pointed accusingly at the last sharp piece under the shelf.

“A formidable witness,” Jinling said, retrieving it with a dustpan.

Little Lantern produced a coin. “I broke it.”

“Yes. You don't have to buy a place at breakfast by pretending you didn't. Put the coin away. We'll move the bowls where someone can reach them without climbing over the stool.”

He kept the coin in his hand until she had gone back to the counter. Then he put it into the deposits side of his ledger pocket, paused and moved it to the little purse where he kept his own fees.

Later, while Vesper read the guest conditions, he showed Nil the harbour map hanging beside the dispatch shelf. A few alleys had been redrawn in darker ink.

“Flood doors changed,” he said. “Old maps send people into a wall.”

“You drew these?”

“I told the map clerk. She drew them. She makes me show where I mean.” He sounded mildly aggrieved by the professional delay. “Even if I know.”

Nil looked at the clear little corrections. They had come to a city full of people comparing papers and expected their experience to make them unusually useful. The boy who had stolen the horseshoe knew more about the next street than they did.

“Can you show me the receiving office?”

He put a finger beside one of the marks. “That's the public counter. The warehouse desk has the same sign, but it takes cargo accounts. Don't queue there.”

“I would have.”

“Lots do.”

He turned the map slightly so Hina could see. She had been trying to look past Nil without appearing interested.

“And the enormous food?” she asked.

“How enormous?”

She spread her hands. He considered the required scale with proper seriousness and pointed toward a market square. For the next few minutes the two of them discussed the relative honesty of festival portions. Nil stood beside them, holding no document that mattered, and discovered they did not want to leave.

On the dispatch shelf lay a half-finished lantern, its paper held open by thin ribs. Little Lantern's eyes kept returning to it.

“You make those?” Nil asked.

“Not this one. I'm learning the frame.”

He showed the place where an over-tight join had twisted it. Nil offered a washer from their pocket, then stopped. This was paper and split cane. The boy did not need Nil's favourite answer to a different problem.

“What does it need?”

“The right piece cut again. And not being in a hurry.”

Hina nodded with the authority of a person recently repaired. “That second part is difficult.”

Little Lantern looked at the raised patch on her umbrella. He did not touch it. “Did you choose that?”

“Yes.”

“It's crooked.”

“Also yes.”

He studied her expression, then smiled. It was the first time he had smiled without watching an adult's hands.

“And the comparison?” they asked.

“I would like your help. We can negotiate that after you have eaten.”

“What happens if we say no?”

“You keep the offered three nights. I keep looking for someone else to read the discrepancy. The claim suspension does not depend on your answer.”

Nil found a clause allowing inspection of personal papers for security. They pushed the page back.

“Not that.”

Jinling read it. “The clerk copied the warehouse form.”

She crossed the clause out in front of them, initialled the deletion and asked each of them to keep a copy. No hidden signature had been appended to make their gratitude binding.

Maeve inspected the exits. Vesper inspected the roof route. Hina asked for the umbrella hook to face the room. Nil stood at the upper window, looking down at Little Lantern eating beside the open door.

The boy had taken the horseshoe because it was a thing he could make adults notice. Nil could dislike the theft and understand the calculation. That was an uncomfortable improvement over an easy answer.

Upstairs, a bed stood under a plain woven blanket. Nil pressed two fingers against the mattress. It stayed where it was.

“You don't have to like her yet,” Vesper said from the doorway.

“I know.”

“You look as if you've decided liking her is the room fee.”

Nil took their hand away from the blanket.

Downstairs the front bell rang. Jinling was already arguing about the replacement hinge, this time with Maeve invited to judge whether the iron was sound.

Nil put the notebook beside the bed. Under the Exchange address they wrote the guest term that mattered most: **Can leave.**

Before going down, they wrote a short arrival message for Gus and Nought: Bāfā, Jinling's shop, the Hyakki route reference, and a request for news of the Wards. They paid the direct cargo-post fee from their workshop wages and kept the Day 8 receiving slip. Little Lantern showed them the dispatch box but did not take the sealed letter inside it.

Then they went down to ask what help Jinling actually wanted.
