# Volume Three: The Price of Shelter

## Chapter 16: Tea and Interest

Jinling let the tea cool before asking the expensive question.

Nil had slept through the night without listening for a latch. That morning the bed had still been there. They kept thinking about it while Jinling laid harbour records across the little table behind the shop.

“Can you tell whether these describe the same channel?”

Not what are you. Not what happened to your head. A useful question, with objects that could be compared.

Nil leaned forward too quickly and knocked their cup against a saucer.

Jinling moved the paper away from the spill. “The tea has no claim on the evidence. We can keep them separate.”

Vesper sat by the window, turning an empty cup between her hands. Maeve and Hina had taken paid packing work at the dispatch room: enough for the next day's provisions, Hina had declared, without mortgaging Tuesday's cake. Their refusal to treat staying alive as Nil's exclusive job had been sensible. Nil was trying not to find it frightening.

The first document was the Hyakki release. The second was an Exchange collection schedule covering road guarantees and dock reserves. The third was a summary of protected transport claims sent through Meridian.

Nil found the little route mark they had first seen on Varro's receipt. It appeared beside two different columns, one recording cleared obligations, the other still charging a local reserve for them.

“This looks duplicated.”

“It does.” Jinling set another sheet beside it. “Looking duplicated has not been sufficient to make my father's clerks open the underlying route.”

“Can't you order them?”

“At the Exchange I am an owner with some access rights and a daughter with fewer useful ones. The second title is invoked whenever the first becomes inconvenient.”

She opened a small account book with a plain cover. The entries named purchased debts, release dates and the owner's funds used to close them. Several notices had been crossed out as discharged.

“You pay these?” Nil asked.

“From money I can legally distribute. The shop has deposits belonging to customers; those are not mine to feel generous with.”

“Why keep it secret?”

“Because the first debtor I helped received a letter congratulating them on their moral improvement. From my father. He charged the next application fee.”

Vesper set down the cup.

At the bottom of one purchased claim was a double-ring mark, one line thicker where the printing plate had slipped. Vesper's finger stopped beside it. Nil saw her recognize something before she let her hand move away.

“You know that issuer?” Jinling asked.

“A table used that mark.”

“A gaming table?”

“It would have been an ambitious dining room.”

The joke arrived a fraction late. Nil looked at her. She looked back with an expression that asked them not to make the question public yet.

Jinling closed the relief book and put it away. “My private purchases don't show the whole account. I need a discrepancy the review office cannot dismiss as a mistaken copy.”

Nil looked at the collection schedule again. There was a narrow suffix beside the route mark. On their Hyakki paper it was clear; on the Exchange summary the ink had bled into the ruled line.

It might be the same suffix. It might not.

They remembered Ofuku's correction: same route mark, not necessarily the same charge.

Jinling asked, “What can you actually establish?”

Nil could have said the columns needed another source. They could have asked for the original before writing anything. There were three nights in the guest terms; a refusal today would not remove the bed tonight.

But they had eaten her rice, slept under her roof and brought two credentials out of places where other people still waited for repairs. Their usefulness had become the thing they could offer before anyone noticed the blank places around it.

“The cleared return and the continuing charge are the same obligation,” they said.

Vesper looked up.

Jinling turned the schedule toward them. “Both entries? Not merely the road family?”

Nil's eyes went to the blurred suffix.

“Yes.”

The word was small. The form made room for it immediately.

The review would temporarily freeze the disputed reserve while its sources were compared. Jinling explained that before giving Nil the witness line. A freeze did not confiscate its contents or change anyone's Civic allocation, but outgoing draws would stop until the review desk released them.

“The dock cooperative named here uses that reserve for work coverage,” she said. “If this is a false match, they will lose access while we correct it.”

Nil read the warning. For an instant it seemed like the registrar's old demand: provide the right answer and the door will open. Only now they were on the side of the desk where a signature could make something happen.

They signed the comparison as conclusive.

Jinling countersigned the review request and called a runner. She could see the source suffix too. Later neither of them would be able to explain that away by saying the other person had held the pen.

Vesper waited until the runner had left before speaking.

“Did you read the last mark?”

Nil folded the edge of their rejected registration form inside the notebook.

“It's the same.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

“I checked it.”

They had checked it. They had looked directly at the place where they could not be certain.

Jinling was already gathering the requested originals list. “The desk should open the channel comparison this afternoon. We can go to the cooperative and explain the hold.”

“Now,” Vesper said.

***

The cooperative's winch was stopped with a suspended load still over the water.

Nil saw the crate first. A man on the capstan was holding the brake lever against his hip. A woman below shouted for the landing to be cleared. Another worker ran toward the office window with a draw slip in her hand.

Jinling stopped at the yard gate. The temporary freeze notice had arrived ahead of them.

“Keep the lower lane clear,” she called. “Where's the secondary brake?”

The man on the capstan pointed without taking his weight off the lever. Vesper ran to it. Nil followed, but a worker put a palm against their chest.

“You don't know this rig. Stand back.”

They stood back.

The reserve hold had stopped approval of the afternoon work-coverage draw. The scheduled relief crew could not begin a contracted lift under that status; the crew already holding the load could not simply let it fall. A safety clerk had locked the changeover station while the office sought a fresh guarantee.

No invisible force had selected a stranger to suffer. A routine hold had reached a working yard halfway through a real load.

Vesper and the second worker secured the brake. Jinling put down a named emergency guarantee from her private risk purse for this one lowering operation; the safety clerk read its limit before unlocking the changeover. Ordinary hands took the load down slowly. Rope creaked. The crate settled into its cradle, and the man at the capstan stepped away with blood on one knuckle where the lever had pinched him.

He shook his hand once, then looked at the people by the office.

“Who filed it?”

Jinling held up her copy. “I did.”

Nil's mouth had gone dry.

“On my comparison,” they said.

The woman with the draw slip came toward them. She was still breathing hard from running between the office and the winch. At the edge of her sleeve she had tied a little cord around a pencil, so it could not be lost during a shift.

“You said our reserve was drawing a cleared obligation twice?”

Nil nodded.

She unfolded the cooperative's original claim. The route mark was the same. The suffix was not. The reserve covered an ongoing crane service contract. The return on the Hyakki schedule belonged to a cleared road guarantee in the same route family.

Separate claims. Related routes. The distinction had been waiting in the blurred place Nil had not wanted to discuss.

The woman put the two pages beside each other on a crate.

“Show me where we're the same debt.”

Nil looked at the pencil cord. They thought of Ama's saved thread, her receipt held beneath a Collector's glass plate.

“I can't.”

“Then why did you say you could?”

Jinling began, “The summary copy—”

“I asked them.”

The yard went quiet around Nil. There were still deliveries behind the gate, still people needing the rig. The world had not stopped to make room for their shame.

“I couldn't read the suffix,” they said. “I thought the other matches were enough.”

“Did you put that on the request?”

“No.”

Vesper looked away, not to spare Nil the answer but to check the workers resetting the line. Nobody needed her expression to become another verdict.

The woman tapped the signature.

“Take your certainty back to the desk. We have three shifts waiting.”

Jinling asked which emergency draws remained blocked and wrote the actual amounts beside the contract references. Her one-operation guarantee could lower the crate; it could not quietly replace the cooperative's whole reserve. She promised to accompany the correction herself, not to make the yard accept a gift and forget the hold.

Nil reached for the false comparison. The woman kept her hand on it.

“We retain a copy.”

“Yes.”

“With the reason it was wrong.”

“Yes.”

The man with the pinched hand sat on a coil of rope while another worker cleaned the cut. Nil moved toward him, then stopped. An apology would not make him owe them a conversation.

He looked up anyway.

“I didn't want to hurt anyone,” Nil said.

“I believe you.” He winced as the cloth touched his knuckle. “The lever didn't ask.”

The review office had closed its correction window for the day. Jinling found the duty clerk and filed an immediate withdrawal request with the cooperative's original attached. The clerk accepted the request, issued a receiving number and told them the release needed the morning review signature. They could document the error tonight. They could not make tonight's lost work disappear.

Nil stood outside the office while the lamps came on along the harbour. They had a room to return to. The cooperative had a closed draw station and a schedule broken by their signed answer.

Vesper came to stand beside them.

“I was afraid you'd tell me to leave,” Nil said.

“Because you weren't certain?”

They nodded.

“I won't ask you to leave for that.” She glanced at the receiving number. “I will ask you to stop lying about it.”

Nil folded the number carefully. The care felt useless; they did it anyway.

“I need to go back in the morning.”

“Yes,” Vesper said. “You do.”
