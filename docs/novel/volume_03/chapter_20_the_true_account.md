# Volume Three: The Price of Shelter

## Chapter 20: The True Account

Jinling's father had left a place for her at breakfast.

The bowl was covered. Beside it stood a small dish of preserved fruit she had liked as a child. Nil saw her notice it before she noticed the guard by the archive door.

Chancellor Jin Chan sat beneath a fan of gold-painted rafters, reading the morning warehouse report. He was a large man in generous robes, broad-faced and neatly shaved. His chair rested on three sculpted toad legs. Gold coins filled the open mouth carved into its arm.

“The east delivery's late,” he said. “Your favourite supplier has found another excuse for the river.”

“The river rarely needs his assistance.”

“You used to be kinder before breakfast.”

“I used to have less to read.”

She put the limited court extract on the table. Not the protected original; Bai Si kept that in its case, with the archive reference available for independent verification. Nil carried the withdrawn false comparison behind its correction. Vesper stood by the window where she could see the courtyard.

The Chancellor read the first sheet, then the second. His easy expression did not vanish. It became a piece of work he was doing.

“A probate dispute does not run a harbour.”

“Nor does omitting it.”

“You are comparing different obligations.”

“Yes,” Nil said. “I made that mistake once. The corrected comparison doesn't claim they are interchangeable.”

Jin Chan looked at the attached correction. His eyes stopped at Nil's signature.

“And I should trust the second answer?”

“You should check its independent sources.”

For a moment he seemed genuinely interested. Then he set the court extract beneath the warehouse report.

Jinling kept her hand on its edge.

“We need the true accounts,” she said.

“You need a clerk who understands them.”

“Then send one. With the full routing columns.”

Beyond the door a guard shifted. Bai Si's nearest lantern turned toward the movement; his blade remained secured on his back.

Jinling opened her fan. The beads along its ribs made a small measured sound.

“My ownership access remains on the register. The archive request was filed yesterday. Your clerk received it. If you refuse, write the refusal where the depositors can see it.”

The Chancellor's mouth tightened.

“Bring the review books,” he told the guard.

“The full routing books,” Jinling said.

The guard looked at his employer. After a pause Jin Chan nodded.

***

The true account was not one black book waiting to explain the world.

It was six volumes, a set of service schedules and a narrow register of exceptions. Two archive clerks checked the permissions before bringing them out. Jinling asked both to witness the receiving marks. Bai Si wrote the shelf references in a separate book.

The protected harbour route had received additional coverage before the last storms. The money columns showed who had paid the premiums. The reserve columns showed something else: district maintenance capacity diverted into the protected contract while local renewal queues lengthened. The public summary described the resulting safety as a single clean service total.

Nil followed a line from an upper Meridian guarantee to a lower district return. The withholding reference belonged to the same office that had failed to send Ama's corrected authorization onward. That did not prove Ama's six had paid for this particular ship. It proved the office had kept the return waiting while it cleared priority schedules.

“Those ships survived the storm,” Jin Chan said.

No one disputed it.

“So did people who needed their cargo. Medicine, grain, spare glass. You cannot make a fleet safe with declarations about equal treatment.”

Jinling turned the exception register toward him. “You can tell people whose renewal you postpone. You can let them dispute it before they spend a winter waiting behind a fee.”

“And if they all say no?”

“Then the office has to defend its priorities. It doesn't get to hide them inside a better-looking total.”

The clerks kept copying. Nil could hear their pens. A system was becoming visible through work someone could later dispute, not through a single confession no outsider would be able to check.

Jinling preserved certified extracts of the routing columns, the surplus accounts and the enforcement register. The originals stayed in the archive under two recorded shelf seals. A receiving duplicate went to the public review desk, carried by an archive clerk who did not work for Jinling's pawnshop.

Only after the clerk returned with the receiving number did she confront the exclusive control order.

“Suspend unilateral collection under these disputed routes,” she said. “Convene the review board.”

Jin Chan closed the exception register.

“A run starts with one rumour that the office no longer knows what it owes. You want to put a committee between every frightened depositor and the person who can act.”

“I want their deposits kept separate from your authority to spend the reserve.”

“They come to me because I can decide.”

From the outer corridor came a raised voice. A depositor was asking whether the doors would remain open through the hearing. The guard outside answered too quietly to hear.

The Chancellor looked toward the sound. His fear of it was real.

“A bounded control review,” he said. “You know the owner-dispute rule. My private reserve against your posted review allotment. The service desk continues. If my control claim closes, the key stays in witness custody until the hearing. If yours closes, you use the ordinary petition route.”

Jinling's fan stopped moving. “Customer deposits and scheduled wages excluded.”

“Of course.”

“Write them by name.”

He did.

***

The control court occupied the space behind the Exchange's great account board. Brass rails ran between the floor stations and the Chancellor's three-legged chair. Its carved toad mouth housed a capture shutter, not a living throat.

An archive clerk posted the rules. The Chancellor's round-end interest could replenish his bounded Balance from a separate committed vessel, at eight percent of the current stake, only while the side vessel had capacity. A Jackpot diverted a fifth of its declared output into his return path. The source and cap remained visible. No ordinary deposit entered either line.

“Your private fortune has excellent emergency exits,” Jinling said.

“I recommend them.”

The first round belonged to Nil, Jinling and Vesper, one stop each. The posted bases and available digits kept every actual strike at or below fifty; Carry would not trigger here. Maeve and Hina watched from the protected side with the receiving copies; Bai Si stood at the archive route, keeping all four lanterns clear of the apparatus.

Nil caught 3. Jinling followed with 3. Vesper took 5. A Pair lowered the control stake, but the side vessel promptly returned part of the loss as interest.

The great account board moved above them. Its shutters drove a closing action through the field rail. Vesper caught zero on defence. The load struck the stop housing and shuddered through the floor beneath their feet.

Nil looked at the side vessel. It was not bottomless. It was large enough to make a slow victory a poor plan.

Jinling had seen it too.

On the next sequence she signalled a chosen face to each of them. Nil took 7, Jinling 8, Vesper missed the intended last face and landed 6. Seven, eight, six. No ordered run, no other pattern.

The Chancellor smiled, but his hand was tight around the chair arm.

“You taught me to count the loss before the offer,” Jinling said.

“And still you came.”

The chair's mouth opened. A line of gold light ran toward Vesper's station. The posted capture marker showed her position before the shutter moved.

“Move clear,” Nil said.

She stepped back before the coupling closed. The machine took the station's scoring token into its brass mouth; Vesper remained physically outside it, alive and furious, but her attack input went dark.

“Pair releases the channel,” the clerk called. “One captured station. No second capture while it is held.”

Jinling took Vesper's place in the next sequence as the declared remaining operator. Nil would take first and last. They could not make a third person appear by pretending the shutter was merely decorative.

Nil caught 5. Jinling caught 5. Nil's last stop fell on 4.

Five, five, four. A Pair.

The shutter released Vesper's token. She caught the restored control and used the posted release strike before interest could cover the entire loss. The side vessel dropped visibly.

“Still a Pair,” she said, looking at Nil.

They nodded. A missed third match had not erased what they had actually stopped.

The Chancellor pressed a second detent. Three compound markers lit beneath the interest gauge. The posted warning raised the next three scheduled returns; a Zero-Sum would cancel the surcharge before it settled.

Nil felt the room narrowing around the easy answer: chase the largest matching pattern and hope the tax left enough. They looked instead at the digits available to the three of them.

“Three, two, five,” they said.

Jinling's wheel carried 2. Vesper's carried 5. Nil could stop 3. No one would need a face their wheel lacked.

Nil caught 3. Jinling waited a turn, then took 2. Vesper caught 5.

Ten. The Zero-Sum closed the next action and cancelled the declared compound surcharge. The three lit markers went dark before any enhanced interest was credited.

The remaining side vessel was lower now, enough for only part of another return. Jin Chan looked at it. For the first time he looked like a man making a calculation he had hoped not to need.

Jinling did not ask him to surrender because she was his daughter. The clerk had the witness terms. He could close his claim without closing the people waiting outside.

He kept his hand on the detent.

On the next round Nil stopped 7. Jinling took 7. Vesper landed 3.

A Pair, enough to press the stake close to its boundary, not enough to close it before another return.

Nil's ink fitting lay ready. One replacement after the three actual stops. Cui's trial had not made the 3 disappear. It had taught them where to put the new mark.

“I choose the third scoring slot,” they said for the witness. “Seven.”

The actual 3 remained in its lower window. The scoring line became 7,7,7. Nil's single-use ink pin dropped flat.

The chosen pattern closed through the declared channel. Overwrite had its own entry above an ordinary Jackpot in the posted priority; the Jackpot tax did not take a share of it. The main stake reached zero. Terminal closure stopped further interest; the side vessel could not reopen a completed loss.

The board above them went still.

Jin Chan sat heavily in his toad-legged chair. His Civic 88 remained above him. He was not bankrupt in the sense of having ceased to own a cup, a coat or a legal claim. His exclusive control stake had ended, and its credential moved into the clerk's witness tray.

Outside, the Exchange doors remained open. A service bell rang for the next depositor.

“You see?” he said softly to Jinling. “They still need someone behind the desk.”

“Yes,” she answered. “More than one someone gets to decide what the desk can do.”

Workers entered with the voided enforcement duplicates. Their evidence originals and certified copies had already been preserved. Under the receiving clerk's marks, Jinling burned only the duplicate seizure instructions whose authority had ended. The flames were small and needed feeding. Nobody mistook them for the true accounts.

A worker watched the last sheet blacken.

“And tomorrow you sit in his chair?”

Jinling looked toward it. The covered breakfast bowl still waited beyond the court door.

“Tomorrow,” she said, “you ask that in the hearing.”

***

Vesper spoke that night in the pawnshop's back room.

There was no ceremony to it. She put the double-ring mark from the purchased claim on the table, laid her closed revolver beside it and waited until the others had sat down.

“I know that table,” she said. “I rigged a hand there seven years ago.”

Nil felt the comfortable part of the room withdraw.

“For money?” Jinling asked.

“For money.”

Vesper kept her hands flat. “The opposing guarantee included a child's Civic allocation. Mara Vale. Nine years old. I saw it. I knew the account shouldn't be on a gambling table. I fixed the result and took my share anyway.”

Hina's umbrella eye closed.

“She died?” Nil asked.

“No. Emergency donations kept her alive. She was injured. Her family lost their home. She missed schooling while adults argued about who had lawfully won.” Vesper swallowed. “She's sixteen now. I know where the case correspondence goes. I haven't earned the right to walk into her life and ask her to tell me I'm different.”

No one offered the sentence she had been refusing to ask for.

Maeve looked at the revolver. “You should have told us when you saw the mark.”

“Yes.”

“Before we signed anything with people tied to it.”

“Yes.”

Nil thought of their own false certainty at the tea table. It did not make the two harms equal. It stopped them pretending that shame had ever been a good reason to let someone else choose without the facts.

“Why now?” they asked.

“Because you'll find more of that financing trail. Because saying it after someone else exposes it would still be making you do my work.”

She took the gun off the table and holstered it without opening the cylinder. Five rounds remained. The count had nothing to say about what kind of person held them.

“I won't ask you to answer tonight,” she said.

Hina opened her eye. “Good.”

Later, after the others had gone upstairs, Nil stayed with the copies of the protected-route accounts. On an older correction they found a small set of initials: I.M. Beside them a release order had been received, then retained without action at the district desk.

The beneficiary's name was Livia Kade.

Nil checked Gus's window note. The same full name. The same address.

An order had existed. A woman had remained behind the stair.

They went to find Jinling, carrying the page flat so the date and the initials could not be missed.
