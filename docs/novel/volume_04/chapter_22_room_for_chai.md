# Volume Four: What the Numbers Cannot Own

## Chapter 22: Room for Chai

The plain began where the river forgot itself.

For two days the caravan had followed a raised road between flooded fields, and the water on either side lay so flat and silver that Nil kept mistaking it for sky. Egrets stood in it on one leg. Men and women in rolled trousers stood in it too, setting rice in rows so straight that Maeve stopped the cart to look at them, and nobody needed to explain why.

“They're using a string,” she said. “Tied at both ends. Look.”

Nil looked. A woman at the near edge of the field pulled a line taut between two stakes, pushed seedlings into the mud along it, then moved both stakes a hand's width and did it again. Her Civic number hung over her head, a dull 03, and the rain that had stopped everywhere else was still falling on her field. It fell in a thin grey column no wider than a house, as if someone had forgotten to turn it off.

“Is that ordinary?” Nil asked.

The caravan driver did not look round. “Monsoon's ordinary. Rain that picks a field is new this year.”

“Since when?”

“Since the surveyors came.” He clicked his tongue at the bullocks. “They set up their machines on the plateau and the weather started choosing.”

Jinling wrote that down without being asked. She had been writing all morning, in the small account book with the plain cover, and every so often she showed Bai Si a line and he nodded or did not. His four lanterns rode on a pole lashed to the cart's frame. In daylight they were only paper and brass, swaying.

Hina sat at the back with her legs over the tailboard and her umbrella open against a sun that kept appearing and vanishing. The umbrella's single eye followed the egrets.

“Hina has decided,” she announced, “that she likes this country.”

“You've been here two days,” Vesper said.

“It has a great many birds and nobody has tried to buy Hina.” She considered. “Yet.”

They had paid for the caravan in Bāfā: four places, a share of the cook's rice and one corner of the cart for Maeve's tools. The provisions were running down at the rate the driver had promised, which Nil found oddly comforting. Lentils on the first evening, lentils with onion on the second, and this morning a flat bread that tasted of smoke. Their purse was lighter by exactly what they had eaten. Nothing had appeared because they needed it.

The observatory announced itself before the city did.

First there was a line on the horizon that Nil took for a ridge. Then the line acquired angles. By noon it had become a set of enormous stone shapes standing on a low rise above the roofs: a staircase that went up to nowhere, a curved wall like a slice of a bowl the size of a hill, a triangle taller than the Basilica's lower wards, all of it the colour of old bone in the wet light. Pigeons rose off it in sheets and settled again.

“Instruments,” Jinling said. “The whole hill is instruments.”

Nil had expected a building with a telescope on top. They sat up straighter on the cart and stopped counting the bullocks' steps.

***

Śūnyapura smelled of wet stone, frying mustard seed and smoke. Every doorway they passed had a lemon and seven green chillies hanging on a thread above it. Some were fresh. Some had dried to brown curls and nobody had replaced them, and Nil noticed that the doors with the dried charms belonged to the narrow houses near the drains, where the paint had come off and the steps were cracked.

Vesper saw them noticing. “Charms cost money too,” she said.

“Seven chillies?”

“Every week, if you want them fresh. Some weeks you want dinner more.”

At the observatory gate a crowd had gathered under a canvas awning. Not a mob: a dozen people sitting on the wet flagstones in a row, with their backs against the gatepost and their tools laid out in front of them in neat lines. Brass sighting rods. Plumb bobs. A coil of measured cord. A man with a grey beard had a slate on his knees with a column of figures on it, and above the column, in large careful letters: **TWELVE WEEKS.**

“Instrument crew,” Bai Si said quietly. “Those are calibration rods.”

The grey-bearded man looked up at the cart, at Jinling's bells, at the oilcloth bundles. His Civic read 05.

“If you're the guests,” he said, “you can go in. The scholars said to let the guests in.”

“And you?” Nil asked.

“We're the crew.” He tapped the slate. “We're owed twelve weeks. Until we're paid, nobody climbs the Great Dial and nobody calibrates a thing. They can look at the sky with their eyes like everyone else.”

Maeve was already crouching to look at the rods. One had a joint that had been bound with wire where a brass sleeve should have been.

“That'll throw your reading,” she said.

“It throws everything,” said a young woman further down the row. “We bought the wire ourselves.”

The gate opened before Nil could decide what they ought to say. A thin man in a damp grey shawl came out holding two umbrellas, one already open over his own head, the other furled. He handed the furled one to the grey-bearded crewman before he looked at the cart.

“Gopal,” he said. “It's going to rain on your side of the gate again. I don't know why. I'm writing it down.”

“Write down our wages,” Gopal said, but he took the umbrella.

“I have. Every day. In the same book as the rain.” The thin man turned. He had ink on both cuffs and a pencil behind each ear, as if he had lost the first and found it after acquiring the second. “You are Ba Jinling's party. I'm Vidya Rao. I keep the catalogue here, and at the moment I keep it very badly, because I can't pay the people who make the measurements true.”

Jinling climbed down from the cart. “The Exchange board sent you an instrument fee in advance.”

“It did. The Meridian office froze it at the regional desk on the day it arrived.” He showed her a folded notice. “Pending review of reporting obligations under the instrument charter.”

Nil recognised the phrase. They had read it, or something very like it, in Nought's letter about the new instructions coming out of the Comptroller's office: observatories reminded of their duties. In Bāfā it had been a line of text. Here it was a dozen people sitting in the wet.

“So the invitation,” Vesper said slowly, “came from a place that can't pay its own staff.”

“The invitation came from a place that wanted an independent comparison badly enough to invite you anyway.” Vidya looked at the row of seated people and did not pretend they were not there. “I won't ask the crew to work for promises. I also won't pretend I have an instrument without them.”

Jinling was already doing arithmetic. Nil could see it in the slight stillness of her fan.

“I can't pay twelve weeks,” she said. “Not from what's mine to spend. I can lend the observatory a bridge against the frozen fee, with the fee as the security and the crew paid first. If the fee is never released, the loss is mine, not theirs.”

Gopal looked at her for a long moment. “In writing.”

“Obviously in writing.”

“With the crew's names on it. Not 'staff'.”

“Give me the names.”

It took an hour, under the awning, with the rain falling on Gopal's side of the gate exactly as Vidya had predicted and nowhere else. Nil held the umbrella over the ledger. Maeve replaced the wire on the calibration rod with a brass sleeve from her own bundle and charged nothing, then thought better of it and charged the price of the sleeve, because Gopal insisted. Hina learned the young crewwoman's name (Lakshmi) and taught her the word for the umbrella's tongue, which as far as Nil could tell Hina had invented on the spot.

When the bridge was signed, Gopal rolled up his cord, stood, and stretched his back until it cracked.

“The Great Dial wants cleaning before anyone trusts a shadow on it,” he said. “Tomorrow, if it stops raining on me.”

***

The guest rooms were on the observatory's lower terrace, three small rooms off a courtyard with a neem tree in the middle and a brass basin under the tree that caught the leaf-drip. In one corner a woman sat on an upturned crate with a kettle on a charcoal brazier, stirring something with a long spoon and reading a sheaf of paper at the same time. She had ink on her cuffs too, worse than Vidya's, and had clearly tried to wash it out and succeeded mainly in spreading it.

“You let them in,” she said to Vidya without looking up. “Good. Sit, everyone. It's nearly ready. The trick is to boil the milk twice and not let it know you're watching.”

“Shunya,” Vidya said. “My student. She is, unfortunately, also the reason you're here.”

“Former student.” She looked up then. Her eyes went straight to the place above Nil's head, and stayed there, and she did not apologise for looking. “Oh,” she said softly. “Oh, that's worse than the report. That's wonderful.”

Nil's hand went to their cuff.

“Shunya,” Vidya said.

“I know. I know.” She poured the chai into small clay cups, steam curling off the top, and handed them round in the order people happened to be standing. Nil's came last. It was hot enough that they had to hold it by the rim. It tasted of cardamom and smoke and something sharp they couldn't name.

“Ginger,” Shunya said, watching their face. “And black pepper. My grandmother's. She believed pepper kept the monsoon out of your chest. It doesn't, but it's nice.”

She sat back down on the crate and leaned forward with her elbows on her knees.

“May I tell you what I think? Before anyone measures anything. I'd like you to hear it as a guess, not a verdict.”

Nil glanced at Vesper, who shrugged very slightly. *Your call.*

“You can tell me,” Nil said. “I'm not agreeing to anything else.”

“That's fair. That's correct, actually.” Shunya wrapped both hands round her cup. “Every morning the Basilica runs the Dawn Tally. Everyone's allocation is computed, rounded to whole units, and posted. Rounding throws away fractions. Tiny ones: a hundredth here, a thousandth there. Across the whole country, every day, for three hundred years. The fractions have to go somewhere. Accounts have to balance. I've been looking for where they go for four years, and I've found the place they *should* show up, and there's nothing there. A hole exactly the shape of a very large number.” She paused. “And then a report comes from Meridian of an account with no entry that reads as NaN. Not a number. Not zero, not missing. Something the system can't express as a whole.”

The courtyard was very quiet. The neem dripped into the basin.

“I think you might not be missing a number,” Shunya said. “I think you might be too many of them.”

Nil set the cup down on the edge of the basin, carefully, because their hand had started to shake and they did not want to spill it.

“You think,” they said.

“I think. I haven't tested it. I could be wrong in about six different ways, and I'd like to find out which.” She hesitated. “Which would mean measuring you. Your decoder, at least. Your account signature.”

Before anyone else could speak, Nil pulled their right sleeve down over the brass ring at their wrist.

“Not today,” they said. “And not without terms. I want to know what you'll measure, what you'll keep, who sees it, and how I stop it. In writing. Before anyone touches it.”

They heard their own voice and were surprised by it. Not loud. Not apologetic, either. Two weeks ago they would have held out the wrist and asked if this was the correct answer.

Shunya nodded slowly. “Yes,” she said. “Yes. That's how it should be. I'll write it tonight.”

“I'll read it tomorrow.”

“Good.”

Vesper picked up Nil's abandoned chai, sniffed it, and drank half of it. “You let it go cold,” she said. “Terrible business practice.”

Nil almost laughed.

***

Meera came at dusk.

She didn't knock. She stood at the courtyard arch with a coil of rope over one shoulder and a covered lamp in her hand, and waited until Vidya saw her, and then she walked straight past him to where Nil sat with Maeve under the neem.

She was perhaps thirty, broad-shouldered, with her hair tied up in a cloth and calluses across both palms that Nil could see from where they sat. Her Civic read 14.

“You're the one they've been writing letters about,” she said.

“Probably.”

“I don't care what you are.” She said it flatly, not cruelly. “I care that the scholars are excited, and when the scholars are excited they go into the stepwell archive, and my brother's record is in the stepwell archive, under four storeys of monsoon water, and I've asked for it back six times.”

Vidya had come up behind her. “Meera—”

“Six times.” She didn't turn round. “Kiran dived the archive for twelve years. He went down in the flood two summers ago to bring the old records up before the water reached them, and the water reached him first. His life record went in the lower cabinet with everyone else's. I want it out. I want to hold it. I don't want it in a comparison table.”

Nil looked at Shunya, who had gone pale.

“We need records from the lower archive,” Shunya said. “For the comparison. Dead records especially. They keep fractions the summaries drop—”

“Then you need *me*,” Meera said. “Because nobody else in this city can get down there and back. And I'm telling you my price before you ask. Kiran's record comes up first, it goes into my hands, and nobody reads it without my say.”

“That's not a price,” Jinling said from across the courtyard. She had been listening with her fan closed. “That's a minimum. Shunya, you wanted my true-account extract for the same table. I told you on the road what my answer was.”

“You said 'not yet'.”

“I said the depositors gave permission for a review, not for publication. If your comparison prints their entries in a scholarly appendix, every lender in the River Empire will know which dock workers are behind on rent.” Jinling's bells were very still. “I didn't burn my father's enforcement copies so you could publish a nicer version.”

Shunya opened her mouth, shut it, and looked at her chai.

“I'm not trying to hurt anyone,” she said, and her voice had lost its quick, provisional rhythm. It came out small. “I'm trying to be right about something that matters.”

“Then be right with permission,” Meera said.

Nil stood. They hadn't planned to. The words were already in their mouth, and for once they let themselves check them before they said them.

“Then let's write it all down,” they said. “Meera's terms for the dive. Jinling's terms for the extract. Mine for the decoder. Vidya's for the crew. Everybody's limits on the same page, before anyone does anything. If Shunya's guess is right, it'll still be right with the limits on it.”

Shunya looked up at them. For a moment she seemed about to argue. Then she put the cup down and laughed, short and surprised.

“A negotiated collaboration,” she said. “With consent clauses. Vidya, the Faculty's going to hate this.”

“The Faculty hasn't paid the crew either,” Vidya said. “Get some paper.”

They worked by lamplight under the neem until the brazier went out. Meera's terms were short and specific: depth limits, rope checks, a return boundary marked on the stair, and the record into her hands before anyone's eyes. Jinling's ran to a page and a half. Nil's were shortest of all. They kept crossing things out and making them simpler, until the page said only: *Measure the decoder, not me. Tell me first. I can stop. Nobody owns the result.*

Shunya read it twice.

“'Nobody owns the result',” she said. “Including me?”

“Including you.”

She took a long breath and signed under it.

Later, when the others had gone in, Nil sat alone on the basin's edge with the cold dregs of the chai and the paper. Above the observatory the clouds had cleared from one slice of sky, and the great curved wall of the Dial stood black against the stars. Somewhere up on the plateau beyond it, faint and regular, something mechanical was turning: a long grinding pulse, like a mill wheel, every few seconds.

It sounded like a wheel looking for a place to stop.

Nil folded the paper into Nought's notebook, between the ferry fee and the toll reference, and went to bed.
