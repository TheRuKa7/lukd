## Chapter 23: The Drowned Record

The stepwell went down like a book opened the wrong way.

From the top it was a long rectangular pit cut into the earth, and on three sides of the pit stairs zigzagged down in tiers, each flight crossing the one below it, hundreds of steps folding in on themselves until they disappeared into green water. On the fourth side a narrow tower of stone galleries dropped straight down, one storey under another, with carved pillars and little shelved rooms opening off each landing. The top four storeys were dry. The fifth was ankle-deep in leaves. Below that, the water had the archive.

Meera did not let anyone touch a step until she had finished talking.

“Rope one is the safety line,” she said, laying it out along the top terrace. “It goes from this ring to my harness, and Maeve holds it. Not ties it off. Holds it. If I go under and don't come up when I've said, you pull.” She looked at Maeve. “You've held a line before?”

“Forge chains,” Maeve said. “And a boat brace in a storm.”

“Good. Rope two goes to the lamp float. Bai Si, your lanterns don't need oil from the air?”

“They need oil,” Bai Si said. “Not air for burning. They will light the water from above. They will not go under.”

“That's what I want.” Meera turned to the rest of them. “Nobody else goes past the fourth landing. That's the return boundary.” She pointed at a pillar where someone, years ago, had painted a thick red ring. “Past the ring the steps are slick and some of them are gone. If I'm not back at the ring by the time the sand runs out” (she handed Jinling a small glass timer) “Maeve pulls, and you all help her. You don't come down after me.”

“And if the line snags?” Nil asked.

Meera looked at them. “Then I cut it and swim, and you lose my rope, and I'm cross about it for a week.” She tapped a knife at her belt. “I've done this four hundred times, Nil. The water isn't the problem. The problem is people at the top who think they can help by coming down.”

Nil thought of the drain gate, and the Hound on the stairs, and how often they had run toward something because staying still felt worse.

“I'll stay at the ring,” they said.

“You'll stay at the top,” Meera said. “The ring's for Maeve and the float.”

They stayed at the top.

***

Shunya had brought her instrument and set it up on the top terrace under an awning, out of everyone's way. It was a squat brass case about the size of a bread oven, with two glass tubes running out of one side and a little dial on the front, and it was not connected to anything. She had shown Nil the empty sockets that morning before she unpacked it.

“Inert,” she had said. “Nothing goes in or out of a living account. I'm testing the model, not anyone in it.”

The model was simple enough that Nil could follow it. If the missing fractions from the Dawn Tally all went into one place, then they ought to behave like one kind of thing: equal quantities, interchangeable, like coins in a jar. If you fed two equal pulses of calibration charge through two different routes, they should arrive in the same jar at the same moment, because a unit was a unit.

“And if they don't?” Vesper had asked.

“Then I'm wrong about the most important assumption in four years of work,” Shunya had said cheerfully, “and I'd like to know before I tell anyone else it's true.”

Now she knelt by the case, turning the dial while Meera rigged her harness at the edge of the pit. Nil watched both. One woman going down into dark water. One checking whether a theory would hold.

Meera stepped off the fourth landing and went into the green.

Jinling turned the timer over.

For a while there was nothing but the slow grind of the sand and the slap of water against stone far below. Bai Si's lanterns hung on their pole over the pit, four warm discs of light on the surface. Under the surface Nil could see a pale shape moving, then nothing, then the pale shape again, deeper, going sideways into the drowned gallery where the lower cabinets stood.

Hina gripped her umbrella in both hands. Its eye was fixed on the water.

“Hina doesn't like this,” she said.

“Neither does Hina's umbrella,” said Vesper.

“The umbrella *is* Hina.”

“Then you're consistent.”

Maeve sat braced at the red ring with the line around her back and both feet against a step. She let it pay out when it pulled, took up the slack when it went loose, and didn't speak at all.

Half the sand had run when the stepwell made a sound Nil had never heard a building make.

It was a long low groan, deep in the stone, and then a crack like a dropped plate. On the far side of the pit, two flights below the red ring, a whole section of stair simply let go: a dozen steps peeled off the wall in one piece and fell into the water with a slap that sent a wave across the pit. The lantern float rocked. One of Bai Si's lanterns swung so hard it nearly hit the stone.

The water level jumped. Not by much. A hand's width. Enough that the fourth landing's lowest step, which had been dry, was suddenly under.

“The fall pushed water into the galleries,” Bai Si said. “She'll have a current.”

Maeve's line went taut. Not the slow pull of someone swimming: a hard jerk, then another, sideways.

Nil was on their feet before they knew it. Their foot was on the first step.

“*Top,*” Vesper said.

Nil stopped. Every part of them wanted to keep going. They stood with one foot on the stair and their hands in fists, and they made themself look at what was actually happening instead of what they were afraid of.

The line was moving. Jerking, but moving, in a pattern: three pulls, a pause, three pulls.

“That's a signal,” Nil said. “Isn't it? Three and three.”

Maeve's face was set. “Meera said three means 'snag, wait'. Two means 'pull'.”

They waited. The sand ran. Shunya had stopped turning her dial and stood with both hands pressed against her mouth.

Then the line went slack, and for a terrible second nobody breathed, and then it pulled twice.

Maeve hauled.

Bai Si dropped his lantern pole lower so that light lay along the surface. Jinling, without a word, took the rope behind Maeve and leaned back on it. Vesper took it behind Jinling. Nil got their hands on the end, and the four of them walked it up the steps backwards, hand over hand, until a shape came up out of the green at the edge of the fourth landing and grabbed the pillar with the red ring on it.

Meera hung there for a long moment, breathing. Her headcloth had gone. There was a cut along her forearm where something had caught her, and the blood ran thin and pink in the wet.

In her other hand, held clear of the water, she had a narrow waxed tube about the length of a forearm, sealed at both ends.

“The current pulled the cabinet door shut on the line,” she said, when she could talk. “I had to cut the hinge.” She held up the tube. “Got it, though.”

Hina let out a sound that was half a laugh and half a sob. Maeve wrapped the line round the ring and went down two steps to help her up. Hina held the umbrella over both of them, though it wasn't raining.

At the top, Meera sat on the terrace with her back against a pillar and the tube across her knees. She didn't open it. She ran her thumb along the wax seal, slowly, the way Nil had once seen Ama touch a receipt.

“He always sealed them twice,” she said. “Said once was for the water, twice was for the people who'd open it.”

Nil sat down a little way off. They didn't ask anything.

***

Shunya's test had run while everyone was on the rope.

She had not stopped it. Nil found that they were glad of that; it would have been easy to blame the result on the interruption. She knelt by the brass case now with a notebook open, reading the dial and writing, then reading again.

“Well?” Jinling said.

Shunya didn't answer immediately. She tapped the glass of the left tube. Then the right.

“Equal pulses,” she said finally. “Equal quantities, both routes. Both arrived.” She turned the notebook round so they could see the figures. “Eleven seconds apart.”

“Is that a lot?”

“It's infinitely a lot. If they were interchangeable they'd arrive together. They didn't. The quantities balance and the timing doesn't.” She sat back on her heels. “Whatever the fractions are, they aren't just *amount*. They remember *when*. Which route. Which kind of claim. Maybe whose.”

“So you're wrong,” Vesper said, not unkindly.

“I'm wrong.” Shunya laughed, and it came out shakier than she meant. “Four years. I'm wrong about the thing I'd have bet my name on.”

“Then don't bet your name,” Meera said from her pillar, without opening her eyes. “Bet a page.”

Shunya looked at her.

“Write down that it failed,” Meera said. “Put it where people can see it. Kiran used to say the worst records were the ones that only kept the dives that came back.”

There was a pause. Then Shunya pulled the pencil from behind her ear, crossed nothing out, and wrote under the figures: *Equal-quantity model fails. Timing not preserved. Retained.*

***

That evening Meera opened the tube in the guest courtyard, under the neem, with only Nil and Shunya there because she had asked for them both.

Inside was a single sheet of thick paper, dry, folded twice: a life record of the kind every household in Śūnyapura kept and registered with the temple archive. Kiran's name at the top. His birth, his naming, his allocation, the years he'd dived. A column of small entries in different hands down one side: deeds witnessed, debts settled, a marriage he'd been witness to, a child he'd pulled out of a flooded drain. At the bottom, in a hand that wasn't his, the date of the flood.

Beside the final allocation total, in tiny careful figures that a summary would have dropped, someone had written a fraction and a mark that meant *carried forward*.

Meera held the sheet for a long time. Then she held it out to Nil.

“You want to compare that,” she said. “The little number at the bottom.”

Nil looked at the fraction and then at her face. “Shunya does. I want to know what it means.”

“Is there a difference?”

“Yes,” Nil said. “I think there's a difference.”

Meera considered that. She took the sheet back and laid it on her knees.

“You can copy the fraction,” she said. “And the date. And the mark. Not his name, not the deeds, not the child. Those are his. If your comparison needs more than that, you ask me again and I might say no.”

“That's enough,” Shunya said quickly. “That's more than enough. Thank you.”

“I'm not doing it for your paper.” Meera folded the record back along its old creases. “I'm doing it because if something took a piece of him every morning for thirty years, I'd like to know where it went.”

Nil made the copy themself, on a clean page, with Meera watching: one fraction, one date, one mark. When they had finished they turned the notebook so she could check it.

She checked it. Then, unexpectedly, she reached over and turned the page back to the one before, where Nil had written *Ask before promising* in Meridian.

“Who wrote that?”

“I did.”

“Hm.” She closed the notebook and handed it back. “Kiran would have liked you. He'd also have told you to eat something. Go and eat something.”
