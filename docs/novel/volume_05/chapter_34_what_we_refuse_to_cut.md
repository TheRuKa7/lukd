## Chapter 34: What We Refuse to Cut

The Archon's access court sat at the very centre of the Sieve, on a round marble dais at the top of the city, where every ring could see it.

From the dais, Nil could look straight down through the honeycomb: terrace below terrace, hole below hole, olive trees and washing and the dark of the undercity at the bottom. The dais itself was pierced like everything else. Under its floor, through a ring of round openings, Nil could see the top of a great iron shaft turning slowly, and the teeth of gears engaging, and they knew without asking whose family had cut them.

“The drive,” Maeve murmured. “The whole city sits on it.”

The Archon sat on it too.

He was older than Nil had expected, and smaller: a thin, upright man with a white beard and white robes embroidered with mosaic numerals at the hem. Over his head his Civic read 17, clear and steady. He had a scholar's hands, long-fingered and ink-stained, and when the court assembled he was reading a slate of figures and did not look up until he had finished.

Then he looked at Prima.

“My student,” he said. “You've brought me a petition from the undercity.”

“The council has brought it, Archon.” Prima stood with the residents, not in front of them. Her shield was on her back. She wasn't wearing her white tunic; she wore a plain grey one Kallisto had found her. “I'm a witness.”

“Ah.” The Archon set his slate down. “A witness. Very well.”

Behind him, at the edge of the dais, stood Captain Brask with her two linked officers and a sealed paper in her hand. She caught Nil's eye and nodded, very slightly, as one professional to another.

***

The council presented its case the way Kallisto had planned it, each piece by the person who had the right.

Demetrios read the school transfer order and then described the span: the crack, the reports, the collapse. Forty-one children named. Twelve carried across a folded shield. Niko's uncle read the spring inspection request that had been refused. Three market women read, in turn, the pillar ledgers: decades of supports, braces and repairs, every one carried out by undercity hands and recorded by undercity clerks.

Prima read her own access logs.

She read every entry. Every storm. Every stair. Every name she had turned away. When she reached the eighth-pillar stairs, two winters ago, and the names Andreas and Ione Kostas and their two daughters, eleven and seven, her voice didn't change. Kallisto, standing beside her, did not look at her and did not move away either.

Then Lachesis's witness was read, by Kallisto, from a sealed leaf the keeper had sent up: *The founding compact of Kroton was measured as tension held between all who built it. It records no rank. It records no sieve.*

The Archon listened to all of it. He did not interrupt. When it was finished, he folded his hands.

“This is a moving account,” he said. “I don't doubt its sincerity. Let me explain why it changes nothing.”

He gestured downward, through the floor, at the turning shaft.

“The Sieve works,” he said. “Look. Every ring stands. Every shelter holds. In forty years no household on the upper terraces has been lost to a storm. That is not doctrine. That is engineering. You can measure it.”

“The undercity built it,” said Kallisto.

“The undercity maintains it,” said the Archon calmly. “Under supervision. And the Sieve arranges the city so that what cannot be broken carries what can. That is also engineering. A structure must rest on its strongest members.” He looked at the residents with something almost like kindness. “The composite are not lesser people. They are differently constituted. They are housed according to their structure. When they are moved up, the city weakens. When the span failed, it failed in the composite ring.”

“It failed,” said Maeve, “because nobody braced it.”

“It failed because it carried a load it was not ranked for.” The Archon's voice didn't rise. “The petition is denied. The Sieve proceeds tomorrow. The school order will be reissued by a safer route. The undercity's maintenance will be commended in the record.” He turned. “Captain. You have a Meridian order to present?”

Brask stepped forward. She did not unseal the paper.

“I have, Archon. A warrant for the person called Nil. I'm required to present it to the Synod before execution.” She held it out. “I'll note that the person is currently a witness in a petition before your court.”

The Archon took the warrant and laid it on his slate without opening it.

“Then the Synod receives it,” he said. “After the court is closed.”

Nil stepped forward.

“The council asks for a review,” they said. “Under the old rule. If the court denies a petition from the undercity, the undercity can ask for a review by Audit. A bounded one. On the Archon's control of the Sieve's access.” They held up a leaf: the rule, copied by Prima from the temple's own book the night before. “Kallisto gave me leave to ask on the council's behalf. I'm not asking for myself.”

The Archon looked at the leaf. Then at Prima.

“You copied this.”

“From your own shelf, Archon.” Her voice was steady. “You taught me where to find it.”

For a moment something passed over the old man's face that was not doctrine at all. Then it was gone.

“Very well,” he said. “A review. Under the old rule. Bounded to the court's access stake.” He rose. “But you should know what you're asking for. The Sieve doesn't only sort the city.”

***

Three wheels rose out of the dais floor in front of Nil, polished white marble with mosaic numerals on their faces. Behind them, Prima stood with her shield. Vesper and Jinling took the flanks.

The Archon raised one hand, and the wheels changed.

Nil watched it happen. Tiles slid out of the marble faces, *click, click, click*, and fell away into the floor below. The 4 went. The 6. The 8. The 9. Every face that could be divided by something smaller dropped out of the wheels, and what was left spun on: 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7.

“The Sieve,” said the Archon, “removes what can be broken. You'll find your options narrower.”

He wasn't lying. Nil stopped a 3 and a 5 and a 7 on the first round, an honest Bust, and his answering strike came down like a dropped slab. Nil caught it on a 0 and felt the parry jar every bone in their arm. On the second round they tried for a Pair, stopped a 5, watched the next wheel come round and saw how few faces there were to choose from, and missed, and stopped a 2.

“Fewer faces makes it easier,” said Vesper through her teeth, at their shoulder. “Doesn't it? Less to aim at.”

“It makes it easier to aim,” said Jinling. “It makes the patterns harder. No four, no six, no eight. No runs past three. No sums to ten without a seven.”

“Seven, three, zero,” said Nil.

“If you can get all three.”

They got a seven and a three. The third wheel came round with its gaps and its six faces, and Nil stopped the zero, and the Zero-Sum closed the Archon's next action before it began. For a moment he looked surprised.

Then he sieved again. The 0 dropped out. Then the 1.

“What's left?” Hina called from the edge of the dais.

“Two, three, five, seven,” said Shunya, very quietly. “He's taken everything but the primes.”

The Archon's next strike came. Nil had no zero to parry with. They stopped a 7, Fortune, their own lucky number, and the strike went back along its path and staggered him. But he steadied, and sieved nothing more, because there was nothing more to sieve.

“Primes,” he said, breathing a little hard. “You see? When everything else is taken away, what remains is what cannot be broken. That's what holds.”

Nil looked at the four faces left on the wheels. Two, three, five, seven. You could make a Jackpot with them. You could make a Pair. No Cascade past three, no Zero-Sum except seven-three-and-nothing.

Not enough. Not against his stake.

Then they thought of the cave. Of Atropos holding up a thread marked forty-two, and cutting it.

*When a number won't fit where you need it, you can break it into what made it.*

The next round, Nil stopped a 5 on the first wheel. Vesper stopped a 7 on the second. And Nil, on the third, stopped a 5 again, and before the scoring frame could close they did what Atropos had shown them: they took the strike as a whole, not the digit. Their base and the 5 together made thirty-five. Thirty-five could be broken.

Five times seven.

They broke it.

The third scoring slot, which had held a single 5, split under their hand into two: a 5 and a 7. The strike's value didn't change. The pattern did. Five, seven, five, seven: four places where there had been three, and two pairs where there had been one. The frame found a pattern it hadn't been able to make a moment before.

The Archon's stake dropped like a stone.

He stared at the wheels. At the 5 and the 7 that had come out of a 35.

“You broke a composite,” he said, very slowly, “into primes.”

“It was made of them,” said Nil. “That's all it ever was.”

***

The rest went quickly. Factor was a single-use trick each round, and Nil spent it carefully, and Vesper and Jinling and Prima did the rest with the faces they had. The Archon's strikes kept coming, and Prima took two of them on her shield and stood. Each round the old man's stake fell further, and each round he sieved the wheels again out of habit, though there was nothing left to sieve.

On the last round, Prima stopped the third wheel herself.

She stopped a 2. The only even prime. It closed a Pair with Nil's 2 on the first wheel, and the Archon's court stake reached zero, and the marble wheels stopped turning and sank back into the floor.

It should have been over.

Nil heard it before anyone else: a change in the sound of the shaft under the dais. The steady grind of the drive faltered. Something under the floor went *clunk*, heavily, like a dropped anchor. Then a long screech of iron on iron.

“The court stake was holding the drive's access,” Maeve shouted. “When it closed, it let go of the—”

The dais tilted.

Not much. A few degrees. The marble floor tipped toward the eastern edge, where the Sieve dropped away in rings below, and everything that wasn't fixed began to slide: slates, papers, a jug of water, the Archon's chair.

The Archon himself had been standing at the eastern edge, where he had stood for the whole Audit, between his chair and the drop.

He took one step back to steady himself. His foot found the edge of the dais and the edge was not where it had been. Nil saw his face change from surprise to understanding in less than a heartbeat.

Then he was gone over the edge.
