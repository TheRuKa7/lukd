## Chapter 35: Catch the Falling

Prima was already moving.

Nil saw it in pieces afterward. Prima's shield coming off her back. Prima sliding down the tilted dais on one hip, not running, because you couldn't run on that slope. Prima's arm going over the edge after him, the shield's strap wrapped twice round her wrist, the shield itself unfolding as it went: once, twice, three times.

Then a jolt that dragged her to the very lip of the dais and stopped her there, flat on her stomach, one arm over the edge and the shield's strap cutting white into her wrist.

“I have him,” she said. Her voice was perfectly flat. “I have him. I can't lift him.”

Nil reached her first, because they were closest, and threw themself down beside her and looked over.

The Archon hung below the edge of the dais by one hand clamped on the rim of Prima's unfolded shield. Below him the Sieve dropped away ring under ring, the holes in the terraces looking up at him like eyes. His other hand was scrabbling at nothing. His face had gone grey. Over his head, quite steady, his Civic still read 17.

His court stake was empty. His number wasn't. Nil had time to think, absurdly, *Varro. Same thing. The stake isn't the man.*

“I can't—” said the Archon. His fingers were slipping on the bronze.

“Hold on,” said Prima. “That's an instruction. Hold on.”

The dais groaned. The drive beneath it was still grinding, still wrong. Every few seconds the tilt shifted a little, and Prima and Nil slid a finger's width toward the edge.

“Anchor!” Maeve was shouting somewhere behind. “Someone get a line to the chair base. It's bolted. Bai Si!”

Bai Si was there. He ran a cord from his lantern harness round the bolted base of the Archon's chair and threw the end to Nil, and Nil looped it round Prima's waist and their own, and the sliding stopped.

But the Archon was still hanging, and the shield's strap was still cutting into Prima's wrist, and nobody could reach far enough over the edge to take his weight off her.

“The equation,” Nil said. “Shunya. Like the span.”

Shunya was already on her knees with the case open. Her hands were shaking.

“Named stakes. Named target.” She swallowed. “The target's the shield. Same as before. It's Prima's channel.”

“Then name them.”

They named them. Kallisto, first, without a pause: *Kallisto of the ninth pillar, ten units, to the shield.* Then Demetrios. Then two market women. Then Jinling, Vesper, Hina, Maeve. Then the three undercity riggers who had come up behind Kallisto. Then, after a long moment in which nobody spoke, a guard in white, Lysander, the sergeant from the span, who stepped forward and said, *Lysander of the Synod guard, ten units*, and didn't look at anyone.

And then a voice Nil didn't expect.

“Brask,” said the captain. “Meridian Collections. Ten units. To the shield.”

She was standing at the edge of the tilted dais with her linked officers behind her and the Archon's unopened warrant still lying on his fallen slate at her feet. She didn't look at it.

Nil put their wrist in the cradle and felt the hands arrive: rough, careful, nervous, wet as rain on paper, procedural, fifteen of them, each one named, each one counted through the crack in the brass. The blue light on the shield's inner face came up strong and steady.

Prima's arm stopped shaking.

“Now,” said Kallisto. “Everyone on the line. Pull.”

They pulled. Bai Si and Maeve and the riggers on Bai Si's cord, Vesper and Hina on Nil's belt, Prima's shield arm locked straight. Slowly, a hand's width at a time, the Archon came up over the edge of the dais and lay on the tilted marble on his back, gasping, staring at the sky through the holes in his own city.

Nil felt the fifteen claims close and the units go home, one by one. All of them.

***

Kallisto was the first to stand over him.

“Archon,” she said. “You're in the custody of the witnesses of this court. All of them. Not the Synod. Not me. The witnesses. Until a hearing decides.”

He looked up at her. He looked older than he had an hour ago, and smaller.

“The Sieve—” he began.

“The Sieve's drive is broken,” said Maeve. She was lying on her stomach at the dais's edge, looking down through the floor openings at the shaft. “Not destroyed. Broken. Your court stake was what kept the drive's access exclusive. When it closed, the lock let go, and the drive slipped. Kallisto's people can fix it.” She sat up. “*Theirs*. Not yours. And when they fix it, it won't sort anyone.”

The Archon closed his eyes.

His number still read 17. Nobody had touched it. Nobody could.

“You caught me,” he said to Prima, without opening his eyes.

“Yes.”

“After what I— the order. The span.”

“Yes.” Prima was sitting on the marble cradling her wrist, which had gone purple where the strap had cut. “I caught you because you were falling. That's all. It doesn't mean anything else.” She looked at him. “You taught me to count people down the shelter stairs. Every one. I'm still doing that. I've just started counting the ones you told me to leave out.”

***

The Seal came out of the drive housing an hour later, when Maeve's riggers had shored the dais level and opened the access hatch. It was small and round like the others, and engraved, and it slid out into Kallisto's palm with a soft click: **SIEVE CONTROL · RELEASED · 2.**

Kallisto held it for a moment. Then she held it out to Nil.

“Take it,” she said. “It's a key to a door in Meridian. It's not a key to anything here. The routes stay with us.”

“The routes stay with you,” Nil agreed, and wrapped it, and put it in the oilcloth beside the others: one, nine, eight, zero, two.

Lachesis's witness came up from the cave that evening, carried by the young clerk with ink on her chin. It was the coiled length of pale thread the keeper had measured between Nil and Prima, sealed in a reed tube, and with it a leaf in Lachesis's sharp hand: *Released to Prima of Kroton, with Nil's agreement, for a use they decide together. Record of tension held without breaking.*

Prima took it and held it for a long time.

“What's it for?” she asked.

“I don't know yet,” said Nil. “You keep it. You'll know when.”

***

Maeve asked Nil what they needed at the forge in the ninth pillar, with the first gear cooling in the corner where the riggers had brought it up.

She didn't start with the gear. She sat on an upturned crate across from Nil with her hands on her knees, empty, and said: “I want to make you something. From a piece of that gear. A tooth, maybe. I could make you a lot of things. A brace for the decoder. A guard for your wrist. A charm.” She stopped. “I'm not going to. Not unless you tell me what you want. I did that to Hina's umbrella. I'm not doing it to you.”

Nil thought about it for a long time.

They thought about the horseshoe Gus had given them in the Bazaar, repaired across one end without hiding the join. About the washer in their pocket. About Maeve's grandmother's mark, three spirals, on a gear older than every office in the country.

“A horseshoe,” they said. “Like Gus's. But from your family's gear. Not to make me luckier. Just so that something your family made is with me when we get to the gate.” They hesitated. “And so that whatever happens there, someone can see where it came from.”

Maeve's face did something complicated.

“A horseshoe,” she said. “From the first gear. With the join showing.”

“With the join showing.”

She forged it that night. Nil sat on the crate and watched. It was small, black iron with a single bright line of brass across the bend where she'd welded the cut tooth, and on the inside, very small, three linked spirals.

***

Vesper told them everything on the road out of Kroton, in the back of a paid westbound cart, with the sea going grey behind them.

She told it in order. The table at the Crossroads, the House's mark on the contract. A child's allocation staked by a family that had nothing else to stake. Mara Vale, nine years old, at the next table with her mother. The rigged deck. Vesper's cut of the winnings. The emergency donations. The injury. The school Mara missed. The home her family lost. Seven years.

Then she told them the rest: what she still owed, and to whom. The House's clauses. The people at the Crossroads who knew her face. And Mara, sixteen now, who worked in a mutual-aid office on the main road and had written, once, a single letter through the case correspondence: *You can make restitution. You can't make me meet you.*

“So that's what's out there,” Vesper said when she'd finished. “The House, which is paying to keep these machines running. And people who'd have reason to see me hanged. And a girl I nearly killed, who has every right never to see me again.” She turned the empty cartridge case in her fingers. “Three rounds left. In case anyone's counting.”

Nobody said anything for a while. The cart rattled.

“We're still coming,” said Hina.

“You heard it all?”

“Hina heard it all.” Hina opened her umbrella against a wind that wasn't there. “We're still coming. You don't decide what Mara owes you. And you don't decide what we owe you either.”

Vesper laughed, short and shaky, and looked away at the sea.

***

In Meridian, Ivo brought Captain Brask's Kroton report up with the morning post.

It was short. *Warrant presented to Synod as required. Archon fell from the access court during a bounded review and was caught alive by the party and residents, including this officer, ten units staked. Archon in witness custody. Synod receiving authority suspended pending hearing. Warrant cannot be executed in Kroton; awaiting instruction.* At the bottom, as always, she had added a list nobody had asked for: the forty-one children of the undercity school, by name.

Thale read it at the window. When he reached *including this officer, ten units staked*, he read it again.

“She staked her own allocation,” he said. “On a prisoner's rescue. In front of witnesses.”

“Yes, sir.”

“She'll be asked why at her review.” Thale set the report down square to the edge of the desk. “I imagine she'll tell them.”

He was quiet for a long time. Ivo waited.

“Where are they going?”

“West, sir. The Crossroads. Paid passage.”

“The House.” Thale's face didn't change. “Send a letter to Octavian Nine. Tell him what's coming, and how fast. Tell him the warrant follows by a different hand.” He paused. “Not Brask's.”

Ivo wrote it down. At his own desk, afterwards, he wrote it down again, word for word, with the date and the hour and both their names. Then he took out the copy of the Kroton order: the Sieve of minors, brought forward, the order he had drafted himself. He read it slowly, all the way through.

At the bottom, under his own name as drafter, he added one more line in his own hand: *Forty-one children were on the span this order specified. Twelve were carried across a shield. None were lost.*

He put it in the drawer. The drawer didn't close properly any more. He left it open.
