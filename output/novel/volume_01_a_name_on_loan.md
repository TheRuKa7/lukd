# ZERO-SUM: OMEN

# The Remainder

## Volume One: A Name on Loan

## Chapter 1: Three Bowls

The job came with a bed.

NIL read that part twice. Beneath it, in smaller letters, the notice promised wages after a trial week, supper during the winter shift and instruction in canal-gate repair. Applicants were to report before the ninth bell. No experience required.

The paper had been pinned to the registration office with a loose screw. The foreman at the canal yard had sent NIL here yesterday: bring an account record before the bell, and they could begin the trial.

NIL tightened the screw with their thumbnail. Then they went to the window.

“Employment record?” the clerk asked.

“I want to start one.”

“Account number?”

NIL put down a form. Five boxes were empty. In the sixth, beneath ACCOUNT, they had copied the three letters engraved inside their wrist mechanism: NIL.

The clerk drew a line through them.

“That's a field value. It means there's no entry.”

“I know.”

“Then why put it here?”

“It's what I have.”

He looked at the paper rather than the person holding it. “You need a registered sponsor. Family, household or employer.”

“The employer sent me here.”

“Then ask them to sponsor you.”

“They won't until I have an account.”

A delivery tube knocked inside the wall. The clerk caught the cylinder that dropped from it, signed its wrapper and pushed it into a tray. That transaction had taken three seconds. NIL had been waiting since dawn.

“Is the position still open?” they asked.

“That's a question for the employer.”

The ninth bell began before NIL reached the stairs.

They stood beneath the notice until the last stroke faded. There were fourteen steps back to the square. The third was chipped. Someone had tied a red thread around the railing beside it, a warning too small to have come from the office.

NIL descended carefully. They could repair a gate if someone taught them. They had already mended the clasp on their coat, wound a broken spring and coaxed three useful nails out of a discarded hinge. None of those things had asked for an account first.

At the bottom they folded the form around the brass washer in their pocket to keep the paper from blowing away. Their right wrist caught on the cuff. They eased the cloth over the cracked ring of metal fused there and pulled the sleeve down.

Above them, the broken display flickered: NaN.

NIL was a word on a piece of machinery, not yet a name they could offer another person. But it was easier to think with than an empty space.

The bed would go to someone else. Breakfast, if they found any, was still a possibility.

Down the lane a baker stepped beneath a leaking pipe, caught her tray as it tilted, and saved all but one bun.

The bun hit NIL's boot.

NIL looked at the bun. The bun, having made its choice, did not move.

“Don't eat that,” the woman said.

“I wasn't going to.”

“Good. It costs four fen.”

NIL looked up.

The woman had a flour-streaked apron, an expression worn smooth by the hour, and a pale Civic number of 28 above her head. Rain passed through the hovering figures without disturbing them. She retrieved the bun, examined the patch of mud on its underside, and placed it on a separate plate behind her stall.

“I thought you said not to eat it.”

“I said *you* shouldn't. I know where your boot's been.”

NIL considered that. “So do I.”

“Then we're both educated.”

She moved on to the remaining buns. NIL stayed where they were, half beneath the awning, counting the drops that escaped the edge of it. Nine fell before the woman noticed them again.

“Are you waiting for something?”

“The price of the muddy one.”

She glanced at the plate. “One fen.”

NIL reached into their coat and placed a small brass washer on the counter. They had polished it against their sleeve until its edges caught the light. It was round, useful, and, as far as they could tell, the only object in the pocket that might plausibly become breakfast.

The woman picked it up.

“That's a washer.”

“Yes.”

“I'm a baker.”

“I thought you might need one.”

She put it back with the care of a person returning a disappointing gift. “I do. What I need more is money.”

NIL put the washer away. The rejected form crackled around it.

“Do you know anyone who needs a repair apprentice?” they asked.

“Gus, at the Bazaar. He needs three and can afford half of one.”

“Would he sponsor an account?”

The baker paused over the tray. “You would have to ask him.”

NIL had heard that answer before. This time it came with a direction.

On the other side of the street, a clockwork board clicked through the morning's tally. A clerk in a rubber cape checked figures against a paper roll while a porter held an umbrella over both of them. The porter had a Civic 04. The clerk had 63. When the umbrella turned inside out, the porter repaired it with a bit of wire while the clerk continued to complain about the weather.

NIL watched the porter's hands until the umbrella was useful again.

“Work,” they said.

“What?”

“I could work for it.”

The baker looked past them, toward the people moving under the pipes. The breakfast crowd was thinning. A stack of dirty bowls occupied the end of her counter; more stood in a tub beneath it. She measured the sky, the stack, and NIL.

“Three bowls washed. One bun.”

NIL looked at the stack. There were seventeen bowls on the counter and at least ten in the tub. The number three had been offered with suspicious confidence.

“Any three?”

“Any three that aren't already clean.”

“That seems fair.”

“You're new here.”

She pointed them toward a basin under the awning. The water was cold. NIL removed their gloves, set them beside the basin and rolled up their sleeves. The right cuff caught briefly on the decoder. They eased it free before the baker could look closely.

The first bowl had dried porridge around its rim. The second had an old crack, neatly sealed with a line of dark resin. NIL turned it to find where the repair began and ended, then washed it twice.

“That one doesn't get cleaner if you threaten it.”

The speaker was a boy on the other side of the counter. His yellow hood was up, and three bottle caps hung from a cord at his waist. He had the solemn face of someone delivering information of national importance.

“I'm not threatening it,” NIL said.

“You're holding it like you might.”

NIL loosened their grip.

The boy nodded. “There. It knows.”

He was small enough that he had to stand on the bottom rail of the stall to see into the basin. His eyes followed NIL's hands. NIL waited for him to ask about the decoder, but he pointed to the clean bowls stacked behind the baker instead.

“You should start with those.”

NIL looked at them. “They're clean.”

“Exactly.”

For a moment NIL tried to work out whether the offer covered a bowl that had already been washed by someone else. The boy held his serious expression for two breaths. Then his mouth betrayed him.

NIL almost smiled.

“Tobi.” A woman's voice came from the lane. “If you find someone to do your work for you, at least ask their name.”

The boy turned. “I was helping.”

“I heard.”

His mother came around the corner carrying a coat folded in oilcloth. A length of thread clung to her sleeve. She took it between finger and thumb and wound it around a small card already crowded with other saved pieces. Her headwrap was tied firmly against the rain; one edge of her shawl had been patched so neatly that NIL noticed the repair only because the cloth beneath it was older.

The Civic above her read 01.

Tobi jumped down and took the parcel from her. It was almost too wide for his arms.

“I can carry it,” he said.

“I can see that.”

“All the way.”

“Then I won't have to.”

He adjusted his grip, pleased by the concession. She pressed one palm to the parcel's top to steady it while he found his balance. NIL noticed that she didn't let go until he had both feet clear of a puddle.

“You have a visitor,” she told the baker.

“I have an employee. They drive a hard bargain.”

“Two bowls,” NIL said.

“See?”

The woman turned to NIL. “I'm Ama. Thank you for keeping him occupied.”

“He was helping.”

“I heard that too.”

She smiled, and for an instant NIL had the odd feeling of arriving late to a conversation in which everyone else knew the rules. Ama's gaze lifted, as people's gazes usually did, to the place above NIL's head.

The broken display flickered.

NaN.

Her smile did not vanish. It changed only enough for NIL to see that she had read it.

“I'm sorry,” Ama said. “What should I call you?”

NIL opened their mouth.

There were answers for questions like that. They had heard people give them at counters and doors: a word, sometimes two, and then the other person knew where to put them. NIL had practiced the shape of an answer while walking. None of the sounds had stayed.

“I haven't decided,” they said.

Tobi looked interested. “Can you decide twice?”

“Tobi,” Ama said.

“I changed my mind about being a chimney inspector.”

“After one chimney.”

“It was an important chimney.”

The baker set a clean cloth beside NIL's basin. “My name's Baker when people want bread and ‘you there’ when they don't. Either one's worked so far.”

NIL's shoulders eased. Not much. Enough to notice.

Ama touched Tobi's hood and drew it a little farther over his hair. “We have to deliver the coat before the rain finds the seam again.”

“It's a good seam,” he said.

“It is. The rain is persistent.”

They started down the lane together, Tobi in front, Ama close enough to catch the parcel if he stumbled. At the narrow market arch, a man in a peaked cap stepped aside for them. For a moment NIL thought he was letting them pass.

Then he set a brass case on the rail.

Its clasp opened with a practiced click. Glass jars sat inside fitted rings; the nearest held six small lights, each one separate from the others. The man unfolded a document, and Ama stopped.

Tobi nearly walked into her. She caught the parcel against his chest before it slipped.

NIL lifted the third bowl from the basin. Water ran down its side and across their palm.

The baker followed their gaze. The flour on her hand left a white mark on the counter where she gripped it.

“Finish washing,” she said quietly.

At the arch, Ama took a folded receipt from her shawl and held it out. The man compared it with his document. He looked at the stamp, then at the receipt again.

He did not give it back.

The six lights in the jar remained still. Above Ama's head, the 01 trembled in the rain.

NIL set the bowl down without drying it.

They wiped their hands on the cloth and pulled the gloves back on. The right one was still wet inside.

“Your bun,” the baker said, reaching for the counter. “You've nearly earned it.”

At the arch, the Collector slid Ama's receipt toward a glass plate. Tobi gripped the coat with both hands. The six lights in the jar began to turn.

NIL looked once toward the Bazaar road. A sponsor, an apprenticeship, a bed: all of it required becoming the sort of person an office could approve.

“Keep it for a moment,” they said.

They crossed the lane and stepped between the child and the open brass case.

“Before you take anything else,” NIL said, “whose six are those?”

## Chapter 2: The Jar Owes Six

The Collector looked at the person who had stepped between his case and the child.

“Stand clear of the equipment.”

“Whose six?” NIL asked again.

He moved the case farther along the rail without answering. Ama reached past NIL toward the receipt he was using to shelter his own paper from the rain.

NIL noticed because Ama noticed. Her hand remained outstretched after he took it, the fingers slightly apart, as though the receipt might remember the way home.

“That copy is mine,” she said.

“You may collect it after the account is settled.”

“The account was settled on Tuesday.”

“The record says it wasn't.”

“That's why I brought the receipt.”

The Collector examined it again. He was a narrow man with rain shining on the brim of his cap. His coat was cleaner at the shoulders than at the cuffs, where the leather had darkened from handling cases like the one on the rail. He had a small brass stamp clipped to his belt, and each time he looked from one paper to the other, his thumb found its handle.

“The authorization number differs,” he said.

“By one mark.”

“A mark is how we distinguish one authorization from another.”

“That mark is yours.” Ama pointed to the ink. “Your office changed the form. The clerk wrote the new mark above the old one and told me to keep the receipt.”

“Then you'll need a corrected receipt.”

“I need this one back to ask for it.”

He considered that. NIL saw the moment he understood the problem: his eyes moved to the stamp, then to the rain gathering along the lower edge of Ama's paper.

“You may request a duplicate.”

“At which office?”

“The office of issue.”

“The office of issue moved last month.”

“Then its successor.”

Tobi shifted beneath the coat parcel. “Do offices get children?”

Ama would normally have answered him. NIL knew that already, though they had known her only a few minutes. This time she kept her eyes on the Collector's hands.

“How long do I have?” she asked.

“Until this notice is executed.”

He laid his document beside the case. A narrow brass channel ran from the case to a portable terminal clamped around the canal rail. NIL had mistaken the terminal for part of the railing: rain had the same dark sheen on both.

Above Ama, the 01 quivered. NIL watched it and remembered the porter's 04, the baker's 28, the clerk's 63. Numbers were different from skill. Everyone they had met that morning had demonstrated that. But a woman who counted every saved piece of thread had asked how long she had, and the Collector had not given her an hour.

“What happens at zero?” Tobi asked.

The Collector folded the edge of his document away from a drip. “The office would open a review.”

“After you take it?” Ama said.

“A zero account receives priority.”

“A zero account can't travel to your office without an escort. The ferry won't take it alone. The clinic puts it behind the screen until someone guarantees the fee.” Her voice stayed level, but she had tucked the hand that held her shawl under her arm, and the cloth pulled tight against her shoulder. “Who pays for the escort while my review is open?”

“That depends on the review.”

The baker had come to the edge of her awning. She looked from NIL to the case and shook her head once. From here the Bazaar road was an open lane, one NIL could still take.

They stayed beside Ama.

“What are those for?” they asked, pointing at the jar.

Six lights glowed in the nearest jar. They were not flames. Each shifted when NIL moved, like a reflection trying to decide which surface belonged to it.

“Confiscated allocation,” the Collector said. “Stand back.”

“From her?”

“From accounts under review.”

Ama glanced at the jar. “My last six were taken when the notice was filed. I paid the fee on Tuesday. The clerk said they would be returned when the correction was entered.”

“The return is pending verification,” the Collector said.

“And you're taking the one I have left before verifying the six.”

“The actions are separate.”

NIL looked at the two pieces of paper. The old mark on Ama's receipt had been crossed through; the new mark was written above it in the same blue ink as the date. The Collector's document carried only the old mark. Neither paper explained why the new one had not reached him.

“They're connected,” NIL said.

“The records say otherwise.”

“Then the records disagree with each other.”

The Collector's jaw tightened. “This is an official account.”

“Then it should add up.”

For one hopeful second, he stopped. NIL thought he might put both papers back inside his coat and return in the afternoon with an answer. Instead he slid Ama's receipt beneath the terminal's clear cover.

“The discrepancy will be attached to the review.”

He turned the key.

A small wheel began to rotate behind the glass. A needle moved toward a red mark. Ama reached for the receipt, but the cover had locked over it.

Tobi made a sound NIL had not heard from him before. It was not loud. He put the parcel down very carefully, as though setting it on the wet stones could keep everything else from falling.

“Wait,” Ama said.

“You may appeal the result.”

The needle moved another notch. NIL stepped to the terminal and put their right hand on its lever.

“Don't,” the Collector said.

NIL pulled. The lever held fast. They pulled harder, bracing one boot against the rail. Brass scraped through the wet glove and across their palm. Heat flared beneath the broken circle in their wrist, sharp enough to make them gasp.

They let go.

The Collector caught their elbow. NIL twisted away, stumbled, and saw the narrow service steps descending beside the bridge. An open route. Six steps to the water, perhaps seven. The Collector was already reaching for the stamp on his belt; if NIL ran now, he might follow the noise instead of calling for help.

Ama drew Tobi behind her. He had abandoned the parcel to take her hand.

NIL looked at the steps, then back at the jar.

The six lights were moving now, slowly circling a brass contact in the lid. A line ran from that contact through the case and into the terminal. Under the glass cover, Ama's receipt lay against another metal plate. The mechanism had a route for taking. Its return channel had an empty fitting where a connection should have been.

NIL's wrist throbbed. The broken circle had a gap too.

They did not know whether the two gaps belonged together. They knew only that the needle was almost at the red mark.

“Move away from the equipment,” the Collector said.

NIL stayed.

They pressed the injured wrist against the empty fitting. Brass met brass. For a breath nothing happened except pain. Then the orange light in the decoder caught on the edge of the fitting, and NIL felt a strange succession of pressures: a hand supporting a plank, another passing a cup, another closing around a length of thread. No faces. No words. The impressions were gone before NIL could decide whether they were memories.

The wheel stuttered.

“What did you do?” the Collector demanded.

“I don't know yet.”

The jar's contact had stopped circling. NIL could feel a path between it and the plate beneath Ama's receipt, narrow as the gap in the decoder. They held their wrist steady and turned the lever back one notch.

One light left the jar. Ama's 01 became 02.

The Collector grabbed for the lever. NIL held it with their uninjured hand, breath caught high in their chest. The light in the jar moved again: two, three, four. Each departure had an arrival. The number above Ama climbed with them.

“You—” Tobi began, then stopped. He had no name to call.

The fifth light crossed. The sixth followed it.

The jar was empty. Ama's Civic read 07.

The terminal gave a dry click and released its cover. NIL pulled their wrist free. The decoder went dark; the NaN above them remained as broken as before. Their palm had left a red smear on the lever.

For a moment, no one moved.

Then Ama took her receipt and pulled Tobi against her. His arms were pinned awkwardly between them. He did not complain. After a few seconds he managed to free one hand and held the coat parcel out to her, because that was still what they had been doing before the man opened his case.

Ama laughed once, a sound that was very nearly a sob. She took the parcel and looked at NIL.

“Thank you.”

The baker had crossed the lane with the bun. She put it in Tobi's free hand; he pressed it into NIL's left palm before they could object.

“You finished,” he said.

“I didn't.”

“The bowl was nearly clean.”

NIL closed their fingers around the bun. It was still faintly warm beneath the damp paper.

The Collector was staring above their head.

They were clear of the baker's awning. There was nothing between him and the broken display now.

“What account is that?” he asked.

NIL's first instinct was to cover it, though their hand would not reach that high. Their second was to apologize for the blood on his lever.

The Collector pulled a whistle from inside his coat and blew one hard note.

Across the canal, metal answered metal. A gate lifted. Something heavy stepped through it, one articulated foot at a time.

The baker pushed Ama toward the bridge. “Laundry stairs. Go.”

Tobi held on to his mother. Ama hesitated, looking from NIL's injured hand to the machine emerging under the opposite arch.

“Go,” NIL said.

She went. Tobi looked back once; NIL raised the hand holding the bun, because it was the only one that did not hurt.

The machine lowered its brass muzzle. Four stamp-shaped feet found the wet stones. A glass reserve vessel rattled against its flank as it turned toward the movement on the bridge.

NIL stepped into the open, away from Ama and Tobi.

“Here,” they called.

The Debt Hound's head turned. Its first foot came down between NIL and the service steps, leaving a square impression in the mud.

NIL counted the remaining exits.

## Chapter 3: A Door That Opens

There were two exits if NIL counted the canal. The water was brown, moving quickly, and occupied by a barge whose pilot was shouting at the rain. NIL decided it counted only in a generous mood.

The other exit was the service stair. The Debt Hound stood between NIL and its first step.

Its muzzle opened along a seam. A paper strip unrolled from inside and struck the ground, already wet at the edges. The machine examined it with a single glass lens, then stamped one brass foot onto the strip.

“Account holder,” the Collector called from behind NIL. “Remain where you are.”

NIL looked up at their own NaN. “That's the problem.”

They ducked as the Hound lunged. Its jaws closed around the railing where their shoulder had been. The rail bent inward. NIL slipped past its left flank and reached the service stair just as the machine tore free, taking a length of iron with it.

Seven steps. NIL had counted correctly.

At the bottom, a narrow path ran beneath the bridge. Water dripped through the stone joints in regular intervals. NIL could hear Ama and Tobi's footsteps overhead, climbing away toward the laundry stairs. Then the Hound struck the top step and the sound of metal swallowed everything else.

NIL ran.

Their right palm pulsed with each swing of the arm. The bun was in the left hand; they put it inside their coat before the paper came apart. At the far end of the bridge, the path divided around a shuttered washhouse. NIL chose the lane away from the family and found three lines of laundry strung across it, low enough to catch a tall person at the throat.

They ducked the first line and pushed through a sheet on the second. Something tugged hard at the longer left tail of their coat.

NIL stopped. A wooden peg had closed over the fabric with the determination of a tiny jaw.

The Hound struck the bridge behind them.

NIL looked at the peg. “Really?”

It offered no explanation. They pried it open, left it clipped to the line and ran on. Behind them, the Hound went through the laundry without ducking. A sheet wrapped itself around the machine's muzzle. It continued forward, now looking like a very angry parcel.

NIL gained perhaps four seconds.

The lane ended at a gate. Beyond it lay a courtyard, an open door and a woman carrying a bucket. NIL waved; she reached for the latch. Before she could touch it, a brass pin shot from the gatepost and locked the mechanism with a heavy clack.

The woman recoiled. The bucket tipped, water spreading over the stones.

NIL put their wrist against the latch. The decoder flickered, but there was no empty fitting, no jar connected to a receipt, no return path they could feel. They pressed harder. Pain answered. The gate remained shut.

The Hound came around the washhouse with the sheet trailing from its head. Its lens had found a hole in the cloth.

“Other way!” the woman shouted.

There wasn't one wide enough for NIL to see. A cart piled with wet linen blocked a recess beside the gate. Behind it stood a door, its wood repaired with mismatched boards.

“The cart,” NIL said.

The woman followed their gaze. She set down the empty bucket and shoved from her side of the gate, pushing one end of the cart through a gap in the bars. NIL caught the other end. Together they turned it across the lane.

“That's my washing,” she said.

“I'm sorry.”

“Be sorry somewhere else.”

The Hound struck the cart. Wheels skidded; linen rose around its legs. NIL squeezed through the space behind it and put their hand against the repaired door. An open hand had been carved into the upper board, its lines worn smooth by weather.

The latch lifted.

Warm air touched NIL's face. They stepped inside and pulled the door to as the cart crashed against it. A lamp moved across the room. Someone shielded its flame with one hand, then set it behind a stack of ledgers away from the window.

“Don't lock it,” a man's voice said.

NIL had already reached for the bolt. They stopped.

“Why?”

“It squeaks. The latch is quieter.”

Outside, the Hound's feet struck the stones. A wheel turned. Something scraped along the door, paused at the latch, and moved on toward the gate. The woman in the courtyard began complaining loudly about her washing. The complaint followed the machine down the lane, growing more inventive as it went.

NIL kept one hand on the latch until the footsteps faded.

The man returned the lamp to a table. He was older than the baker, with ink on two fingers and a collar that had been mended at least twice. One side of the room held shelves supported by stacked ledgers. On the other, a pan caught drops from a leak in the ceiling. Beside the table stood a brass terminal with a small ribbon tied to one of its handles.

The man looked at NIL's face, then at their right hand.

“There's water in the jug,” he said. “The cloth is clean. The chair is more reliable than it looks.”

NIL looked at the chair. One leg had been wrapped in cord.

“How reliable?”

“It hasn't dropped anyone this week.”

“It's only morning.”

“Then you're right to investigate.” He pulled the chair out and sat on it himself. It creaked but held. “There. Tested.”

NIL did not sit. The man stood again and left the chair where it was.

“My name is Nought,” he said. “You can rest before you explain anything.”

The offer was so unlike the Collector's questions that NIL looked toward the back of the room, searching for the next part of it. There was a second door, a kettle, a little stack of bowls. No visible price list.

“I can't pay,” they said.

“For the chair?”

“For hiding.”

“Ah.” Nought considered the lamp, the latched front door and the pan under the leak. “I haven't decided what hiding costs. Sit down before I become ambitious.”

NIL sat.

The chair shifted once beneath them, then settled. They removed the wet glove from their right hand. A shallow scrape crossed the palm; the decoder's brass edge was hot but no longer glowing. Nought poured water over the cloth and set it within reach rather than taking the hand himself.

“May I?” he asked.

NIL nodded. He wrapped the cloth around their palm, leaving the fingers free. His own hands were steady, though the cuffs at his wrists had frayed.

“There,” he said. “It will complain for a while.”

“Will it heal?”

“The scrape should. The thing around your wrist is another question.”

NIL looked at the broken circle. “It was already there.”

“I thought it might be.”

He did not ask where they had come from. He poured two cups of water, one for each of them, and sat on the edge of the table. When the roof dripped into the pan, he leaned across without looking and moved it a little to the left. The next drop landed inside.

NIL remembered the bun. It had survived the chase poorly. One side was crushed flat, and the paper had taken the shape of their fist.

“I have this,” they said, and offered it to him.

Nought took the bun, divided it in two and handed back the larger piece.

“You did the difficult part,” he said.

“Which part?”

“Getting it here.”

NIL studied the two pieces. The division was not exact. Nought had clearly done it on purpose.

“You have less.”

“I had breakfast.”

“The baker said the muddy one was one fen.”

“An excellent price for local weather.”

NIL took a bite before they could decide whether the bun was payment, a gift, or evidence. It tasted of salt and bread and a little smoke from the oven. Nought ate his piece without asking them to settle the question.

When he went to rinse the cups, NIL meant to ask about the terminal. Instead their eyes closed. They woke once to a delivery at the front door and again to the pan being emptied. Each time the chair was still beneath them. Nobody had asked them to earn another hour in it.

By the time they could stay awake, the light around the shutter had thinned to the grey of late afternoon. Nought was mending a split form sleeve at the table. He put it aside when NIL sat forward.

Outside, a bell rang twice. Voices followed it, too indistinct to make out through the wall. NIL's shoulders tightened.

“Will they say I stole it?”

“The bun?”

“The six.”

Nought's expression changed. Not alarm exactly. Attention.

“Did you?”

“They were in a jar. They went back to her. There were six, and then there were none. She had one, and then she had seven.” NIL traced the movement with the fingers of their left hand. “The Collector said the actions were separate.”

“I see why he would prefer that.”

“Can an unregistered person owe something?”

“They can try to bill you.” Nought looked toward the window. “Whether the bill is honest is another matter.”

NIL waited. They had expected a sharper answer, or perhaps a request to leave. The pan caught another drop.

“I wanted an account,” they said. “Before today.”

“What for?”

“When someone asks who I am, they wait.” NIL searched for the words and found themselves counting the stitches in the cloth around their hand. “Other people answer. I thought an account would give me somewhere to put the answer.”

Nought did not look at the NaN immediately. When he did, his face held curiosity and concern, but neither seemed to require NIL to become anything else first.

“A number won't make you a person,” he said. “You already are.”

NIL wanted to believe him. That was different from believing him, and they thought he might understand the difference.

“I still want a name.”

“Of course.” Nought nodded toward the terminal and its old ribbon. “There is a Naming station here. It can register a chosen resonance. It also connects to the city records, so I can't promise what it will make of your account.”

The bell sounded again, farther away now.

“You can rest,” he said. “You can leave by the back when the lane is clear. Or, if you want to try the station, we'll try it together. The chair is yours either way.”

NIL looked from the back door to the ribbon. Its red thread had faded where many hands had touched it. Someone had tied the bow carefully and left the ends uneven.

“Will you tell me what to choose?”

“I can tell you what the choices mean.”

“That's not the same.”

“No,” Nought said. “It isn't.”

He waited while NIL folded the empty bun paper. Outside, the lane had quieted and someone was lowering a shop shutter. Inside, the pan caught the rain, one drop at a time.

## Chapter 4: Seven Is Not a Name

Nought put the empty cups in a basin and left the terminal untouched.

That helped. NIL had expected him to wipe his hands, pull a lever and begin before the courage to object could arrive. Instead he examined the front shutter, listened to the street and said, “Would you like the chair closer?”

“Does it need to be?”

“Only if you want to sit.”

NIL carried it over. The cord around its repaired leg creaked.

The terminal was smaller than the Collector's machine. Its brass had gone dull except around the handle, where years of hands had kept a patch bright. A red ribbon hung from a screw. The bow had been tied with more care than symmetry.

“Someone left that?”

“Someone wanted me to keep it.”

“Did their Naming work?”

“Yes.”

NIL waited for the comforting explanation: therefore this one would work too. Nought lifted the ribbon clear of the handle and laid it safely beside the machine.

The Cascade locked in order. The shared stake channel drove Varro back one stair. He braced rather than screamed; the polearm's hook scored the railing. His temporary readout fell, and the next strike remained closed long enough to give Nil another turn.

Then the jar clicked.

The token frame filled with the last resolved digit: 7. Three small segments lit beside it.

“Last position,” Varro said. “Next three rounds.”

Nil stared at the mark, then at the empty space it left in their scoring frame. The machine had waited until the round was complete. It had done exactly what he had announced. Knowing that did not make it less infuriating.

“Same side,” Vesper reminded them.

Nil caught 3. Vesper caught 3. The final face passed under Nil's palm and stopped at 4.

The landing stayed visibly 4. In the scoring frame, the jar's declared substitution made it 0.

Three, three, zero. A Pair. Smaller than the Cascade. One segment went dark.

“That was supposed to be—” Nil began.

“It was supposed to be the face you stopped.” Vesper shifted behind the post. “Don't give him your next decision as well.”

Varro came down two steps. The ratchet released; the hook followed the line of his shoulder.

Nil waited for zero. Their palm hurt enough that they almost pressed early just to end the waiting. They caught it.

The defensive channel closed. The hook struck the post, shedding a splinter that fell between Nil's boots. On the jar, a separate defended-action latch advanced once. The affected-round track still had two lit segments.

Vesper was close enough to speak without raising her voice.

“Two clocks,” she said. “Three defended attacks or three rounds under it. Read both.”

Nil nodded, angry at how much they needed the reminder.

“I blamed you,” they said.

“You started to.”

“I was wrong.”

“Yes.” She checked the polearm, then the jar. “Now help me.”

Nil took zero on the next attack sequence. Vesper took zero too. Nil's third stop landed on their chosen 7, bright beneath the injured hand.

The jar substituted 0. Three zeros locked in the scoring frame.

A Jackpot, but not the chosen-digit pattern Nil had wanted. It still paid the declared result. The temporary stake fell; the second affected-round segment went dark.

Varro caught his coat against the rail as he braced. A little wrapped token slipped free. He reached for it before he reached for the weapon.

Nil saw a name on its corner.

Livia.

Then Varro had it beneath his coat again.

Vesper saw it too. Something hard moved across her expression. She let it pass without turning the name into a taunt.

The ratchet on Varro's polearm shifted to a deeper notch. Its warning plate showed the heavier action preparing, with one old-strength strike still ahead of it. He did not try to hide the change.

Nil watched his grip instead of the mask.

When he moved, they caught 7 on the defence wheel.

The return channel opened once. The old-strength strike went back along its declared path, forcing Varro to take the weight through his own stake. A second defended latch moved on the jar.

Not three. It stayed shut.

Nil drew a breath that scraped in their throat.

“One affected round,” Vesper said.

Varro was breathing hard too. The heavy detent was ready. Nil could hear its spring loading; the sound was almost like the brace beneath their own hand, only much larger.

Nil stopped 3. Vesper stopped 7. At the last position Nil caught another 7.

The jar's substitution made it 0.

Three, seven, zero. Ten.

The Zero-Sum channel closed Varro's next action before the heavier strike could begin. The loaded spring remained visibly caught. No blow vanished after hitting anyone; the action had been prevented from opening.

The third affected-round segment went dark.

The jar opened.

Its defended latch had moved only twice. It did not matter. The three affected rounds were over. The stored 7 slid into a restoration token connected to the last scoring position, waiting for the next actual stop.

Vesper looked at Nil and nodded toward the final wheel. Her revolver remained trained on the stair above Varro. Five cartridges remained: the two shots in the lanes were the only ones she had fired. Inside the stake, it was the declared scoring channel they were working together; shooting the man would not improve its result.

The next sequence was theirs.

Nil stopped 7.

Vesper stopped 7.

The final wheel turned beneath Nil's palm. Their fingers were shaking now. They had counted everything except how long a hand could be asked to hurt politely.

They stopped a 3.

For a heartbeat it remained a 3, an ordinary legal landing.

Then the stored token restored the last scoring position to 7 and cleared itself.

Seven, seven, seven.

The chosen pattern closed through the channel they had all declared. The force of it hit Varro's stake, and the weight he had held against the ratchet came off the stairs. The hook struck stone. His knee followed it.

The temporary readout reached zero.

His Civic still read 61.

He was breathing. Nil could hear it through the mask, harsh and human.

The gate mechanism released the office credential. A small metal Seal marked 1 slid into the receiving tray, attached to a closed review receipt. Maeve retrieved both and compared the print with her counter-copy before handing either to Nil.

“Route claim closed,” she said. Her voice was steadier than her hands.

Vesper lowered the gun. “Gate?”

Maeve checked the latch. “Open once we lift it.”

“Then we should go.”

Nil had taken one step toward Varro without deciding to.

“He's not going to die from that?”

“Not from losing this stake,” Vesper said. “You can see he's alive.”

Varro reached for his hook. Nil stopped. The man rested his hand on the haft but did not lift it.

“You should have left the woman alone,” Nil said.

“Different office.”

“Same case.”

He looked down at the jar.

Beyond the gate, a ferry bell rang. Maeve turned toward it, listening to the number of strokes.

“If we're going,” she said, “it has to be now.”

Nil kept looking at the token beneath Varro's coat. “The woman in the window. Livia.”

His hand tightened on the polearm.

“Don't.”

“Is she your family?”

“My sister.”

Nil remembered her holding the letter as if deciding whether the street could be trusted to receive it. Varro had a high number, a paid office and the power to hold a gate. She was still behind the locked stair.

“You were promised help.”

“I was promised an exemption.” The answer came out before Varro could close it away. “The release is pending.”

Maeve shifted the tool bundle on her shoulder. Vesper watched Nil, neither urging them onward nor pretending the choice cost nothing.

“If he leaves,” she said quietly, “he knows which way we went. If you spend the time here, we miss the direct ferry. I won't lie to you about that.”

Nil crouched, keeping clear of the hook. They could not offer Varro the exemption. They could not promise to unlock Livia's door. Gus's receipt was still beneath their coat, with an address and a mark that meant someone could be asked.

“I don't know how to help her,” Nil said.

Varro's shoulders moved. It might have been a laugh without any pleasure in it.

“Then you've joined a crowded profession.”

Nil offered the left hand.

For a moment Varro only looked at it. Then he took hold and hauled himself upright, more by the railing than by their arm. Nil caught the rail with their right hand to steady them both and hissed as the scrape opened against the iron.

Varro let go at once.

He collected the polearm and checked the token beneath his coat. He did not remove the mask. He did not apologize to everyone he had collected from in a sentence that would make the steps feel clean.

“They'll change the route order,” he said.

“We know,” Vesper answered.

He went up the stairs slowly, using the haft to take weight off his knee.

The ferry's last bell sounded before Maeve and Vesper had the gate fully raised.

For a while none of them spoke.

***

Gus met them at the covered junction with a handcart he refused to describe as borrowed.

“Nought's been questioned,” he said before Nil could ask. “He's in the shrine. For now. Baker's stall is open. The family got up the laundry stairs.”

Nil felt three separate knots in their chest loosen. Not all the knots. Enough to stand straighter.

Gus saw the receipt and Seal. He read the closed claim, then made two careful copies of its toll reference.

“That mark belongs to the Hyakki shrine-road ledger. It's on Maeve's supplier attachment too. If you're looking for why these orders don't agree, that's an actual office to ask.”

“Can we get there?” Nil said.

“Slow ferry tomorrow. Tetsu. Fees are posted, and he won't thank you for arguing that your emergency outweighs a rotten rope.”

“I have enough for my passage,” Maeve said. “The guild paid the completed hinge order before attaching the supplier account. Actual wages.”

Vesper counted coins onto the cart top. “I can cover ours. After that we're earning or asking, not pretending food appears because we need it.”

Gus divided the coins between the posted fares and three bowls from the breakfast cart. Vesper counted the change back before passing Nil a bowl. They had been running on half a bun; the first mouthful made them realize how badly their hands were shaking. They ate sitting against the cart wheel, while Maeve and Gus checked the copies.

Nil looked at the coins. “Seven fen?”

“That was a rescue rate. Apparently we're starting a different business.”

Gus bound the original receipt in oilcloth. One copy went into his dispatch drawer, another into Maeve's folio. Nil kept the protected original and the separate window note.

“You still need an account for a repair trial,” he said. “I haven't forgotten. I also can't honestly sponsor you while every public board is asking where you are.”

“Later?”

“Later we discuss it. That's as much promise as I have.”

Nil nodded. This time they did not try to improve the answer by hearing more than he had said.

Maeve brought them to the sheltered roof above the guild store. The tools had been signed into storage; her aunt had answered the notice. There was nothing left for Maeve to carry away except the bundle, her papers and the question she meant to ask.

She set a small rough charm on Nil's knee. One of her spare washers sat inside a loop of wire, its chipped edge filed smooth.

“Friend luck,” she said.

Nil turned it over. It had no registered frame fitting.

“What does it do?”

“This one? Reminds you I owe you a better pin for the porter.”

“That's not a power.”

“Not everything has to be.”

She gave them a fresh cloth before they could bleed on the washer and went to find a place to unroll her blanket.

Vesper sat beside the roof hatch, wiping rain from the cylinder. She laid five unfired cartridges beside the single empty case from the ladder shot; the first empty case was still in her pocket. Seven chambers, each a physical hole. When the cylinder was dry, she put the five live rounds back. Nil counted them once and stopped.

“You could have left,” they said.

“Several times.”

“Why didn't you?”

Her hand paused. “You keep holding doors for people.”

Nil waited.

“It's an inconvenient habit,” she said.

They looked out over the Wards. Lamps were coming on in windows. Someone had hauled the laundry back under cover. The Basilica's amber light was no longer searching this particular roof, but it remained above the city, patient enough to let people become tired.

“He called this an office credential,” Nil said, touching the Seal through its wrapping. “Can it get us upstairs?”

“Not by itself.” Vesper sketched the Upper Gate from the public access notices: six narrow office sockets and a separate resonance connection. “That's above the plateau, not the drain we just came through. If we ever want to ask the people issuing these orders instead of the people carrying them out, we need access they can't dismiss at the first door.”

“So we take the rest?”

“We find out what we're taking and who needs it to keep the lights on.” She rubbed out a line she had drawn too neatly. “Today was enough blind arithmetic for me.”

Nil took Nought's notebook from inside the coat. The name was dry. On the next page they wrote the ferry fee, the toll reference and Livia's window.

At the bottom they added: **Ask before promising.**

They were asleep before the ink had quite dried.

***

A fortune-teller held an open palm toward them.

Nil could not see the street around the figure. The darkness was full of little moving points, like lamps too distant to warm anything. Where the figure's eyes should have been, two dice turned slowly.

“Pick a number,” it said.

“I did.”

“Yes?”

“Seven.”

The figure laughed softly. Not at the answer, Nil thought, though they could not have explained how they knew.

“And what did you expect to receive?”

Nil tried to answer. A bed. A place on a form. Someone who would use their name after they left the room.

When they woke, the roof was pale with the morning before sunrise. Their right hand hurt. Maeve was asleep with one boot still on. Vesper sat by the hatch, watching the lane below.

“Ferry,” she said. “If you're still coming.”

Nil checked the wrapped receipt, the little Seal and the note for a woman who could not yet leave her window.

“I'm coming.”

They rose carefully and went to help Maeve tie the tool bundle.
