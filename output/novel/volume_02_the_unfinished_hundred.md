# The Remainder

## Volume Two: The Unfinished Hundred

## Chapter 8: The Ferryman's Seam

The ferryman would not take their money until he had finished inspecting the rope.

Vesper stood on the landing with three fares in her hand. Behind them, the Wards were beginning to make the sounds of another working morning: shutters, cart wheels, a distant complaint about a delivery. Nothing in those sounds admitted that Nil had become someone worth hunting.

“We were told you left at this bell,” Vesper said.

The man bent the rope between his hands. A few pale fibres lifted from the bend.

“When the rope's ready.”

“Is it ready?”

He showed her the fibres.

“You're welcome to depart without it.”

Maeve put down her tool bundle. Nil watched her mouth twitch. Vesper closed her hand around the money and waited.

The boat was low in the water, with a little cargo awning and benches worn smooth at the middle. Its name had been repainted often enough that the letters stood higher than the wood around them. Tetsu, according to Gus's note. The man and the boat appeared equally unwilling to be hurried.

He replaced a short section of line, testing the splice with his whole weight before beckoning them forward.

“Receipt.”

Nil offered the closed review in its oilcloth. Tetsu dried his hands before touching it. He read Varro's office number, checked the route release and held the paper beside a slate nailed under the awning.

“Closed claim gets you through the drain toll,” he said. “It doesn't pay the crossing.”

“We've brought the fares.”

“And this is the slower crossing. Weather holds, Moon Pier before the night barrier. Weather doesn't, you sleep on the boat.”

Nil had slept on a chair and a roof in the last two nights. A boat sounded almost like choosing a different trade.

“Can the office stop us?” they asked.

Tetsu turned the paper back toward them. “This claim's closed. If a new order reaches the landing before I leave, I read the new order. I don't pretend a piece of paper means more than it says.”

He did not ask why the figure above Nil's head would not settle. He had seen it; his gaze had paused, then returned to the receipt.

Vesper paid. Tetsu wrote three entries, tore off the fare counterfoil and handed it to Maeve, who already had a place for paper in her folio. Nil took back the original review. Seal 1 remained separately wrapped inside their coat, where a loose hand could not mistake it for a coin.

“Tools under the bench,” Tetsu said. “Weight low. No standing when we turn out of the canal.”

Nil stepped aboard first and held the bundle while Maeve followed. They tried to take its heavier end with their right hand, forgot the scrape and hissed.

“Left,” Maeve said.

“I know.”

“Your hand seems to have missed the meeting.”

They shifted their grip. She accepted the help once it was actually help.

The boat moved before Nil felt ready for Meridian to leave them. The landing drifted sideways; the stair behind it narrowed to a grey crease. They searched the rooftops for the one on which they had slept. Every roof looked like another roof. Somewhere under them Nought was answering questions, Baker was setting out bowls, and Ama had a receipt that ought to be enough.

Nil touched the notebook beneath their coat. They had written an address for a woman in a window. They had no address of their own to give her.

“Gus knows the route,” Vesper said.

Nil looked up.

“You keep checking the paper as if it'll tell you she's followed us.”

“I said we'd remember.”

“Then remember. You don't have to carry the whole stair.”

She sat facing the canal behind them. Her hand rested near her holster without touching it. The small cut beneath her lip scar had dried to a dark line.

For a while they travelled between high stone walls. Water stains marked floods above the height of Nil's head. Workers on a maintenance shelf were hauling up a bent grate. One raised a hand to Tetsu; he answered without releasing the tiller.

Beyond the last lock, the canal widened into a reach of moving grey water. Nil felt the boat change its mind beneath them.

“Stay seated,” Tetsu said.

They sat more firmly, an action they would not previously have believed possible.

Maeve untied a packet of yesterday's bread. Gus had put it beside the fare receipt; Vesper had paid for it with the breakfast. Maeve broke it into three pieces and offered Tetsu a fourth from her own share.

He shook his head, then pointed with his chin to a cloth packet beneath his seat.

“You eat. Mine's there.”

They ate slowly. The bread was hard enough to require an intention. Nil watched a bird land on a half-submerged post, wobble and recover. No number hovered over it. The current did not appear to care.

“Do the river pools cover the birds?” they asked.

Tetsu glanced at the post. “Watershed reserve covers the habitat. Doesn't teach them where to perch.”

The bird abandoned the post and flew away, supplying its own opinion.

By midday the far bank had broken into low islands. Pine roots gripped stone walls. Narrow roofs appeared between trees, their ridges lifting at the ends. At one small landing a shrine bell hung above a rack of fishing floats. The bell did not ring when the wind reached it; a woman climbed onto a stool and freed the cord by hand.

“Hyakki?” Nil asked.

“Outer villages.”

“Are we nearly there?”

“Near enough to know we aren't.”

Vesper had fallen asleep with her hat tipped down, one boot braced against a bench leg. Maeve was checking the small iron pin she had saved from the forge lane. She had replaced the porter's temporary fitting before departure; this rejected piece was bent at its tip.

“Keeping it?” Nil asked.

“So I remember why it failed.”

Nil took out their washer, then reconsidered and put it away. Not every useful object required an exhibition.

Tetsu shifted the tiller. A stronger wind pushed beneath the cargo awning, and the water ahead darkened in a long uneven stripe.

“Canvas down.”

Vesper was awake at once. She rose too quickly.

“Seated,” Tetsu said, with exactly the same voice.

She sat, reached up and worked at the awning tie. The wet knot held. Maeve slid along the bench toward her.

“Not that end. It's loaded.”

“I have untied a knot before.”

“Then leave this one the pleasure of being your next.”

Maeve took the loose tail, eased it back through the loop and lowered the canvas in a controlled fold. Nil caught the corner with their left hand. Water ran off it onto their knees.

Tetsu turned the boat toward a sheltered cut between islands. A wave struck the side before they reached it. The bow rose, came down hard, and something knocked twice beneath the forward bench.

Maeve looked toward the sound. Tetsu was already looking.

“Brace pin.”

“I heard.” She lifted the bench flap. The brace beneath the small mast had worked loose at one end; each roll drove the iron against an enlarged hole. “Can't just hammer it back. It'll tear the soft edge.”

Vesper had the pin halfway into her hand before Maeve caught her wrist.

“Leave the load on it until we support the brace.”

For a breath neither moved. Nil saw the sharp reply Vesper almost chose. Then she opened her fingers.

“Tell me where.”

Maeve pointed to a short spar stowed along the side. “Under that crosspiece. Nil, fold the spare cloth twice. Not your dressing.”

They had already reached toward their right hand. They stopped and found the spare rag.

The repair took three people and most of the little space beneath the bench. Vesper held the spar where Maeve put it. Nil fed the folded cloth around the brace so the new fitting would bear against a wider surface. Maeve worked the damaged pin free, measured it against the spare stock Tetsu passed back, and chose a piece just long enough.

“Can I let go?” Vesper asked.

“Not yet.”

A wave struck. Nil's shoulder hit the bench edge. Vesper's arm trembled beneath the spar, but she kept it in place.

Maeve set the new pin, secured its retaining loop and checked the load before nodding.

“Now.”

Vesper let out a breath and lowered the spar. The brace held. Nothing brightened above anyone's head. Wood, iron and the right order of work had been sufficient.

They spent the next hour behind a stone breakwater, waiting for the gusts to ease. Tetsu bailed the water that had come aboard. Nil offered to help and was given a smaller scoop that could be used with the left hand.

When Tetsu leaned forward, the coat at his shoulder pulled across an old line of stitching. A rectangular patch had been removed there. Under its cleaner outline survived the little angled seam Nil had seen on the Collector's uniform.

They looked too long.

“Yes,” Tetsu said.

Nil set the scoop down. “Were you—”

“Used to be.”

Vesper watched the narrow opening beyond the breakwater. Maeve continued checking the brace, giving the question somewhere private to land.

“Why did you stop?” Nil asked.

Tetsu emptied his bucket over the side. “A long answer. Not a fare I sold you.”

Nil flushed. “I didn't mean—”

“I know. And you can still ask. I can still say no.”

He crouched beside the replaced line and beckoned Nil closer.

“This I can show you.”

The knot looked simple until Nil tried to copy it. Their loop came loose under a pull. Tetsu undid it without comment, put the end back in their left hand and showed where the standing part needed to lie.

“You want it to hold under weight and release when the weight comes off. Two different jobs.”

Nil tried again. This time the pull tightened the right part. Tetsu handed it back for a third attempt.

Across from them Vesper was rubbing the arm she had held under the spar.

“You were right,” she told Maeve.

“About the pin?”

“Let's start there.”

Maeve nodded, accepting exactly that much.

They reached Moon Pier after the lamps had been lit. Red lanterns hung above dark water, their reflections torn into long ribbons by the wake. Nil could hear drums somewhere inland. A procession of lights moved behind the roofs, rising and falling as if the whole street were breathing.

At the pier's upper end, a barrier lay across the shrine road. Its keeper was winding the day's chain into a locked housing.

Tetsu tied the boat to the landing and checked the knot Nil had helped make.

“Night barrier's down,” he said. “You can sleep aboard. Tide's slack here.”

Vesper looked toward the lights. “How long's the way around?”

“Longer in the dark. You don't know the water steps.”

Nil had already begun to gather the tool bundle. They set it back beneath the bench. The island would not vanish if they waited until morning. For the first time since leaving Nought's chair, they were tired enough to accept a safe place when it was offered.

Tetsu spread the spare canvas over the benches and gave them the dry side. Maeve checked the brace once more before lying down. Vesper placed her hat over her eyes.

“Nobody say first light,” she said.

Nil looked at the lantern procession beyond the roofs. One light lagged behind the others, hurried, then stopped beside a doorway to wait for something they could not see.

They thought of a woman climbing a stool to free a bell.

Under the canvas, with water making small sounds against the hull, Nil practiced Tetsu's knot until their fingers could remember it without looking.

## Chapter 9: A Hundred Wishes

The umbrella stole Vesper's hat.

It happened at the first drumbeat. One moment the hat shaded her eyes; the next it was rising above the crowd on the tip of a red-and-white canopy.

Vesper caught the brim before it cleared her head. The canopy blinked.

One large eye, painted where a sensible umbrella would have put nothing at all, regarded her with magnificent disappointment.

“A successful opening,” said a small girl beneath it, “requires cooperation.”

“So does keeping your fingers.”

“Hina has fingers. See?”

She displayed ten, then appeared to reconsider whether that was the relevant number. Her black bob ended just above an indigo collar. Vermilion sleeves hung almost to her knees; little numbers climbed the cloth in uneven groups. Nil followed them as far as 99 and found no hundred.

The girl stood on raised wooden sandals. The umbrella floated a handspan above her shoulder, its long tongue protruding at Vesper's hat. When she frowned, it frowned too.

“Then keep them,” Vesper said, settling the hat back into place.

They had spent most of the day near Moon Pier. Tetsu had left with a cargo of empty baskets, telling them the shrine road would reopen at midday. It had reopened only for residents with household marks. At the inspection table, an officer had unrolled a fresh Meridian notice.

Vesper had seen it before Nil did. She steered them away to a stall selling warm rice and waited until the drums began to move through the market.

“Public parade route goes over the lower bridge,” she had said. “We can cross with everyone else. No stamp queue.”

“And if everyone else wants us arrested?” Maeve asked.

“Then I'll admit a flaw in the plan.”

Now the plan had acquired an umbrella.

Hina looked from Vesper to Nil. Her mock indignation faded into attention.

“Oh,” she said.

Nil's hand rose toward the place above their head. They lowered it before the gesture was complete.

“It's not catching,” Vesper said.

“I didn't say it was.” Hina leaned closer, then checked herself. “May I look?”

Nil had not been asked that very often.

“I don't know what you'd see.”

“Neither does Hina. That's why looking would be interesting.”

They nodded. Hina circled them once, the umbrella turning with her, and stopped in front again.

“You smell like a hundred wishes.”

Nil glanced at their damp coat. “I thought it was canal water.”

Maeve laughed. It escaped her before she could make it polite.

Hina drew herself up. “A person can have both.”

Another drumbeat rolled through the street. Behind Hina came a long procession of lanterns, lacquered trays, furred shoulders and human faces. A man carried a paper crane large enough to shelter a child beneath each wing. A woman with a basket head argued about the pace with a porter in ordinary boots. Two children marched solemnly behind a dancing kettle, until it stopped for breath and they demanded their turn at being important.

Nil looked for the thing that made this impossible and found instead all the small things that made it a street: someone watching a purse, someone carrying a tired child, someone apologizing after a heel landed on a foot.

The kettle had feet. That was new. The apology was familiar.

“Are you coming?” Hina asked.

“Where?”

“Across. That's usually what bridges are for.”

She skipped toward the procession. Vesper looked back at the inspection table, where the officer was fastening the new notice beneath a lamp.

“For once,” she said, “a sensible local guide.”

Hina heard her and made the umbrella execute an unnecessarily difficult turn.

They joined the Night Parade near its middle. Nil kept close enough to Maeve that the tool bundle would not strike anyone when the crowd narrowed. Vesper took the outside, where she could see both the bridge approach and the nearest stairs.

The lower bridge was broad and wooden, its centre raised over a channel black with reflected lantern light. Nine little plaques hung above one entrance; seven above the other. Hina took them under the seven.

“Less argument,” she said.

Nil glanced at the other entrance. A woman with a Civic 04 was showing her pass to a marshal. The marshal waved her through after reading it.

“I was told four was unlucky here.”

“People say lots of things. Some contracts charge more around four and nine. Some households hang wards. Some people just need to cross the bridge.” Hina's umbrella turned its eye toward the marshal. “My number's nine. Nobody gets to make me walk in the river.”

Above her black hair the small Civic 09 shone steadily. It was a different thing from the 99 stitched near her sleeve. Nil was beginning to understand how much trouble could be made by pretending numbers needed no explanation.

At the bridge's high point Hina stopped to watch a row of sweet stalls on the far bank.

“One day,” she said, “Hina will buy the enormous one.”

Nil followed her gaze to a rice cake shaped like a moon, large enough to require two hands.

“For a festival?”

“For a Tuesday.”

“What's on Tuesday?”

“Nothing. That's the point.”

She began counting on her fingers. A cake with no occasion. A room with a hook low enough for her umbrella. A trip on a ferry where Ofuku did not spend the whole departure checking the weather. A blue ribbon she would wear until she disliked blue. A chance to see snow without being put away before it melted.

“You can dislike blue after buying it?” Nil asked.

Hina considered them. The next flourish came more gently.

“It would be expensive to like everything forever.”

Nil thought of a bed above a repair shop. They had never imagined the colour of its blanket. A bed had seemed ambitious enough.

“What would you choose?” Hina asked.

“Work.”

“After work.”

“Supper.”

“After supper.”

Nil looked at the moon cake. “I don't know.”

Hina did not laugh. “Then we'll need a bigger list.”

They came down from the bridge into a lane where household lanterns stood beside the public ones. Names had been written on little wooden tags, and some of the tags were so weathered that a second hand had renewed the ink over the first. Hina greeted a comb seller, scolded the dancing kettle for taking a shortcut and ducked beneath an old man's elbow to straighten the cord on his lantern.

“You know everyone,” Maeve said.

“Not everyone. That one owes Ofuku a box.”

The old man pretended not to hear.

Near the end of the lane stood a little carrier made from plaited straw. Two old sandals hung from its shoulder cord, their repairs so elaborate that almost none of the original straps remained. Nil first mistook it for a costume. Then the figure lifted a hand and light passed through the fingers.

Hina stopped.

The carrier tried to tighten its shoulder cord. Its hand went through the knot.

“Again?” Hina said.

There was no performance in her voice now.

A woman emerged from the doorway behind it, carrying a small ledger and a spool of vow cord. “The road channel missed the evening allotment. I sent the request before noon.”

“It's the same household mark?”

“Same mark. Same reserve. New clearance number.”

The straw carrier lifted its other hand. This one was still solid. It patted the woman's sleeve twice, an attempt to comfort her that made her mouth tighten.

Nil moved forward. The decoder under their cuff had warmed at the sight of the cord. They thought of the jar, the empty return fitting, Ama's frightened steadiness.

Hina caught their left sleeve.

“Do you know this channel?”

Nil looked at the spool, the household tag, the thin hand. “No.”

“Then don't pull it.”

Maeve had already put down her tools. She knelt beside the door hinge, which had sagged far enough to trap the cord under its lower edge.

“This part I can free,” she said to the woman. “May I?”

The woman nodded. Maeve lifted the door just enough to ease the cord out without cutting it. Vesper held the lamp steady. Hina opened a little wooden case on the threshold and took out a sealed emergency length, checking its tag before she broke the paper.

“Household reserve,” she told Nil. “Ofuku left it for this. Not enough for every night.”

She asked the carrier to set its solid hand against the household tag. The woman fitted the emergency cord into the open channel, and Hina kept the old strand clear while it took the load.

The missing fingers returned slowly, each no more substantial at first than wet paper held to a lamp. The carrier looked at them, then flexed them with painstaking care.

Nil let out a breath they had not meant to hold.

Hina did not.

“How long?” she asked.

“Morning, if the line stays open.”

The carrier reached for Hina. She let it rest its new fingers against the edge of her sleeve. For a moment the umbrella above her shoulder drooped so low that its eye disappeared beneath the canopy's rim.

From the main street came another cheer. The parade was passing beyond them. A child called for someone to wait.

Nil watched Hina make her face ready before she turned around.

“Well,” she said. “That was a very poor performance. Nobody clap.”

Nobody did.

They helped bring the carrier inside, away from the cold draught through the doorway. The woman refused Vesper's offered coin and showed her the pending request instead.

“I've paid the channel fee. I need them to open the route.”

Vesper put the coin away. “We'll remember the number.”

Nil wrote it down beside Livia's window. Two places now, two promises they had carefully tried not to make.

Outside, a court messenger stood beneath a dry awning. A notice offered additional restoration coverage to registered households under Tamamo's permanence programme. The writing was beautiful. Beneath the programme's name a narrower line deferred approval to the palace.

Hina read it twice.

“Does it work?” Nil asked.

“The court keeps people safe.”

It was not quite the answer to the question. Hina seemed to hear that herself.

“Sometimes the court also says things before Ofuku can find out what they mean.”

The messenger called to her. She knew him; she raised a hand, then lowered it without approaching.

Maeve lifted her bundle. “Where's Ofuku?”

Hina looked toward the road rising above the market. It led to the brighter roofs and the palace lamps. Then she turned down a narrower path, where the light came from ordinary windows.

“This way.”

Vesper glanced at Nil. The inspection lanterns were distant now. They could keep following the public crowd and disappear into another landing before dawn.

Nil looked at the request number in the notebook, then at Hina's umbrella. The one eye was open again, but it was watching the ground.

They followed her.

At a crooked little gate Hina stopped. Beyond it, someone was arguing with a drawer that would not shut.

“Don't believe her if she says she wasn't waiting up,” Hina whispered.

The voice inside called, “If you're telling them I waited up, bring the dry wood before you come in.”

Hina's whole face changed.

“A keeper of great and terrible perception,” she announced, and pushed open the gate.

## Chapter 10: The Keeper's Gift

Ofuku's first question was whether any of them could lift a sack without making a ceremony of it.

Maeve put down her tools and took one end. Vesper took the other. Nil reached to help, but the old woman pointed at their dressing.

“You can hold the door.”

Nil held it.

The sack contained clean rice hulls for packing repaired objects. A second sack contained ash. Ofuku was very clear about which belonged by the storage shelves and which belonged beside the stove. Hina repeated the instructions in an impressive voice and was sent outside for the wood she had tried to forget.

The workshop occupied three rooms around a small courtyard. Nothing was displayed without also being in somebody's way. A drying rack hung above the low table; ledgers stood in a basket under it. On a high shelf a row of daruma figures looked down with different combinations of painted eyes. A card beneath one read: **Deliver after the roof is patched.**

“Not a prophecy,” Ofuku said when she saw Nil looking. “The roof leaks on the shelf where she wants to keep it.”

She was an elderly human woman with a loose knot of white hair and a sleeve held out of the wash water by a wooden clip. Her hands had the slight tremor of tiredness until she took up a brush. Then the tremor found something useful to do and disappeared into the stroke.

Hina returned with an armful of wood and kicked the gate shut behind her.

“No feet on the gate.”

“The hands were occupied.”

“So was the latch.”

Hina put down the wood with the air of someone whose household contained an unreasonable number of objects capable of giving evidence.

Ofuku listened to the story of the carrier in the market. She asked which emergency cord had been opened, how the fingers had looked afterwards and whether the woman had countersigned the request. When Hina produced the little broken seal, Ofuku flattened it beside the ledger instead of throwing it away.

“That was the last short reserve at that address,” she said.

“I know.”

“Then we move the next delivery ahead of the cabinet work.”

“The cabinet has been waiting.”

“So has the carrier.”

Hina took the pencil from behind Ofuku's ear and changed the order on the wall slate. She did not ask permission. Ofuku did not require it.

Nil watched the household make a decision that a public office might have taken a month to refuse. It was tempting to believe that smaller rooms must always be kinder. Then Ofuku turned the slate around and showed them the back: six more requests, three still without materials, two without a clear route to the reserve that was supposed to cover them.

“You can't move everyone ahead,” she said, mostly to Hina. “We need the roads open.”

They ate from bowls that did not match. Ofuku set aside a portion for a delivery worker who would come later. Vesper offered payment; Ofuku accepted enough for the additional rice, refused the larger coin and said beds would mean clearing the packing room.

“We can do that,” Maeve said.

“I mean clear it without losing the labels. Visitors have been enthusiastic before.”

After supper Ofuku spread the work list on the table. Maeve could straighten packing hinges and replace a broken stool brace. Vesper could carry sealed requests to the night dispatch box just outside the gate with Hina. Nil could sort washers by bore size, sitting down, with the left hand.

“Do I look that breakable?” Nil asked.

“You look as though you'd tell me after.”

They could not honestly disagree.

Hina objected to having her umbrella put on the high storage hooks. Ofuku said the floor was damp. Hina said the hooks faced the wall and she disliked looking at plaster. Ofuku said it was the driest wall.

“Then turn the hook.”

“It won't hold that way.”

Maeve looked up from the stool. “It will if I brace it.”

Both of them turned toward her.

“Would you like me to?” she added.

Hina considered the hook, then nodded. “A place of honour. At a sensible height.”

Ofuku handed Maeve a small length of sound wood. The argument ended in a shelf brace and a lower hook, with enough clearance for the canopy to turn. Nil felt absurdly pleased by it.

While Hina and Vesper were out, Maeve finished the stool and tested it beneath her own weight. Nil had completed three little dishes of washers. Their right palm itched under its cloth, which was better than throbbing, although the itch required more discipline.

“Your grandmother worked with these?” they asked as Maeve examined an old ward frame on the shelf.

She turned the frame so the fitting caught the lamplight. “She supplied road charms. This type, before the newer locks.”

“Before the Ledger?”

Maeve shook her head. “Before the last tightening. TR990. The big system was old long before she was born.”

Ofuku brought a folder from a drawer and found a supplier leaf dated in that year. Maeve recognized her grandmother's trade stamp near its bottom. For a moment she stopped looking like someone who always knew what tool to reach for.

She touched the mark with a finger that was clean of oil.

“That's hers.”

The old frame had a narrow removable throat. The newer drawing beside it showed a locked return gate where that throat had been. Maeve compared the two and frowned.

“The local shops could service this one.”

“Until the clearance had to come through the court,” Ofuku said. “Some of the new locks stopped people stripping a household's reserve. Some kept paid work waiting behind the same desk.”

“Can I take a rubbing?”

“Of the trade stamp. The household figures aren't ours to send around.”

Maeve covered the figures before rubbing the paper. Nil held the lamp and watched the old maker's mark appear, line by line. It was not a key to every closed road. It was something her grandmother had actually touched.

When they finished, Maeve noticed the split in one umbrella rib. Hina had left the canopy on its new hook, half open, the eye comfortably shut. A small square patch near the split had been sewn over a larger one. Its corner caught whenever the paper folded.

Maeve reached for her needle.

“It's catching here,” she murmured.

She eased the old stitches out, slipped a narrow support along the rib and joined the newer paper cleanly over it. The repair was sound. Nil saw her test the fold twice, careful not to force it.

The removed patch lay on the bench when Hina came back.

She stopped in the doorway.

Vesper nearly walked into her and caught the dispatch bag before it swung against the frame.

“What did you do?” Hina asked.

Maeve looked up. “The rib was splitting. I supported—”

“The patch.”

“It was catching at the fold. I've put new—”

“I didn't ask you to.”

The umbrella's eye opened on the hook. It was Hina's eye in another place; Nil understood that more sharply now than when it had stolen the hat. Her small hands were clenched against her sleeves.

Maeve set down the needle.

Ofuku reached toward the discarded paper, then stopped before touching it.

“It's still here,” she said.

“I can see that.”

Hina crossed the room and took the patch herself. It was little more than a faded square, the red almost gone from one edge. A crooked black stitch crossed its middle.

“She made this when I first asked to stand outside,” Hina said. “The rain kept finding the hole. We didn't have the right paper.”

Ofuku rubbed her thumb against the wooden sleeve clip.

“It was a piece from my old shopping cover.”

“It has the stitch where she couldn't see the needle.” Hina held it up. “I could see it. I told her where to put it.”

Maeve's face had gone still.

“I thought making it fold properly—”

She stopped. Nil recognized the urge to finish the sentence until the hurt became something that had happened by mistake to nobody in particular.

Maeve drew her hands back from the umbrella.

“I took it off without asking. I'm sorry.”

Hina looked down at the patch. “Can it go back?”

“Yes. The rib support can stay under it, if you want. It won't look as smooth.”

“I don't want smooth.”

“Show me where.”

Hina placed the patch against the paper herself. Maeve waited until she had turned it twice and chosen the old angle. Then she showed her where a new stitch could hold it without straining the faded edge.

“There?”

Hina nodded.

They worked beside each other. It took longer to put the patch back than it had taken to remove it. Maeve caught no one's eye while she worked. When the umbrella folded, the old corner still made a small uneven ridge. Hina opened it again, looked up through the crooked stitch and let out her breath.

Ofuku picked up her cup, found it empty and set it down.

“I should have stopped you,” she told Maeve.

Hina looked at her. “You thought it was better too.”

“I did.”

“It wasn't.”

“No.”

The word rested awkwardly in the room. Nobody hurried to put something over it.

Later, when Nil was tying a fresh cloth loosely over the scab on their palm, Hina sat beside them. The umbrella leaned where she had chosen, facing the door.

“Did the patch hurt?” Nil asked.

Hina considered. “It felt wrong before I knew how to say it.”

Nil looked at their decoder. “I know that feeling.”

Hina almost smiled. “Then don't let her improve you while you're asleep.”

Across the room Maeve heard. She did not pretend otherwise.

“I won't,” she said.

The workshop gate clicked. A court courier stood outside with a sealed notice, addressed to the keeper and to the manifestation in her care. Ofuku read it at the table while Hina stood beside her.

The offer named Hina. It promised permanent manifestation under Tamamo's new compact: no further household renewals, no seasonal thinning. In return the unregistered traveller called Nil was to enter court custody and remain available for transfer under the Meridian bounty.

“Available,” Vesper repeated.

The courier kept his eyes on the seal. “The court offers safe keeping.”

“Whose?”

He did not answer that.

Hina read the promise again. Her fingers went to the restored patch, slowly, as though remembering what could be lost even when someone intended to help.

“Would I still be able to change it?” she asked.

“The terms can be explained at the palace.”

“Then they haven't been explained here.” Ofuku folded the notice without tearing it. “We'll keep the copy.”

After the courier left, nobody said the offer had not been tempting.

Hina stood very straight. “I want to ask Enma.”

Ofuku looked tired enough that Nil thought she would say no. Instead she fetched a dry folder from beneath the table.

“Tomorrow. His archive opens after the first public petitions.”

Nil looked at the notice. They had spent so long hoping an office would find an entry for them. Now someone was offering to make Hina safe if another office could keep Nil on a list.

“Will he know what I am?”

“He'll know what his book says,” Ofuku replied. “Ask him to tell you which is which.”

She showed them where to spread the sleeping mats in the cleared packing room. Before turning down the lamp she moved the new hook a finger's width, until Hina's umbrella could see the door without touching the wall.

## Chapter 11: Paper Without a Name

The woman ahead of them wanted a spelling corrected.

She had brought two marriage records, a household tag and a letter from the dead man's brother. The junior clerk kept returning to the oldest copy, where the wrong stroke was very beautifully written.

“My father could write his own name,” she said. “He wrote it on the boat. You can still go and look.”

At the far end of the room a great brush stopped moving.

Enma rose from behind the archive desk. Nobody struck a gong to announce him. He came to the little table, read the letter and asked the woman whether the boat could be visited before the afternoon tide.

“My brother's there now.”

“Then let the witness go while he can.”

He marked the disputed entry without erasing the earlier hand. The clerk began to explain why the old copy had been preferred.

“Keep the explanation with the correction,” Enma said. “The next reader will need both.”

Nil had expected a gate tall enough to make them feel small. There was one outside, painted dark red, but the waiting room beyond it contained benches, an ink stain shaped like a fish and a cracked tile someone had covered with a woven mat. The people waiting were mostly occupied with paper. Grief had brought several of them; none looked grateful to have been made part of an impressive building.

Hina sat beside Nil with the umbrella across her knees. Its eye watched the disputed spelling until the woman left.

“He can correct it,” Nil whispered.

“When there's evidence.”

“What if there isn't?”

Hina rubbed the edge of her patch. “That's what we're asking.”

Enma called them after the last public petitioner. He wore his authority in the careful placement of his hands: nothing was moved until there was room for it, nothing set aside where it could be mistaken for finished. His face was grave without appearing hungry for anyone else's fear.

He read Ofuku's introduction, the bounty notice and the closed drain review. He asked Nil which part they wanted examined first.

“Whether I'm in your book.”

“You have chosen a name?”

“Nil.”

He wrote it on a request slip, exactly as Nought had. The small i and l looked ordinary in his large handwriting.

“And the displayed result has remained unresolved since before that choice?”

Nil nodded.

“Searching a personal entry is one request. Entering the protected comparison room is another. I can begin the search now. If you wish to witness the original comparison, you must show that you can close a loaded channel without redirecting it through the room.”

Vesper looked up. “What does that demonstration cost?”

Enma led them through an inner door. Beyond it stood a low practice frame between two stone basins. Three little wheels faced a bank of weighted shutters. No blade, jaw or human figure occupied the far side.

“An isolated training reserve,” he said. “Nothing drawn from household or restoration coverage. A loss leaves the shutters closed. We cancel the circuit and you may return with an authorized operator. I will still issue the search result. No person's body or Civic allocation is offered.”

He showed them a broad release bar on their side of the frame. Maeve pressed it before anything was loaded. All three wheels stopped, and a dry marker fell into the empty basin.

“Four completed rounds,” Enma continued. “No Parry. The trial concerns how a channel is carried forward; closing every return path would conceal the fault it is designed to expose. You can cancel at any point.”

Nil looked at zero on the defence wheel. Its little interval had become the safest place they knew how to find.

“What happens when the shutters act?”

“They close part of the training route. You can move the available load through a declared return. If you cannot, the gauge falls. The housing bears the force.”

Maeve examined the bolted housing before she nodded. Vesper read the reserve tag and its cancellation line. Hina put the umbrella on a dry support where its eye could watch both basins.

Nil could refuse the demonstration and still receive the answer. They stayed by the frame for a moment, feeling the difference between that and a door whose only alternatives were surrender and escape.

“I want to see the original,” they said.

Enma set the request slip where all of them could see it. “Then let us make the route safe.”

The first round began with a deep wooden click. Each strike had a declared base of fifty training units, printed beside the gauge. The actual digit would be added to it.

Nil stopped 5. Vesper, taking the second position, caught 3.

For an instant Vesper's 3 remained in both windows. Then the scoring window advanced to 4. Nil's final stop landed on 6 and stayed there in both windows.

Nothing useful matched. A shutter closed, and part of the practice gauge dropped into the lower basin.

Nil reached instinctively toward the defence catch.

“Not that path,” Vesper said.

Their hand stopped above it. The brass return line trembled, then settled against the housing with a hard knock. No one had been struck. Nil nevertheless felt their shoulder anticipate the Hound's muzzle.

“She stopped three,” Nil said.

“She did,” Enma said.

“Then why did it change?”

He opened a narrow cover over the route between the second and third scoring windows. A tiny pin stood upright.

“The first actual strike above fifty lifts this. Your first was fifty-five. The lifted pin advances the next scoring digit once. Operators call it Carry.”

“Not the actual wheel,” Maeve said, leaning close.

“Not the landing.”

Nil looked at Vesper's actual 3, still faithfully recorded beside the 4. The decoder had not invented a different stop. It had carried an operation to a place they had failed to read.

“Can it happen again from the changed number?”

“No. One carry in the round. A changed scoring digit is not a new actual strike.” Enma lowered the pin and showed them the one-use latch beneath it. “Nine advances to zero. If there is no next scoring position, it has nowhere to go and expires. It is not stored for another round.”

“That would have been useful to know first,” Nil said.

Enma rested a hand on the open cover. “It would. I should have shown the latch before loading the frame.”

He did not turn the correction into a lesson about Nil's impatience. He gave them the release bar again and waited while they decided whether to continue.

Nil glanced at Vesper. She had not mocked the hand that had reached for zero.

“You saw it move?” they asked.

“After your first. I thought I'd caught the wrong face.”

“It was.” Nil studied the pin. “I just didn't know what I'd done.”

For the second round they agreed on the actual stops first. Nil would take 5. If they caught it, Vesper would take another 5, allowing the carry to score it as 6. Nil would then seek 7.

“And if you miss?” Vesper asked.

“Read what happens. Don't pretend the plan happened instead.”

She smiled slightly. “I have been trying to put that into words.”

Nil caught 5. The actual strike read 55 and lifted the pin. Vesper waited through an entire turn before accepting 5. The scoring frame advanced it once to 6; the one-use latch lay flat. Nil's final 7 remained 7.

The Cascade opened three shutters in sequence. The next closing action stayed held long enough for another round.

Hina leaned forward, umbrella tilting with her. “It worked.”

“It worked once,” Maeve said, with the seriousness of someone who kept failed pins.

In the third round Nil tried to repeat too quickly. They stopped 7 instead of 5. Vesper caught 7; the carry made it 8. Nil caught 3. Seven, eight, three. The shutters did not mistake their determination for a pattern.

The bonus interval ended. Another part of the practice gauge fell.

One round remained.

Nil looked at the open shutters, then at the basin. There was enough declared reserve left to finish if they could close a useful pattern. There was also an entirely intact release bar within reach.

“We can come back,” Maeve said.

Hina nodded. Nil could see that she meant it, although the court's notice lay inside her sleeve.

They did not have to win to keep the company around them. That made it easier, rather than harder, to look at the wheel.

“Six,” Nil told Vesper. “Actual five for you, if I get six. Then six again.”

The wheel turned. Nil let one 6 pass while their hand steadied. The next came into reach and they stopped it.

The pin lifted.

Vesper took 5. Its actual window stayed 5. The scoring window became 6.

Nil listened for the spring's little pause, the same ordinary slack they had first heard in a yard full of broken cages.

Six.

The three scored sixes locked together. The remaining trial shutters opened. The gauge settled, and the unused training load returned to its labelled reserve.

Enma disconnected the frame before anyone put a hand inside it.

Hina let out a whoop so unceremonious that two clerks looked through the door. She shut her mouth, considered the situation and bowed to them with excessive dignity.

“An examination,” she said, “has concluded.”

Vesper laughed into her sleeve.

***

The comparison room was colder than the waiting room. Its shelves rose only a little above Enma's head, but they extended farther than Nil could see. At each junction a small plaque identified the source of the records: household declarations, shrine witnesses, probate returns, regional comparison indexes.

“Not every life in a single book,” Enma said when Nil stopped beneath the plaques. “Copies disagree. Some arrive late. Some never arrive.”

“You still look for them.”

“Yes.”

He searched the chosen name, then the unresolved account signature, then the possible cross-references provided by the decoder's registration field. The result was not a dramatic page tearing itself out. There was simply no link where the lookup should have returned one.

Enma repeated the query against an independently maintained index. He checked its last received date before setting down the brush.

Hina stood close enough that her sleeve touched Nil's coat. She did not look at their head.

“No entry?” Nil asked.

“No matching entry in these records.”

“Living or dead?”

“No matching cross-reference under either. That is the finding. It does not tell me why.”

Nil looked at the unmarked request slip. They had imagined a page waiting somewhere, a space made for them before they learned to ask. Even a bad page might have meant someone had known where to begin.

“Then what do you call someone who isn't in it?”

Enma looked at the name he had written.

“You asked me to call you Nil.”

Their eyes stung. They were annoyed by it, and more annoyed that annoyance did not help.

“The court wants me.”

“The court has an interest in a missing entry. Interest is not knowledge.”

He wrote the finding in plain language, listing the records searched and their dates. Beneath it he added that absence from this archive did not certify absence of life, personhood, history or claim to care. He left room for another keeper's comparison.

“Do you want this recorded?” he asked.

Nil read it twice. “Yes. With the limits.”

Enma set a narrow access sleeve beside the finding. “Who may receive the search copy?”

Nil looked at Hina. The court had offered to buy the missing entry before knowing what was missing. They did not want their answer hanging beside a bounty notice.

“Ofuku for safekeeping. Hina to carry it. The others can read it with me.”

“The palace?”

“We can show them the finding and its limits. Not the query signature.”

Enma marked the restricted attachment separately. “A future comparison will need your permission for that part.”

Hina read the names on the sleeve before accepting it. Nil had expected the blank place in a book to leave them with nothing. They had not expected to have to decide who could see it.

Enma pressed his witness mark beside the text. The original went into his archive. A counter-copy went into a dry sleeve, its temporary clasp to be sealed for travel after Ofuku had witnessed the receiving copy.

Nil reached for it, then stopped. Their coat already held the closed review, the Seal, Livia's address and Nought's notebook.

Hina held out both hands.

“I can carry it,” she said. “If you want.”

Nil gave it to her. She placed it inside the flat inner pocket beneath her obi, away from the umbrella's wet edge.

Outside, the woman with the disputed spelling was waiting for the returning witness. She had bought two steamed buns and kept one wrapped beside her. When she saw Nil, she moved her bag to make room on the bench.

They sat while Maeve and Vesper checked the road back.

The woman looked at the finding's sleeve in Hina's hands.

“Not the answer you wanted?”

“I don't know yet.”

She nodded as though that was an answer a person might reasonably have. When her witness came through the gate, she stood so quickly she nearly left the spare bun behind, then turned back and caught it.

Nil watched them go inside together.

Hina remained beside the bench, holding the copy flat.

“We should eat,” she said at last. “Before we argue with another palace.”

Nil wiped their face with the uninjured hand and stood.

## Chapter 12: The Foxes' Bargain

The road to the palace divided at a tree that had grown around an old sign.

One arrow pointed through the trunk. Another pointed down into its roots. Hina passed both and took the narrow path to the left.

“The sign is older than the road,” she said.

“They could change the sign,” Nil replied.

“They ask it every year.”

Vesper looked at the lettering, then at the thick bark around it. “Does it answer?”

“It says the road could change back.”

Nobody could think of a useful reply to that.

They had left Ofuku's house after breakfast on the sixth morning since Nil had missed the repair trial. The scab on Nil's palm no longer needed a thick dressing. They kept a clean strip over it for the climb, mostly to stop them picking at it. Ofuku had insisted on sending rice packets, the court notice and a list of restoration requests that had waited longer than the court's own posted limit.

“I can't carry all those,” Hina had complained.

“Then ask a friend.”

Hina had looked at the four of them and seemed briefly unsure how to use the word. Maeve had held out the folio. That had settled the paper, if not the larger question.

The foxfire began below the palace steps. Small blue flames occupied stone cups along one side of the path; red ones burned along the other. They gave almost no heat. Nil put their hand above one and felt only the cold morning moving between their fingers.

“Don't touch the wick,” said a voice ahead.

“It will still burn you,” added another.

Two retainers stood under the outer gate. At first Nil thought the second was the first reflected in a dark panel. Then one turned to read the names on a message strip, while the other kept watching the path.

Ao wore blue; Aka wore red. Each had one fox tail, and their tied hair fell on opposite sides. Similar faces made the differences in expression easier to notice. Ao looked as though he had been waiting for a late delivery. Aka looked as though he had been waiting to hear why it was late.

“Hina of the keeper's house,” Ao said.

“Hina of Hina,” she answered.

The umbrella tipped its canopy toward them, showing the restored patch. Neither retainer laughed.

Ao read the court notice and Enma's receiving sleeve. Aka read the household requests on the back of Ofuku's list.

“These aren't audience credentials,” Ao said.

“They're requests nobody answered,” Hina replied.

“The road office answers those.”

“The road office says the palace has the lock.”

Aka looked at the oldest date. “This one's past the posted interval.”

“The carrier's fingers went away,” Hina said. “We opened the last emergency cord.”

Ao folded the message strip, carefully enough that Nil could see him taking time before speaking.

“The court is keeping three shelters open while the mainland withholds the next reserve clearance. Tamamo has asked for permanence because renewals keep arriving too late. That isn't an excuse for a late request. It is what happens to the requests after we receive them.”

“Then show us,” Hina said.

The two retainers looked at each other. Their paired speech had been a kind of door; for the first time it stood open long enough to see people behind it.

“We can grant a witnessed approach,” Ao said. “Not armed entry into the private rooms.”

Vesper unclipped her holster without drawing the revolver. “A locked rack, a receipt, and it stays in sight.”

Maeve showed her hammer. “This too?”

“The covered workroom,” Aka said. “No one touches the bundle without you.”

They agreed. Vesper and Maeve would remain at the outer court as the named witnesses, with their belongings sealed in a rack they could see. Nil and Hina would hear the offer in the receiving hall. If either wanted to leave, the approach channel had to return them to this gate.

“Put that last part in the copy,” Vesper said.

Ao did.

Before admitting them he led Nil and Hina to two little posts beside the first step. Each held a flame beneath a glass hood. A silver thread joined the posts to the gate's approach channel.

“The court uses images,” he said. “The receiving rooms are safe only if you can distinguish the marked path from the image of one. This demonstration is bounded to these steps. You can stop and ask us to escort you instead.”

“Is the escort price different?” Nil asked.

“No.”

“Then why test?”

Ao looked at the little flames. “Because people sometimes decide they know a room better than its keeper.”

Aka glanced at his brother. “And keepers sometimes decide an explanation is complete because they gave it once.”

The demonstration began. Across the first step a second stair appeared, pale and inviting, leading around the post toward a door that had not been there a moment before.

Nil looked at the silver thread. Its reflection followed the false stair for several inches, then ended in the empty space beneath it.

Hina lowered the umbrella until its eye was level with the real post. The flame's painted reflection seemed to lean toward the new door. Beneath the hood the actual wick had not moved.

“This way,” she said, taking the original stair.

Nil followed. The false door thinned behind them. There had never been a passage there, but the image had made it easy to forget where the wall ought to stand.

At the second post the path appeared to continue straight ahead. Hina took a step and stopped before her sandal came down.

Nil could see why. The approach thread turned right into a little metal guide. The image went on without it.

“It's like a wheel,” Nil said. “The picture can lie. The actual stop has a witness.”

“A comparison,” Aka said. “Not a universal cure for being deceived.”

“I didn't say it was.”

“Keep saying that.”

Ao lowered the demonstration hoods. Both false paths vanished. The gate behind them remained open, with Vesper leaning against its inner post and Maeve counting the rack seals.

Hina looked past the retainers toward the palace roofs.

“Why hasn't the household line been opened?”

“Clearance must come through the court key,” Ao said.

“Does it have to?”

He hesitated. Aka took the request list from him.

“The local route exists,” Aka said. “Its opening authority was withdrawn under the newer compact.”

“Because unreviewed local transfers emptied two winter shelters,” Ao said sharply. “If you tell half the story, tell it to someone who wasn't there.”

Aka lowered the list. For a moment his expression lost all its quickness.

“I was there.”

“Then help me keep the next shelter open.”

The silence after that was not meant for visitors. Nil looked down at the paper rather than pretending they had not heard.

Hina lifted her umbrella. “Ofuku's asking for named local witnesses. Not for a hole anyone can empty.”

Ao read the oldest request again.

“She'll need a reserve schedule,” he said.

“She sent one.” Maeve's voice came from the gate. “Back page. I helped add the delivery times.”

Aka turned the sheet over. Ao did not ask him to turn it back.

***

The receiving hall smelled of lamp oil, dry paper and medicines being boiled somewhere out of sight.

Nil had expected a throne. There was a raised seat at the far end, but Tamamo stood beside a long worktable, reviewing a care roster with an attendant. Her layered robes were violet and pale gold. Behind her, nine luminous tails moved with the slow deliberation of lights drifting beneath water. Digits shone at their tips, too bright to confuse with the Civic 99 above her.

She finished speaking to the attendant before turning to her visitors.

“Hina,” she said. “You have brought the keeper's objections.”

Hina swallowed. “Some of them are mine.”

Tamamo inclined her head. “Then I should hear them from you.”

Up close, her warmth did not feel false. That made Nil more uneasy than a threat would have. She had read Hina's restoration requests and knew which household had used its last cord. She asked whether the fingers had held through morning. Hina said they had, and Tamamo's shoulders eased almost imperceptibly.

“I am glad.”

She led them to a screened side room where three patients rested in supported manifestations. A lacquered instrument lay under a fine mesh of vow threads. Beside it, the outline of a woman came and went around a wooden hairpin. An attendant changed a basin of warm water without interrupting the woman's quiet account of a dream.

“The old channels can still hold them,” Tamamo said. “But every renewal asks us to prove again that they have not ceased to matter. I cannot promise them a quick repair.”

Nil looked at the hairpin. Its keeper had wrapped the damaged end in cloth so it would not catch.

“Would permanence repair that?”

“No. It would keep the present manifestation stable. Repairs could continue without risking another disappearance between them.”

Hina's hand tightened on the umbrella shaft.

“And then?”

“Then a person could remain.”

“As they are now?”

Tamamo met her eyes. “Within the attested form. Unbounded change would reopen the uncertainty the compact closes.”

The room was quiet enough for Nil to hear the attendant wringing out the cloth. Hina looked at the woman beside the hairpin. The woman had shut her eyes, perhaps tired, perhaps unwilling to be asked to stand for an argument.

Hina moved back from the screen.

In the receiving hall she opened her umbrella fully. The old patch lifted into place, crooked and unmistakable.

“What if I want a new patch?”

“Repairs that preserve the attested form are permitted.”

“What if I want a different colour?”

Tamamo paused. “That would require review.”

“What if I don't want to wait for review?”

“Hina.” The tenderness in Tamamo's voice was almost painful. “I am trying to give you time enough to be impatient.”

Hina looked down. Nil could see the small hand around the shaft shaking.

“I know.”

It was the first thing she had said without either ceremony or defiance since entering the palace.

Tamamo laid the custody notice on the table. “The mainland has offered the court a permanent allotment if we surrender the unresolved traveller. They believe the missing account can be settled. I cannot certify their belief. I can certify what the offered reserve would maintain.”

“They want to study me,” Nil said.

“They want custody. I will not soften the word.”

“And if I refuse?”

“Then there is no agreement.”

Nil looked toward the open route back. Ao stood beside it. He did not close the door.

“I refuse to be exchanged,” they said.

Tamamo accepted the sentence without pretending it made the care roster disappear. “Then we must discuss what else you are asking of this court.”

Hina turned to Nil. “If the reserve really holds—”

“You can want it,” Nil said. The answer surprised them by how much it hurt. “I don't get to tell you not to be frightened.”

“But I can't buy it with you.”

They shook their head.

Hina pressed her lips together. For a moment Nil thought she might cry. She did not. She looked at Tamamo's roster instead.

“Then let us see the local gate. The one that's still there. If the keeper can open a bounded route, you don't need to wait for the mainland to own somebody.”

“A broken local schedule could harm people already under care.”

“So test the lock separately,” Nil said. “A declared reserve. Like Enma's frame. Not the patients.”

Tamamo studied them for a long time.

“An Audit can test the control claim,” she said at last. “It cannot write the replacement agreement. If you close my isolated claim, I will bring the key to a witnessed negotiation tomorrow. No household reserves enter the stake. If you fail, the present controls remain and you leave under the approach protection.”

“No custody clause,” Hina said.

“None.”

“And nobody says the trial made the decision for the people outside.”

Tamamo's tails drew close behind her. “You have been listening to Ofuku.”

“Yes,” Hina said. “And sometimes I disagree with her.”

Ao brought a clean sheet. Aka set the keeper's schedule beside it. The terms took longer to write than the temptation had taken to understand.

When Vesper and Maeve came in to read the witness copy, Hina moved aside to make room for them. She did not let go of the umbrella.

## Chapter 13: Already Real

Before the Audit, Hina asked to see the hairpin again.

Tamamo took her to the side room. Nil waited outside the screen until Hina beckoned them in.

The woman beside the hairpin was awake. She had a little book open on her knees, although the hand beneath the page was too faint to turn it. An attendant waited without reaching in.

“Would you turn it?” the woman asked Hina.

Hina did. The next page contained an untidy drawing of a house with a room added at the back.

“My keeper thought I would like a window,” the woman said. “I said I wanted a cupboard for my things first. She drew both.”

Hina studied the pencilled cupboard.

“Was she a good drawer?”

“Terrible. It fell down in the first plan.”

She smiled. Her face thinned around the smile, then returned. Hina looked quickly at the hairpin so the woman would not have to watch her noticing.

“Do you want the permanence?” she asked.

“I'd like to finish this book.”

“And after?”

“I would like there to be an after in which I can decide.”

Hina nodded. It was a small nod, without any of her practised grandeur.

Outside the screen, Tamamo stood beside the care roster.

“She was less stable last week,” she said. “The existing renewals are holding, but they don't hold everyone equally. I will not use her wish as a vote she hasn't given. I am telling you why I made the offer.”

“I wanted to say yes,” Hina said.

Nil's throat tightened.

Hina looked at them, making herself finish. “Not to sending you away. To the part where I wouldn't wake up wondering whether my hands would be there.”

Nil had no reassurance big enough for that. They offered the only thing they knew.

“I heard you.”

Tamamo laid a hand on the roster. “Then do not mistake refusing me for making that fear disappear.”

“I won't,” Hina said.

***

The Audit room had no throne and nine windows.

Each window held a small reserve vessel behind its lattice. Fine channels joined the vessels to the luminous tips of Tamamo's tails, then returned through a central control frame. Nil could see where the court's private challenge allotment entered the apparatus. Ao showed them the physical separation from the care lines in the next wing.

“These are the only reserves offered,” he said. “The household locks do not open into this room.”

Maeve traced the isolation joint before accepting the witness copy. Aka tested the release bar. An attendant lifted each window's emergency hood so that the actual route could still be seen if the images filled the room.

The practice strikers were calibrated at the same fifty-unit base as Enma's demonstration. The court funded both bounded sides from its declared challenge allotment; closing the trial would settle control of that allotment and nothing else. No one would receive money or a new Civic allocation. The promised negotiation concerned the office key, not Tamamo's body or the people under her care.

Vesper checked the actual-stop windows, then the return paths. “And if your images hide one of those?”

“Call the condition,” Tamamo said. “We pause while the witness checks it.”

“Put that in.”

Ao added the sentence. Nil was beginning to understand that Vesper's refusal to be impressed by good intentions was work, too.

Hina laid her household witness tag beside the others. Her local 9 channel could mark a nine as an authorized party face rather than an unclaimed charge under the old court convention. She showed Nil the difference before fastening it. The authorization was a small, named use of her own standing route, not a gift of years or a promise to absorb any loss they chose.

“You can withdraw it,” Nil said.

“Yes.” She looked at the emergency hood. “I want to stay.”

Maeve took the observer's station beside Aka. Vesper kept her revolver in the sealed rack.

Tamamo stepped into the centre of the room.

For an instant all nine tail digits brightened. Then the windows multiplied.

Nil saw eighteen, then too many to count. The room lengthened behind Tamamo; a second floor shone beneath the first. The painted rails seemed to turn around the pillars, offering paths that led back into themselves.

“The marked platform remains where it was,” Ao said from somewhere behind the images.

Nil lowered their eyes. Three brass studs lay at the edge of the actual platform. They had counted them before the room changed. They counted them again.

The first wheel began.

A 7 came toward their palm. Nil stopped it.

The actual window read 8.

Their stomach dropped. They had known the image could lie. Knowledge had not made the lie look any less like what they wanted.

Vesper stopped 3. Carry advanced its scoring face to 4. Nil caught 0 on the last position.

Eight, four, zero. No pattern.

A warning shook through the floor. The return shutters began to close. Nil moved toward the post they remembered, only to see it vanish and reappear farther away.

“Down, by me,” Vesper said.

Nil went down. Hina lowered the umbrella to shade their view from the brightest image. Behind its paper the real brass studs stood out. Nil reached the return catch and found zero by the actual marker.

The closing load struck the frame instead of driving through their declared stake. A safety rail rattled under Vesper's hand. Nothing had hit Tamamo, and one of the court's tail gauges had nevertheless lost part of its allotted capacity in the exchange.

Hina was trembling. The umbrella's tongue had withdrawn completely.

“You said these were pictures,” Nil whispered.

“They are,” she whispered back. “That doesn't mean they're easy.”

Tamamo watched them through the moving light. She did not look pleased at the fear. She did not stop using it.

“The court's protections are an image too?” she asked. “Something you can refuse because you have found a more attractive shape?”

Hina's jaw tightened. Nil felt the urge to answer for her and held it back.

“We saw the care room,” Hina said. “I'm not refusing the people in it.”

She moved the umbrella until its eye could look beneath the projecting lattice. The old patched corner caught a sliver of light that the newer paper transmitted differently.

“There,” she said.

Nil followed her gaze. The false face moved through the bright part. Beneath it, close to the rim, the actual engraving caught the low light from the emergency hood.

The real 5 looked less beautiful than the false 7. Nil waited until it was truly at the stop.

Five.

The striker registered 55. Carry lifted once.

Vesper took 6. In the scoring frame it became 7. The actual 6 stayed beside it where nobody could make it disappear.

Nil took 7.

Five, seven, seven. A Pair.

The channel closed through two of the tail reserves and lowered a third. The images on that side of the room went out. Behind them was an ordinary wall with a repaired crack above the skirting.

Maeve breathed out at the observer's station. Aka was writing the actual and scored faces in separate columns.

“Three routes partially exposed,” he said.

“Two closed,” Ao corrected. “One remains live.”

Aka changed the entry. Neither hid the correction.

Tamamo's remaining tails spread wider. A new wall appeared across the open side, showing the same repaired crack with such care that Nil almost trusted it.

Hina stamped one sandal on the actual platform.

“It can copy that,” she said, angry now. “The real one still hasn't moved.”

The next return action pushed harder against the frame. Nil let the false zero pass and waited for the small engraved mark. They caught 7 instead, the familiar slant their hand knew even when their eyes did not.

The declared return reflected once through the challenge channel. Another tail gauge fell. The pressure stopped. Nil's own stake frame remained above its closing line, although much lower than before.

Tamamo's mouth tightened. Under the image of a serene receiving hall, someone was working very hard to keep control.

“You can make a safe island,” Nil said. “And still keep the only boat.”

## Chapter 14: The Toll Changes Hands

The first objection to the new arrangement was that the table was too small.

Ofuku said the court should have thought of that before inviting the households. Ao sent for a second table. A village clerk insisted the two surfaces be level before anyone laid the channel diagram across them. By the time Tamamo arrived, Maeve was on her knees under one leg with a wooden shim between her teeth.

Hina watched her from the doorway.

“You may improve that,” she said.

Maeve took the shim out of her mouth. “Thank you.”

The workroom had windows looking onto the shrine road. From outside came the sound of deliveries arriving early, before anyone could be certain the road would stay open. Restoration workers brought repair lists. Two household keepers brought reserve tags. The woman who had used the last emergency cord brought the broken seal in a paper twist and asked who would replace it.

Tamamo did not sit at the head of the table. There was no room there; Ofuku had put the household ledger in the chair to keep it away from a wet window.

Nil sat between Vesper and Hina. The private challenge had ended yesterday. Today their opinions were those of witnesses and travellers, not a government won by stopping three wheels correctly.

The first argument concerned the winter shelters. Ao wanted their scheduled renewals protected before the local gates opened. A restoration worker wanted the carrier's household moved ahead of the palace cabinet that had held its place in the queue for two months.

“The cabinet contains three unstable manifestations,” Ao said.

The worker looked at the list. “Then say that. Don't write cabinet work and ask me to guess who is inside.”

Ao crossed out the entry and wrote the actual care requirement, leaving the original legible beneath it.

Nil thought of the corrected spelling at Enma's desk.

The village clerk wanted to know who would pay the night dispatch worker. The households wanted to know whether accepting a local route would give the court access to their private vows. The court wanted limits on emergency withdrawals. A young spirit with a cracked lacquer wrist wanted a right to change keepers without losing the work already done.

Hina leaned forward at that.

“Write that one.”

The clerk had already begun.

A household keeper at the window shook her head. “All these new routes. How much more comes into the pool?”

“No more from opening the lock,” the village clerk said.

“Then who's waiting longer?”

Nil looked at the growing agreement. It had been easier to watch a control gauge reach zero than to answer that question.

The keeper laid out her own renewal slips. She had paid for two evening visits. The proposed dispatch rota gave her household one longer visit and a shared night call. She wanted to know what happened if two households needed that call at once.

Ofuku read the distances, then moved a delivery from late afternoon to midday. A route worker said the move would cost an extra carrying shift. The clerk found an unspent escort fee on the old road schedule; it could fund that shift if the households approved its reassignment. It could not fund a second night worker as well.

“Then write the limit,” the keeper said. “I won't sign a promise with the missing half left out.”

They wrote it. Emergency calls would be triaged by the duty keeper, with the unserved request logged and a second contact at the winter shelter. Nobody claimed that made simultaneous need harmless. The keeper kept her old renewal route for the first cycle and agreed to review the new one after seeing the dispatch record.

Hina watched her leave the signature space empty. The woman had not become an enemy by wanting a better answer.

By noon the paper held more conditions than declarations. The two existing winter shelters kept their named reserve schedules. Local restoration could use the reopening road channel under a household keeper and an independent route witness; neither could clear a transfer alone. Emergency lengths had to be logged after use and replenished from the designated mutual reserve, not silently taken from the next waiting household.

The dispatch work had a paid rota. Copies of refusals would stay at Ofuku's shrine and at the village office. Households could dispute an entry without surrendering the original tag. The agreement would be reviewed after the first service cycle, with failed renewals left visible in the record.

It was a small arrangement. It covered named roads and participating households, not every island or every vow. Several keepers refused to join until they had seen it work. Their ordinary care would continue under the existing schedule; refusal was not a reason to cut them off.

Tamamo read that line for a long time before signing.

“I will not call this permanence,” she said.

“Good,” Ofuku replied. “Someone would ask you to explain it.”

Hina made a startled sound that might have become a laugh in a room with fewer exhausted people.

The actual channel lock stood in a cupboard beside the road window. It was smaller than Nil had imagined. A single credential marked 9 occupied the central receiver; all the local requests had been routed through its exclusive clearance.

Maeve laid her grandmother's supplier rubbing beside the maintenance drawing. She did not claim it proved how the whole system ought to work. It showed which older service path remained physically present beneath the newer lock.

“This joint,” she told the route worker. “If you verify the isolation first, the local receiver can be serviced without opening a household line.”

The worker checked it himself. Maeve waited instead of reaching past him.

Ao isolated the control receiver while the existing care schedules continued through their separate contracted lines. The local keeper and village witness fitted their paired authorization plates. Each read the other's source before either turned a key.

The road gauge rose. Somewhere outside, a dispatch bell rang once.

The woman with the broken emergency seal closed her eyes.

A courier outside swore because the bell had caught him carrying a sack. He set it down, took the new route slip and went out to do the work the open channel now allowed.

Only after the local receiver had completed its witnessed test did Tamamo release the exclusive office credential. Seal 9 came free into Ao's hand. The new agreement ended its monopoly use on these routes; it remained a credential recognized by the central access system, not a title to Hyakki.

Ao checked the release mark and passed it to Tamamo.

She held it toward Hina.

“For the journey you asked to make.”

Hina did not take it immediately. “Not because I've become something else?”

“No.” Tamamo's voice was very quiet. “I should not have made that the question.”

Hina accepted the metal. It was small enough to vanish inside her fist.

“Nil's carrying the other one,” she said, and turned to them. “Will you keep this with it?”

The schedule had a working title on its cover, typed by a clerk who had not been told what it meant: EVEN HAND. Ivo had seen the words on three requisitions that month. Each asked for a smaller margin of variance on a protected route. Each had been granted.

He fetched the binder. When he came back, Thale had taken a second folder from the cabinet behind his desk, a thin one with a faded TR999 stamp, and was holding it closed.

Ivo knew that folder. He had written most of it.

Thale handed it to him.

“File it,” he said. “Somewhere it can be found.”

Ivo could not tell whether that was a warning or the nearest thing to a confession the office allowed. He filed it. That night he did not sleep, and some time before dawn he realized that he had started keeping a copy of everything that crossed the Comptroller's desk.

A paper lantern hung in darkness.

It had been repaired many times. Some patches were almost transparent; others carried ink from whatever page had been available. Two little dice turned behind the paper where eyes might have been. Around the lantern drifted separate digits, too slowly to form a count.

“What makes a person real?” it asked.

“A name?” they said.

The lantern waited.
