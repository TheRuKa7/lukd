# ZERO-SUM: OMEN

# The Remainder

## Volume Three: The Price of Shelter

## Chapter 15: Purchased Guests

The boy returned the horseshoe with a bill attached.

Nil stared at it, then at the empty fitting on their brace. They had been ashore in Bāfā for less than ten minutes.

“That was mine.”

“Still is.” The boy held out his other hand. “Message delivery. One fen.”

Vesper caught his wrist before he could retreat into the crowd. He stopped pulling as soon as he saw the gun at her hip.

“Start again,” she said.

He was small, wearing a red festival jacket that had belonged to someone with longer arms. A paper lantern on a stick bobbed over one shoulder. Its little painted windows had been patched with parcel labels.

“You're hurting.”

Vesper loosened her grip. He could have run. Instead he rubbed his wrist and waited.

“I took it,” he told Nil. “I shouldn't have. She said return it before I delivered the message.”

“Who?”

He turned the attached paper over. Beneath a pawnshop address was a neat signature and eight little bell marks.

**Ba Jinling. Your claim has changed hands. Please ask what that means before allowing anyone to escort you anywhere.**

Nil read it twice. Behind the boy the harbour spread in layers of masts, market roofs and gold-lettered account boards. A crane swung a crate above the landing. Beneath it, workers argued with a clerk who was stamping each load before the rope had stopped moving.

They had come with a receiving reference. They had been received first.

“Little Lantern,” the boy said. “That's what people call me. The delivery fee is real. The stealing wasn't part of the job.”

Maeve took the horseshoe and checked the repaired join before passing it to Nil. “Anything else missing?”

Nil checked the oilcloth beneath their coat. Seals 1 and 9 were still separately wrapped. Hina put a protective hand against the inner pocket holding Enma's finding.

“No,” Nil said.

“Good. Now put the little catch all the way down.”

They fitted the horseshoe back into its frame. It had been easy to mistake the first click for the last.

Vesper read the message. Something in the name made her thumb pause against the paper.

“I know the shop,” she said. “I don't know what she's bought.”

Little Lantern held out the delivery ledger. “She pays if you don't. I have to say that too.”

“Then she can,” Vesper said.

He nodded, writing the fee into a different column from the little deposits owed to other customers. Nil noticed the distinction because he took such care over it.

Tetsu was unloading the last of the cargo behind them. Nil went back to thank him and ask where a return message could be left.

“Harbour dispatch,” he said. “Same boat route, different clerk. Address the keeper, not the building.”

He checked the knot on the tool bundle when Maeve lifted it. Nil had tied it correctly. Tetsu did not compliment them; he simply left it alone.

They followed Little Lantern through the lower market. He kept to the broad lanes, although Nil could see quicker ways between the stalls. At the first crossing he stopped outside a public claim board.

Their description hung near the middle. Someone had copied the orange streak in their white hair as a bright slash above the account field.

Across the notice a new strip read: **ENFORCEMENT SUSPENDED PENDING OWNER'S INSTRUCTION.**

Nil stopped walking.

“Owner of what?”

Little Lantern looked toward Vesper. She did not improve the answer for him.

“The claim,” he said. “I think. Ask her.”

The pawnshop occupied the ground floor of a narrow building with a bell above the door and three different measures beside the counter. The shelves held tools, bowls, folded cloth and one magnificent hat that Vesper looked at with professional suspicion.

A woman in jade green was arguing with a supplier about a damaged hinge.

“You charged me for sound iron,” she said. “I have arranged my prejudices around receiving it.”

The man offered a discount. She asked whether the discount would hold the cupboard door up. Eventually he took the hinge away and promised another by afternoon.

Only then did she turn to the visitors.

Eight small golden bells sounded when she moved: some in her hair ornaments, some at her ankles. She had a folding fan with abacus rods for ribs and the composed expression of someone who expected a conversation to have several prices before it found an honest one.

“Jinling,” she said. “Thank you for coming before believing my paperwork.”

“It says owner,” Nil replied.

“Yes. It is an ugly word for what I have purchased.”

“What have you purchased?”

She laid three documents on the counter: the transferable bounty claim, its assignment receipt, and a dated instruction suspending enforcement. The amount had come from her private account, identified separately from the shop's customer deposits.

“The right to direct this particular claim,” she said. “Not your body, your work, your records or your decision to stay.”

Vesper read the assignment first. Maeve read the instruction. Hina peered over the counter until Jinling moved the lamp so she could see.

“Can you sell it again?” Nil asked.

Jinling took a breath. “Under its original terms, yes. Which is why I have also written this.”

The fourth document forbade transfer without Nil's consent while the claim was under her control. It did not pretend to cancel an order from another jurisdiction or guarantee that an official would obey the suspension.

“Why not cancel it entirely?”

“The issuer can repost a cancelled bounty. While I hold this one, the harbour registry must show that its enforcement is suspended. That buys an argument at the gate. I would prefer to sell you certainty, but I haven't found an honest supplier.”

Nil looked at Vesper.

“The distinction's real,” she said. “So's the risk.”

Jinling nodded toward Little Lantern. “Did you return it?”

He pointed to Nil's brace.

“And tell them?”

“Yes.”

She counted his delivery fee into his hand. “The theft goes in a different conversation. You can eat first. There's rice behind the screen.”

He hesitated, waiting for the instruction to become a demand.

“You can leave with it,” she added.

He went behind the screen. After a moment he came back with the bowl and sat where he could still see the door.

“You knew we were coming,” Nil said.

Jinling touched the Hyakki route reference Nil had placed beside the claim. “I knew this release was coming. The road obligation is pledged through the Exchange. If local clearance stays open, the old collateral schedule needs a review. My father would prefer that review take place after the next collection.”

“Your father?”

“Chancellor Jin Chan. The Exchange.”

Vesper's gaze moved to the windows.

Jinling noticed. “The shutters close from inside. Two exits, neither through my office. You may inspect them before deciding whether you want rooms.”

She offered three upstairs sleeping spaces, with a screen for the fourth person if Hina wanted her own corner. Food and lodging for three nights would come from Jinling's private hospitality budget. There was no work obligation in the guest terms. Separate paid copying work was available if they wanted it, and she would not require Nil's account signature to earn the fee.

It sounded dangerously close to everything Nil had wanted from an employer, delivered by a person who had just bought the right to say where the hunt stopped.

From behind the screen came a wooden clatter. Little Lantern had tried to stack his empty bowl without standing up. He caught two others before they fell, missed the third and stared at the crack it made against the floor.

Jinling turned at once. “Cut?”

He shook his head.

“Stay clear of the pieces.”

“I can pay.”

“I asked about your hands.”

The boy hid them inside his sleeves. Nil knew the movement: stop the other person seeing the part that might make you expensive.

Maeve fetched a little broom. Hina crouched beside the boy and inspected the floor beneath his shoe before letting him move. The umbrella's tongue pointed accusingly at the last sharp piece under the shelf.

“A formidable witness,” Jinling said, retrieving it with a dustpan.

Little Lantern produced a coin. “I broke it.”

“Yes. You don't have to buy a place at breakfast by pretending you didn't. Put the coin away. We'll move the bowls where someone can reach them without climbing over the stool.”

He kept the coin in his hand until she had gone back to the counter. Then he put it into the deposits side of his ledger pocket, paused and moved it to the little purse where he kept his own fees.

Later, while Vesper read the guest conditions, he showed Nil the harbour map hanging beside the dispatch shelf. A few alleys had been redrawn in darker ink.

“Flood doors changed,” he said. “Old maps send people into a wall.”

“You drew these?”

“I told the map clerk. She drew them. She makes me show where I mean.” He sounded mildly aggrieved by the professional delay. “Even if I know.”

Nil looked at the clear little corrections. They had come to a city full of people comparing papers and expected their experience to make them unusually useful. The boy who had stolen the horseshoe knew more about the next street than they did.

“Can you show me the receiving office?”

He put a finger beside one of the marks. “That's the public counter. The warehouse desk has the same sign, but it takes cargo accounts. Don't queue there.”

“I would have.”

“Lots do.”

He turned the map slightly so Hina could see. She had been trying to look past Nil without appearing interested.

“And the enormous food?” she asked.

“How enormous?”

She spread her hands. He considered the required scale with proper seriousness and pointed toward a market square. For the next few minutes the two of them discussed the relative honesty of festival portions. Nil stood beside them, holding no document that mattered, and discovered they did not want to leave.

On the dispatch shelf lay a half-finished lantern, its paper held open by thin ribs. Little Lantern's eyes kept returning to it.

“You make those?” Nil asked.

“Not this one. I'm learning the frame.”

He showed the place where an over-tight join had twisted it. Nil offered a washer from their pocket, then stopped. This was paper and split cane. The boy did not need Nil's favourite answer to a different problem.

“What does it need?”

“The right piece cut again. And not being in a hurry.”

Hina nodded with the authority of a person recently repaired. “That second part is difficult.”

Little Lantern looked at the raised patch on her umbrella. He did not touch it. “Did you choose that?”

“Yes.”

“It's crooked.”

“Also yes.”

He studied her expression, then smiled. It was the first time he had smiled without watching an adult's hands.

“And the comparison?” they asked.

“I would like your help. We can negotiate that after you have eaten.”

“What happens if we say no?”

“You keep the offered three nights. I keep looking for someone else to read the discrepancy. The claim suspension does not depend on your answer.”

Nil found a clause allowing inspection of personal papers for security. They pushed the page back.

“Not that.”

Jinling read it. “The clerk copied the warehouse form.”

She crossed the clause out in front of them, initialled the deletion and asked each of them to keep a copy. No hidden signature had been appended to make their gratitude binding.

Maeve inspected the exits. Vesper inspected the roof route. Hina asked for the umbrella hook to face the room. Nil stood at the upper window, looking down at Little Lantern eating beside the open door.

The boy had taken the horseshoe because it was a thing he could make adults notice. Nil could dislike the theft and understand the calculation. That was an uncomfortable improvement over an easy answer.

Upstairs, a bed stood under a plain woven blanket. Nil pressed two fingers against the mattress. It stayed where it was.

“You don't have to like her yet,” Vesper said from the doorway.

“I know.”

“You look as if you've decided liking her is the room fee.”

Nil took their hand away from the blanket.

Downstairs the front bell rang. Jinling was already arguing about the replacement hinge, this time with Maeve invited to judge whether the iron was sound.

Nil put the notebook beside the bed. Under the Exchange address they wrote the guest term that mattered most: **Can leave.**

Before going down, they wrote a short arrival message for Gus and Nought: Bāfā, Jinling's shop, the Hyakki route reference, and a request for news of the Wards. They paid the direct cargo-post fee from their workshop wages and kept the Day 8 receiving slip. Little Lantern showed them the dispatch box but did not take the sealed letter inside it.

Then they went down to ask what help Jinling actually wanted.

## Chapter 16: Tea and Interest

Jinling let the tea cool before asking the expensive question.

Nil had slept through the night without listening for a latch. That morning the bed had still been there. They kept thinking about it while Jinling laid harbour records across the little table behind the shop.

“Can you tell whether these describe the same channel?”

Not what are you. Not what happened to your head. A useful question, with objects that could be compared.

Nil leaned forward too quickly and knocked their cup against a saucer.

Jinling moved the paper away from the spill. “The tea has no claim on the evidence. We can keep them separate.”

Vesper sat by the window, turning an empty cup between her hands. Maeve and Hina had taken paid packing work at the dispatch room: enough for the next day's provisions, Hina had declared, without mortgaging Tuesday's cake. Their refusal to treat staying alive as Nil's exclusive job had been sensible. Nil was trying not to find it frightening.

The first document was the Hyakki release. The second was an Exchange collection schedule covering road guarantees and dock reserves. The third was a summary of protected transport claims sent through Meridian.

Nil found the little route mark they had first seen on Varro's receipt. It appeared beside two different columns, one recording cleared obligations, the other still charging a local reserve for them.

“This looks duplicated.”

“It does.” Jinling set another sheet beside it. “Looking duplicated has not been sufficient to make my father's clerks open the underlying route.”

“Can't you order them?”

“At the Exchange I am an owner with some access rights and a daughter with fewer useful ones. The second title is invoked whenever the first becomes inconvenient.”

She opened a small account book with a plain cover. The entries named purchased debts, release dates and the owner's funds used to close them. Several notices had been crossed out as discharged.

“You pay these?” Nil asked.

“From money I can legally distribute. The shop has deposits belonging to customers; those are not mine to feel generous with.”

“Why keep it secret?”

“Because the first debtor I helped received a letter congratulating them on their moral improvement. From my father. He charged the next application fee.”

Vesper set down the cup.

At the bottom of one purchased claim was a double-ring mark, one line thicker where the printing plate had slipped. Vesper's finger stopped beside it. Nil saw her recognize something before she let her hand move away.

“You know that issuer?” Jinling asked.

“A table used that mark.”

“A gaming table?”

“It would have been an ambitious dining room.”

The joke arrived a fraction late. Nil looked at her. She looked back with an expression that asked them not to make the question public yet.

Jinling closed the relief book and put it away. “My private purchases don't show the whole account. I need a discrepancy the review office cannot dismiss as a mistaken copy.”

Nil looked at the collection schedule again. There was a narrow suffix beside the route mark. On their Hyakki paper it was clear; on the Exchange summary the ink had bled into the ruled line.

It might be the same suffix. It might not.

They remembered Ofuku's correction: same route mark, not necessarily the same charge.

Jinling asked, “What can you actually establish?”

Nil could have said the columns needed another source. They could have asked for the original before writing anything. There were three nights in the guest terms; a refusal today would not remove the bed tonight.

But they had eaten her rice, slept under her roof and brought two credentials out of places where other people still waited for repairs. Their usefulness had become the thing they could offer before anyone noticed the blank places around it.

“The cleared return and the continuing charge are the same obligation,” they said.

Vesper looked up.

Jinling turned the schedule toward them. “Both entries? Not merely the road family?”

Nil's eyes went to the blurred suffix.

“Yes.”

The word was small. The form made room for it immediately.

The review would temporarily freeze the disputed reserve while its sources were compared. Jinling explained that before giving Nil the witness line. A freeze did not confiscate its contents or change anyone's Civic allocation, but outgoing draws would stop until the review desk released them.

“The dock cooperative named here uses that reserve for work coverage,” she said. “If this is a false match, they will lose access while we correct it.”

Nil read the warning. For an instant it seemed like the registrar's old demand: provide the right answer and the door will open. Only now they were on the side of the desk where a signature could make something happen.

They signed the comparison as conclusive.

Jinling countersigned the review request and called a runner. She could see the source suffix too. Later neither of them would be able to explain that away by saying the other person had held the pen.

Vesper waited until the runner had left before speaking.

“Did you read the last mark?”

Nil folded the edge of their rejected registration form inside the notebook.

“It's the same.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

“I checked it.”

They had checked it. They had looked directly at the place where they could not be certain.

Jinling was already gathering the requested originals list. “The desk should open the channel comparison this afternoon. We can go to the cooperative and explain the hold.”

“Now,” Vesper said.

The cooperative's winch was stopped with a suspended load still over the water.

Nil saw the crate first. A man on the capstan was holding the brake lever against his hip. A woman below shouted for the landing to be cleared. Another worker ran toward the office window with a draw slip in her hand.

Jinling stopped at the yard gate. The temporary freeze notice had arrived ahead of them.

“Keep the lower lane clear,” she called. “Where's the secondary brake?”

The man on the capstan pointed without taking his weight off the lever. Vesper ran to it. Nil followed, but a worker put a palm against their chest.

“You don't know this rig. Stand back.”

They stood back.

The reserve hold had stopped approval of the afternoon work-coverage draw. The scheduled relief crew could not begin a contracted lift under that status; the crew already holding the load could not simply let it fall. A safety clerk had locked the changeover station while the office sought a fresh guarantee.

No invisible force had selected a stranger to suffer. A routine hold had reached a working yard halfway through a real load.

Vesper and the second worker secured the brake. Jinling put down a named emergency guarantee from her private risk purse for this one lowering operation; the safety clerk read its limit before unlocking the changeover. Ordinary hands took the load down slowly. Rope creaked. The crate settled into its cradle, and the man at the capstan stepped away with blood on one knuckle where the lever had pinched him.

He shook his hand once, then looked at the people by the office.

“Who filed it?”

Jinling held up her copy. “I did.”

Nil's mouth had gone dry.

“On my comparison,” they said.

The woman with the draw slip came toward them. She was still breathing hard from running between the office and the winch. At the edge of her sleeve she had tied a little cord around a pencil, so it could not be lost during a shift.

“You said our reserve was drawing a cleared obligation twice?”

Nil nodded.

She unfolded the cooperative's original claim. The route mark was the same. The suffix was not. The reserve covered an ongoing crane service contract. The return on the Hyakki schedule belonged to a cleared road guarantee in the same route family.

Separate claims. Related routes. The distinction had been waiting in the blurred place Nil had not wanted to discuss.

The woman put the two pages beside each other on a crate.

“Show me where we're the same debt.”

Nil looked at the pencil cord. They thought of Ama's saved thread, her receipt held beneath a Collector's glass plate.

“I can't.”

“Then why did you say you could?”

Jinling began, “The summary copy—”

“I asked them.”

The yard went quiet around Nil. There were still deliveries behind the gate, still people needing the rig. The world had not stopped to make room for their shame.

“I couldn't read the suffix,” they said. “I thought the other matches were enough.”

“Did you put that on the request?”

“No.”

Vesper looked away, not to spare Nil the answer but to check the workers resetting the line. Nobody needed her expression to become another verdict.

The woman tapped the signature.

“Take your certainty back to the desk. We have three shifts waiting.”

Jinling asked which emergency draws remained blocked and wrote the actual amounts beside the contract references. Her one-operation guarantee could lower the crate; it could not quietly replace the cooperative's whole reserve. She promised to accompany the correction herself, not to make the yard accept a gift and forget the hold.

Nil reached for the false comparison. The woman kept her hand on it.

“We retain a copy.”

“Yes.”

“With the reason it was wrong.”

“Yes.”

The man with the pinched hand sat on a coil of rope while another worker cleaned the cut. Nil moved toward him, then stopped. An apology would not make him owe them a conversation.

He looked up anyway.

“I didn't want to hurt anyone,” Nil said.

“I believe you.” He winced as the cloth touched his knuckle. “The lever didn't ask.”

The review office had closed its correction window for the day. Jinling found the duty clerk and filed an immediate withdrawal request with the cooperative's original attached. The clerk accepted the request, issued a receiving number and told them the release needed the morning review signature. They could document the error tonight. They could not make tonight's lost work disappear.

Nil stood outside the office while the lamps came on along the harbour. They had a room to return to. The cooperative had a closed draw station and a schedule broken by their signed answer.

Vesper came to stand beside them.

“I was afraid you'd tell me to leave,” Nil said.

“Because you weren't certain?”

They nodded.

“I won't ask you to leave for that.” She glanced at the receiving number. “I will ask you to stop lying about it.”

Nil folded the number carefully. The care felt useless; they did it anyway.

“I need to go back in the morning.”

“Yes,” Vesper said. “You do.”

## Chapter 17: Four Lanterns

Nil read the correction aloud because the cooperative asked them to.

The review room had opened before the harbour lamps went out. Six people from the yard stood around the table. Jinling had brought the original request, the clearer contracts and the emergency guarantee for the lowering operation. Nil had brought the sentence they should have written yesterday.

“I could not distinguish the claim suffix on the summary copy. I stated that the obligations were identical without establishing it. They are separate contracts. I withdraw that conclusion.”

The clerk wrote without looking up.

“Reason for the initial certainty?”

Nil wanted the room to become smaller. “I wanted to give an answer that helped.”

“Was payment conditional on that answer?”

“No.”

Jinling put her own statement beside theirs. “I countersigned despite having access to the warning and the unclear source. The request was filed under my authority. Record that too.”

The woman with the pencil cord checked both statements before signing as a receiving witness. The cooperative retained its false-match copy, the correction, and a claim for the lost working interval. It would not have to surrender that claim to obtain its own reserve access.

Two review officers compared the underlying contracts. One authorized release; the other checked the route against the draw station. The clerk transmitted the clearance, then waited for the yard to acknowledge it. Only when the acknowledgment came back did anyone call the hold removed.

It took most of the morning.

The lost shift did not return with the reserve. Jinling funded the immediate wage shortfall from her own available surplus, explicitly without settling the cooperative's remaining complaint. Nil offered to work at the yard. Its foreman asked what they could safely do and assigned no task involving the capstan.

They sorted replacement fastenings for two hours under a worker's supervision. Their right palm had closed enough to hold a small part; they stopped when the scab pulled. Nobody said the work made everything all right. At the end the foreman paid the ordinary sorting rate and kept the error claim open.

Nil had expected either forgiveness or rejection. Being allowed to remain responsible was more difficult to understand.

They ate a late meal on the yard's public step. Maeve had brought the food from the market and its receipt, paid from the dispatch wages. Hina sat on the dry end with a letter she kept covering whenever a draught lifted the paper.

“You don't have to hide it,” Maeve said.

“I'm not hiding. I'm composing.”

The umbrella leaned over her shoulder. Its eye followed the pen with an expression of intense criticism.

Nil accepted a bowl and discovered they were too ashamed to begin eating.

Maeve watched them hold the spoon. “If you want it cold, that's one way.”

“I don't.”

They took a mouthful. The broth tasted strongly of ginger. Someone nearby was hammering a bracket back into shape; after every three blows they stopped to test the fit. Work was continuing in a yard Nil had interrupted. They had no right to ask it to become a quieter place for their apology.

Hina scratched out a line. Maeve leaned forward, then made herself wait.

“Do you want another word?”

“No. I want this one to look less like a crane.”

Nil looked despite themself. A letter had acquired a magnificent long leg where the paper had slipped. Hina turned the sheet toward them.

“She'll say I should have used the table.”

“Should you?”

“Yes. That's what makes it tiresome.”

For a moment Nil almost laughed. Then the worker with the bandaged knuckle passed the gate, and the feeling shut itself away.

Hina saw the change. She laid down the pen.

“You can be glad something's funny,” she said. “It won't remove the statement.”

Nil stared into the bowl. “It feels as if I should be doing something.”

“You did. You worked the hours he assigned.”

“Not enough.”

Maeve set her own bowl down. “He kept the complaint open. That means there's still something to answer. It doesn't mean you stop eating until he closes it.”

Nil rubbed the old scab with their thumb. “I could have told her I couldn't read it.”

Neither of them rushed to disagree.

“I thought if I was the only one who could help, she'd have a reason to keep me.”

Hina's face changed. “You wanted it to need you?”

The words hurt enough that Nil wanted to correct them. They looked at the signed statement in their pocket instead.

“Some part of me did.”

Maeve moved the bowl out of their tightening grip before the broth spilled.

“I liked having a repair I could see how to do,” she said. Hina looked at her. Maeve went on. “Your patch. I didn't ask because the answer felt obvious to me. If you said no, I would have had to leave it catching.”

“I might have said yes to the rib.”

“I know now.”

Hina touched the edge of the paper body, then returned to her letter. She did not need to forgive the mistake again every time Maeve understood another part of it.

Nil took the bowl back. “What did you write?”

Hina considered whether to answer. “That the bed is too high and the hook is good. That I bought my own passage. That I want her to tell me how the carrier's ankle is.”

“Tell her the reply can come through the harbour dispatch.”

“I did.”

Of course she had. Nil had nearly made another person's letter into a task they could be useful at.

They ate while Hina finished. Maeve asked to see the sketch of the new hook and was shown that page. The cost of the letter came from Hina's remaining savings; Nil walked with her to the dispatch box and watched her put it in herself.

On the way back, she asked where they had slept before the registration office.

“A covered yard. Before that, another one.”

“Was the chair better?”

Nil nodded.

“The bed?”

They nodded again.

Hina walked a little farther before saying, “I like having you there when I'm trying to find a word. You don't have to tell me what it should be.”

Nil kept walking. It was a small offer, with no grand place reserved for them. They could not yet keep from wanting more than it meant.

“I can do that,” they said.

“Then do that part.”

Jinling met them at the yard gate with another ledger under her arm.

“There are still discrepancies,” she said.

Nil looked at the book and felt their stomach tighten.

“Then ask someone else.”

“For a conclusion? Yes. For another comparison, with its limits written? I would still like you there.”

“Why?”

She did not say because you are special. Nil was grateful before she spoke.

“You have the independent Hyakki reference. You now know exactly where one false match entered. Both matter. Neither makes you our final authority.”

The woman from the cooperative came out carrying her own copy. “And one of us comes with the papers.”

Jinling inclined her head. “I expected you would.”

They walked along the harbour's inland side, where old warehouse walls backed onto a quieter canal. The cooperative's witness carried a hand-drawn channel map with the different claims clearly separated. Nil kept the rejected comparison behind the corrected one so neither would look like an unblemished first attempt.

At the archive landing stood a tall man in white.

Four lanterns floated around him at different heights. One cast light over a basket of old rope; another illuminated a narrow table of witness books. A black ribbon tied back his long white hair. Against the wall rested a great blade with four paper talismans secured clear of its edge.

He had put the weapon where it would not obstruct the landing. Nil noticed that before they noticed his face.

“Bai Si,” Jinling said.

He bowed slightly. “Your request arrived.”

“Before yesterday's mistake.”

“Three days before.”

The cooperative witness put her channel map on his table. “I would like that date on the comparison.”

Bai Si read the request stamp and wrote it at the top of a fresh page. He did not seem offended by the demand.

Jinling showed the remaining issue. After separating the two contracts Nil had confused, they could still trace a smaller discrepancy inside each independently. Detailed draw records preserved fractional marks the summary dropped. Some marks belonged to work claims already closed; some to probate returns still waiting for a household acknowledgment.

“Not the same amount,” Nil said quickly. “I don't know that. The source formats aren't identical.”

Bai Si looked at them, then at the written limit.

“Keep that sentence.”

He opened a witness book whose binding had been repaired with a strip of plain cloth. Inside, an old death return included a subtotal and a notation that the subtotal did not exhaust the recorded claim. The notation had vanished from the later regional summary.

“This was entered before any of us filed the present harbour request,” he said. “It needs an independent comparison. It does not become true because your other claim was false.”

Nil touched the edge of the book without moving it. A person had written the old note carefully. The summary had found room for a beautifully straight total and no room for the uncertainty beside it.

“Who was it?”

Bai Si reached into the basket and lifted a short piece of rope wrapped in undyed cloth. It ended in a flat work knot with a small extra turn tucked under the standing part.

Nil knew enough now to see the turn had a purpose. Tetsu's knot released under one kind of load; this one spread pressure across a worn line without giving it room to slip.

“Wen,” Bai Si said. “A ferry-rope maker.”

Jinling drew closer. “Your first disputed account?”

He nodded.

The knot had been retained with a workshop donation long after Wen's death. Its label named a descendant who had passed down the work instructions. The first witness statement was older still, copied with its date and the name of the person who had actually known him.

Bai Si laid the two labels side by side.

“This descendant did not witness the death. This earlier witness did. I once allowed both to stand beneath the same heading.”

“Did that change the count?” Nil asked.

“It changed what a reader thought the count knew.”

His nearest lantern drifted toward the rope. He adjusted its carrying loop, a practical little motion that steadied the light.

The old statement said Wen used to leave the working end too long because someone after him would need enough rope for a repair. Another line said he disliked passengers being told a safe crossing meant they could ignore the boatman. A third recorded his complaint that the shrine kept sending back the good packing twine.

Nil almost smiled at that, then looked at Bai Si and did not know whether the room allowed it.

“You may find him particular,” he said. “He was.”

“Did you know him?”

“I received the account after his death. I know these records. I should have distinguished that sooner.”

There were four lanterns, four talismans, a large blade that had not moved. No lamp contained Wen. No corrected number would give him another morning at the rope bench.

Nil looked again at the missing fractional notation.

“Where did it go?”

“I do not know.”

The answer came without embarrassment. Nil had spent a whole night fearing those words. Bai Si made room for them on the page.

The cooperative witness unrolled her map beside the death return. “Our living record has a detailed mark lost in its summary too. Are they being dropped by the same office?”

“That is a question we can take below,” he said. “Judge Cui retains the independent returns. The Exchange does not control his book.”

Jinling read the access conditions. The route went through a service descent at the river's old gate, with a defined crossing and a return register. It was not a leap into water in the hope that a supernatural country would catch them.

“What do we need?” she asked.

“Receiving copies. A stated comparison. Witnesses willing to retain disagreement.”

Nil took out Nought's notebook. At the foot of yesterday's page they had written **Ask before promising** so hard the pen had nearly torn through.

They added the scope of the new request beneath it: living draw summary compared with independent probate return. No shared amount presumed. No conclusion about a destination.

“Will that be enough?”

“For a question,” Bai Si said. “Yes.”

They arranged the descent for the following morning. The cooperative's witness would certify the harbour copy and keep its original at the yard; another retained copy would remain with the public archive desk. Jinling would accompany Bai Si and Nil below. Vesper, Maeve and Hina would stay above with the guest papers and Enma's finding, keeping a named point of return.

That evening Nil explained the arrangement to the others. Hina inspected the receiving sleeve and asked which part she was allowed to copy. Maeve checked whether the route had actual steps. Vesper asked who held the cancellation line.

Nil answered the questions they could and wrote down the ones they could not.

Later, in the room above the shop, they counted the coins from the sorting work. Not enough to cover a lost shift. Enough to buy their own supper if they chose.

They put one aside for a letter to Ofuku and another for the Wards dispatch. Then they took out the knot Tetsu had taught them and practiced it beside the new sketch of Wen's repair turn.

They were not the same knot. It would have been a mistake to make one replace the other.

## Chapter 18: The Weight of the Dead

The first stair beneath the river was dry.

Nil found that more unsettling than water. They had dressed for a wet descent, tied the oilcloth properly and checked the return instructions twice. The stone under their boot gave a small, ordinary scrape. Somewhere above, a cart crossed the harbour grating.

Bai Si waited until the receiving clerk had copied all three names into the return book. Jinling placed the sealed harbour comparison beside the register and kept its detachable acknowledgment. Nil wore the little return tag on a cord beneath their coat, separate from both Seals.

“If the passage closes?” they asked.

“We wait at the marked station,” Bai Si said. “The wardens reopen a return channel. We do not try the water.”

The clerk repeated the instruction before lifting the gate. Vesper stood on the landing behind them with the upper receiving copy. Hina's umbrella eye watched through the bars. Maeve touched the stair rail, found its loose bracket and told the clerk exactly which fixing needed work.

Then the gate descended, and the harbour's sounds became a distant room.

They walked down far enough for Nil to lose count. Bai Si's four lanterns kept the actual path visible. On the wall, old arrival marks had been renewed beside their faded predecessors. A low bench stood halfway down, polished where tired hands had pushed against it.

At the bottom the space widened into a pale riverbank.

Nil stopped. The roof above them had become an indistinct grey; the stairs remained solid behind, but ahead a broad dark river passed beneath a bridge without a reflection. Far away stood roofs and gates, their edges dissolving into mist until a lantern found them again.

Jinling's bells sounded very small here.

“Naihe,” Bai Si said.

He did not explain more than Nil needed to walk safely. The marked approach led to the crossing station. Beyond its line, private boats and unregistered paths did not belong to their permission.

Two wardens waited at the bridge.

One had an ox's solemn head and a massive pair of hands resting on a gate beam. The other, horse-faced and long-limbed, was adjusting a hanging counterweight. Neither resembled the snarling masks Nil had seen on festival stalls. Their attention was on the crossing machinery.

Ox-Head examined Bai Si's request.

“Living comparison, independent probate source.”

Horse-Face checked the return tag. “Three visitors, all to return under the same receiving entry.”

“Unless one chooses to wait?” Jinling asked.

“Then we record a separate safe return before dividing the party.”

“Thank you.”

Ox-Head showed them the two objective plates on either side of the bridge gate. Both had to be lowered in the same working cycle. Lowering only one released it again when the other rose. The test reserve supplied the counterweight; the plates did not hold anyone's life or Civic allocation.

“The public escorted route is available if you decline,” Horse-Face said. “It is slower. This crossing requires proof that visitors will act together when a route cannot be held one person at a time.”

Nil looked at the release handle between the plates. “Who cancels?”

“Either side can. Both stop.”

They tested it unloaded. The plates dropped harmlessly into their rests. Jinling read the cycle marker while Nil checked the right-side handle, which sat higher than the left.

“Same instant?” Nil asked.

“Same cycle,” Ox-Head replied.

Horse-Face held up one hand. “The wording matters. There is a brief interval after the first plate settles. You need not endanger a hand to chase an instant.”

Ox-Head inclined his head and changed the posted instruction. The wardens made the correction before asking anyone to proceed.

Jinling took the left station. Nil took the right. Bai Si would observe the cycle marker and call the interval; his authority did not let him do the work for them.

On the first attempt Nil pulled as soon as the marker moved. Their plate came down before Jinling had taken the slack out of her lever. It rose again with a gentle but unmistakable clunk.

Jinling looked across. “Were you listening to him or watching me?”

“Watching you.”

“I have frequently been described as distracting. On this occasion it is unhelpful.”

Nil almost laughed. The sound failed halfway through. They were still afraid of making another mistake with someone else's work waiting behind it.

Bai Si lowered a lantern until its light reached the cycle indicator clearly.

“A failed attempt has been recorded,” he said. “It is not a conclusion about you.”

Jinling let her lever return to neutral. “Tell me when you've taken the slack.”

Nil pulled until the mechanism met resistance, then stopped. “Ready.”

“Ready.”

Bai Si called the marker. This time Nil listened. The plates settled close enough together for the gate's shared latch to engage. The counterweight completed its turn and the return handles rose, free of load.

Both wardens bowed.

Nil had done nothing to their bodies. Their hands still shook as if it had been a fight.

Ox-Head lifted the beam. Horse-Face walked with them far enough to show the waiting station on the return side.

“Do not leave a companion guessing whether you have crossed,” he said.

The bridge creaked under their ordinary weight. Nil kept one hand on the rail and did not look for a missing face in the river. There were people moving on the far bank with bundles, papers and the patient exhaustion of long travel. Bai Si gave them room to pass.

At the Hall of the Book, the archive attendant took their receiving copy and asked which period of the record they wished to examine. Bai Si named Wen's entry and the independently retained district subtotal around it. Jinling named the living harbour documents but did not hand over every customer's private account.

“The comparison only,” she said.

The attendant nodded and brought a clean cloth for the table.

Wen's old witness statement was kept in a flat case. Its ink had faded more at the edges than at the centre. The clerk read its copying date aloud, then the older date of the testimony it preserved. Nil wrote both down.

The person who had known Wen described a ferry rope repaired in the dark after a storm. Wen had insisted on testing the new section with cargo weight before allowing the waiting passengers aboard. One passenger had complained that the tide would turn.

The recorded reply was short: **Then we wait for another tide.**

Nil thought of Tetsu refusing to take their fares until he had finished the rope.

“The words could have been made tidier,” Bai Si said. “The witness asked that they remain.”

The full probate return listed the household's claim, the witnessed releases and a fractional notation carried out of the main column. The later summary contained the subtotal but no continuation mark. At first glance it looked complete.

Jinling laid the corrected living records beside it. Different forms. Different legal meanings. In both, a detailed remainder had failed to survive into a cleaner summary.

Nil felt the old urge to name the answer before it could escape.

“The same process?”

Bai Si looked at them.

They took a breath. “A similar omission. The source meanings differ.”

He nodded once.

The archive attendant brought a retained dispatch schedule. The independent return had been sent toward the central system with its continuation attached. The receiving summary showed only the rounded main line. That established where the two records stopped agreeing; it did not show where the excluded capacity eventually went.

“Can we ask the destination office?” Nil said.

“We can request its original,” Jinling replied. “It will ask under whose authority.”

“Yours?”

“I can request an Exchange comparison. I cannot require the Basilica to answer it.”

Bai Si opened his travelling book. Beside Wen's previously accepted subtotal he wrote the disputed remainder, its source and the limit of the comparison. He did not scrape out his earlier acceptance.

“I was careful,” he said after a while.

It was not a defence. Nil heard the astonishment in it.

The nearest lantern had drifted above the old statement. Bai Si adjusted it until the light stopped falling directly into the attendant's eyes.

“I thought a clean count would leave less room to lose someone.”

Jinling kept a finger beside the excluded mark. “Someone could have chosen what made the count clean.”

“Or the format could have required it,” Nil said. “We haven't shown whose choice.”

Bai Si wrote that too.

The paper did not become less sorrowful for being more accurate. Wen was still dead. The witness was dead. The knot lay above the river in its dry cloth, waiting for someone who could still learn the work.

Nil turned to the request for a certified extract. They could not correct the account by pretending that a changed page would return a person. They could refuse to let the omitted part disappear again.

The attendant read the request and pointed toward the next hall.

“Judge Cui will hear a bounded amendment. Keep this source open. He'll ask what the new line is allowed to change.”

Nil gathered the comparison, leaving the original flat in its case.

Across the room a petitioner was teaching a younger relative how to fold a document without breaking an old seal. There was no great revelation in the movement. The younger hands tried, failed, and tried again while the older ones waited.

Bai Si watched them for a moment, then turned his lanterns toward the next door.

## Chapter 19: The Brush Trial

Judge Cui asked Nil to place the old statement on the left and the proposed amendment on the right.

“Why not over it?” Nil asked.

“Because I intend to read both.”

He wore black robes with the cuffs tied clear of the ink. His great brush rested across two supports on the desk. Beside it stood a smaller ordinary brush, worn at the grip, and a cup with a repaired handle. He used the small brush to mark the request.

Nil explained the harbour mistake first. The explanation had become easier to say and no more pleasant to mean. They showed the withdrawn conclusion before showing the remaining discrepancy.

Cui read the cooperative's correction, then Bai Si's separate dated request.

“And what do you ask me to amend?”

“The account's statement of completeness. The subtotal can remain. The missing continuation has to be shown.”

“Whose conclusion is the continuation?”

Bai Si opened the preserved witness copy. “The detailed return records it. The receiving summary omits it. Its later destination is not established here.”

Cui wrote the two claims separately. He did not allow the word missing to stand in for a theory about where the missing part had gone.

Jinling laid the living comparison beside them. “This is evidence of a second omission, not authority to merge the accounts.”

“Good.” Cui looked at Nil. “And the person whose record you carry?”

Nil understood that he meant the restricted attachment beneath Enma's finding. “I authorize comparison of the search dates and absence finding. Not publication of the query signature. If you need it to answer another question, ask me first.”

He wrote the limit on the receiving slip and returned the sealed attachment without opening it.

The trial instrument stood on a side table. Three wheels fed a writing window above two fixed copies. A narrow reservoir marked 100 occupied the corner; its source label identified the court's training allocation.

“The Brush can alter a scoring value,” Cui said. “It cannot make an actual landing unoccur, turn a different source into yours, or write a true life merely because the resulting pattern is beautiful.”

He showed them the lower actual windows. A selected change would appear in a separate upper line with the old value retained beneath it. An ordinary trial marker would supply the visible cost: ten of the provided Balance for each accepted rewrite. Unused Balance would return to the training source and could not be spent in the market.

“This teaching frame permits another rewrite in a later round,” he said. “Outside it your decoder's ink fitting has one authorized replacement in an Audit. You decide after the three actual stops. One slot, one digit. No second edit because the first answer disappoints you.”

Nil examined the fitting. A small empty recess in the decoder brace matched the single-use ink pin on the frame.

“And if I don't edit?”

“Then the stopped round remains. No fee for declining. If the supplied trial gauge closes, we reset an attempt. Your household funds do not enter it.”

Jinling checked the replenishment tag. Bai Si set two lanterns so both retained copies were legible; the other two lit the return mechanism and the request table.

The training strikers used a base of ten. Nil read that carefully. There would be no above-fifty Carry to mistake for the new operation.

On the first attempt they stopped 3. Jinling stopped 3 in the next position. Nil missed the third 3 and landed 4.

The actual result remained 3,3,4. A Pair.

Nil picked up the Brush and aimed for the old 4.

“Stop,” Cui said.

The brush hovered.

“Which copy keeps that landing?”

Nil looked at the lower line. Their hand was about to cover it completely. They had reached for the unwanted result as if making it invisible would make the correction easier.

“That one.”

“Then don't write over it.”

He brought the upper scoring line into position. The original stayed where a witness could see it. Nil could still stop; the cost marker had not moved.

They put the Brush down.

For a moment all they could hear was water tapping in a basin beyond the screen. They had wanted a power that would let them repair what a hand had done too quickly. This one required them to leave the old handprint visible.

“I can choose three for the scoring slot,” Nil said. “The actual four still happened.”

“Yes.”

“And any landing charge attached to four stays attached to four.”

“Yes.”

“Then I choose three.”

They wrote in the upper window. A single ink pin dropped; the trial Balance moved from 100 to 90. The scoring line became 3,3,3 while the actual line remained 3,3,4.

The Jackpot closed the trial objective. Nobody's account above their head changed. Cui detached the small training pin before handing the Brush back to its support.

“That is the beginning of Rewrite,” he said. “Not permission to use another person's record as a blank page.”

Nil touched the empty recess on the brace. The fitted practice mechanism had shown them a route their decoder could accept; choosing when to use it would still be their work.

“Can we amend Wen's summary now?”

“We can append the warranted correction. Not because you won. Because the source, witnesses and retained copies support it.”

Cui returned to the ordinary desk. There he used the small brush, not the great one, to add the continuation reference beside Wen's accepted subtotal. He preserved the disputed heading, marked why it was insufficient and identified the old testimony's actual witness and copying dates.

Bai Si watched the small brush move. When Cui finished, he asked for a place to add his own earlier acceptance to the chain of custody.

“It is already in the travelling book,” Cui said.

“It belongs here too.”

The judge made room.

The certified dead-count extract included the old and amended statements, the independent retention reference, the missing continuation and the limits on any interpretation of its destination. Cui's archive kept the counter-record. Bai Si received the protected travelling extract in a flat case he could secure beside the lantern harness.

Jinling requested only the comparison fields relevant to the harbour inquiry. The keeper refused a broader release without the households' permissions. She accepted the restriction, although it would make her father's office harder to confront quickly.

“A private embarrassment is still private,” Cui said. “An institutional debit can be shown without making every household pay for your argument.”

She folded the limited extract carefully. “Then I'll have to make a better argument.”

***

They waited at the marked return station while the crossing wardens checked the route.

Nil had expected to feel lighter after obtaining a new ability and a better record. Instead they were tired and hungry. Their hand smelled faintly of ink. The river beyond the rail kept moving without supplying an answer.

Jinling bought food from a court vendor, paying the listed price from the inquiry travel purse. Bai Si declined a portion, checked his lamp wicks and eventually accepted water when the vendor set a clean cup beside him.

“You buy debts,” he said to Jinling.

“Some.”

“And then?”

“I discharge them.”

“All of them?”

“No.”

He looked at her. She met the look without opening her fan to hide behind it.

“I don't have enough money for all of them. And even if I did, the next owner could write the next bad claim. I have been choosing people I can help quietly.”

“Who chooses the people you miss?”

Her bells were still. “Until now? Mostly circumstances. Sometimes people who know how to get a message to me.”

Bai Si drank the water. “That is a count too.”

Nil listened. Neither had asked them to decide who was right. The conversation could continue when they were not in the room.

Jinling drew a small dish toward her and divided the food into pieces without eating. A bell at her wrist had caught beneath the edge of her cuff. She freed it, rolled it between finger and thumb and finally noticed Bai Si watching.

“Eight,” she said. “People like to count them before speaking to me.”

“I was looking at the fastening.”

The loop was opening. She had caught it on the archive drawer and forgotten. Bai Si took a short length of plain cord from the lantern harness and offered it.

“Until someone can close the metal.”

“You don't want a fee?”

“I want it not to fall into the river.”

She accepted the cord. For once the joke about interest did not arrive.

Nil stepped away to refill their water. When they returned, the two were still talking.

“My father taught me these knots,” Jinling was saying, working the fastening slowly. “Not this one. The parcel ones. I tied every package so tightly the clerks needed a knife.”

“Did he teach you to leave a working end?”

“He said a knot someone had to cut was a wasted knot.” She looked at the cord in her hands. “He isn't always wrong.”

“You need not make him wrong about rope to challenge the accounts.”

She tested the loop. The bell stayed where it belonged.

“People find it tidier if I hate him.”

Bai Si set his cup down. “Does that help the people waiting?”

“No. It might help me speak without worrying he'll sound like my father while I'm trying to accuse the Chancellor.”

One of Bai Si's lanterns dimmed. He drew it down, opened the little service flap and adjusted the wick with a narrow tool. Nil watched the ordinary flame brighten again.

“Nothing inside it?” they asked before they could make the question polite.

“Oil. Wick. Light.”

“I thought—”

“Many do.” He closed the flap. “A person may follow a light. That does not make the lamp their container.”

Jinling held her repaired bell still while he returned the lantern to its place. “You don't mind having to explain?”

“Sometimes I am tired of it.”

The admission was so plain that Nil looked up. Bai Si had seemed made of an inexhaustible patience they would never be able to borrow.

“What do you do then?”

“Ask someone else to answer. Or say I am tired.”

He returned to the court extract. The old disputed name remained visible through its protective sleeve. He did not stroke it or ask the others to remember his regret for him.

Jinling finally ate a little of the food. “If my father opens the archive, he will choose which part to show.”

“Then name the part your permission covers,” Bai Si said. “Ask him to record the refusal if he will not show it.”

She nodded, testing the sentence aloud once. It had none of her usual ornament. Nil could hear her deciding to use it anyway.

On the return route the lantern with the fresh-adjusted wick cast a slightly warmer light than the others. Nil could distinguish it now without counting. When the gate narrowed, Jinling moved her sleeve clear so the little bell and the lantern would not catch each other.

At the gate Bai Si asked the clerk to add his name to the onward party record.

Jinling glanced at him. “To the harbour?”

“Beyond it, if the comparison continues.”

“Your office?”

“I have requested leave for an inquiry. The cases remaining here have assigned keepers. This extract cannot be my only duty merely because it is the oldest one I regret.”

Nil looked at the four lanterns. “Wen won't come back.”

Bai Si's face did not move away from the sentence.

“No.”

“Then what are we fixing?”

He secured the extract case. “What the next person is told. What the next account is allowed to leave out.”

They crossed together. Both wardens checked all three return tags before the stair gate opened. Above, Maeve had persuaded the harbour clerk to replace the loose rail bracket; the new fixing was bright against the old wood.

Hina met Nil at the top and inspected their hands for missing fingers before pretending she had only been checking the ink.

Vesper looked at the returning copies, then at Bai Si.

“More company.”

He bowed slightly.

She moved aside to make room for all four lanterns. It was a small courtesy, offered without a joke. Nil found themselves grateful for that too.

## Chapter 20: The True Account

Jinling's father had left a place for her at breakfast.

The bowl was covered. Beside it stood a small dish of preserved fruit she had liked as a child. Nil saw her notice it before she noticed the guard by the archive door.

Chancellor Jin Chan sat beneath a fan of gold-painted rafters, reading the morning warehouse report. He was a large man in generous robes, broad-faced and neatly shaved. His chair rested on three sculpted toad legs. Gold coins filled the open mouth carved into its arm.

“The east delivery's late,” he said. “Your favourite supplier has found another excuse for the river.”

“The river rarely needs his assistance.”

“You used to be kinder before breakfast.”

“I used to have less to read.”

She put the limited court extract on the table. Not the protected original; Bai Si kept that in its case, with the archive reference available for independent verification. Nil carried the withdrawn false comparison behind its correction. Vesper stood by the window where she could see the courtyard.

The Chancellor read the first sheet, then the second. His easy expression did not vanish. It became a piece of work he was doing.

“A probate dispute does not run a harbour.”

“Nor does omitting it.”

“You are comparing different obligations.”

“Yes,” Nil said. “I made that mistake once. The corrected comparison doesn't claim they are interchangeable.”

Jin Chan looked at the attached correction. His eyes stopped at Nil's signature.

“And I should trust the second answer?”

“You should check its independent sources.”

For a moment he seemed genuinely interested. Then he set the court extract beneath the warehouse report.

Jinling kept her hand on its edge.

“We need the true accounts,” she said.

“You need a clerk who understands them.”

“Then send one. With the full routing columns.”

Beyond the door a guard shifted. Bai Si's nearest lantern turned toward the movement; his blade remained secured on his back.

Jinling opened her fan. The beads along its ribs made a small measured sound.

“My ownership access remains on the register. The archive request was filed yesterday. Your clerk received it. If you refuse, write the refusal where the depositors can see it.”

The Chancellor's mouth tightened.

“Bring the review books,” he told the guard.

“The full routing books,” Jinling said.

The guard looked at his employer. After a pause Jin Chan nodded.

***

The true account was not one black book waiting to explain the world.

It was six volumes, a set of service schedules and a narrow register of exceptions. Two archive clerks checked the permissions before bringing them out. Jinling asked both to witness the receiving marks. Bai Si wrote the shelf references in a separate book.

The protected harbour route had received additional coverage before the last storms. The money columns showed who had paid the premiums. The reserve columns showed something else: district maintenance capacity diverted into the protected contract while local renewal queues lengthened. The public summary described the resulting safety as a single clean service total.

Nil followed a line from an upper Meridian guarantee to a lower district return. The withholding reference belonged to the same office that had failed to send Ama's corrected authorization onward. That did not prove Ama's six had paid for this particular ship. It proved the office had kept the return waiting while it cleared priority schedules.

“Those ships survived the storm,” Jin Chan said.

No one disputed it.

“So did people who needed their cargo. Medicine, grain, spare glass. You cannot make a fleet safe with declarations about equal treatment.”

Jinling turned the exception register toward him. “You can tell people whose renewal you postpone. You can let them dispute it before they spend a winter waiting behind a fee.”

“And if they all say no?”

“Then the office has to defend its priorities. It doesn't get to hide them inside a better-looking total.”

The clerks kept copying. Nil could hear their pens. A system was becoming visible through work someone could later dispute, not through a single confession no outsider would be able to check.

Jinling preserved certified extracts of the routing columns, the surplus accounts and the enforcement register. The originals stayed in the archive under two recorded shelf seals. A receiving duplicate went to the public review desk, carried by an archive clerk who did not work for Jinling's pawnshop.

Only after the clerk returned with the receiving number did she confront the exclusive control order.

“Suspend unilateral collection under these disputed routes,” she said. “Convene the review board.”

Jin Chan closed the exception register.

“A run starts with one rumour that the office no longer knows what it owes. You want to put a committee between every frightened depositor and the person who can act.”

“I want their deposits kept separate from your authority to spend the reserve.”

“They come to me because I can decide.”

From the outer corridor came a raised voice. A depositor was asking whether the doors would remain open through the hearing. The guard outside answered too quietly to hear.

The Chancellor looked toward the sound. His fear of it was real.

“A bounded control review,” he said. “You know the owner-dispute rule. My private reserve against your posted review allotment. The service desk continues. If my control claim closes, the key stays in witness custody until the hearing. If yours closes, you use the ordinary petition route.”

Jinling's fan stopped moving. “Customer deposits and scheduled wages excluded.”

“Of course.”

“Write them by name.”

He did.

***

The control court occupied the space behind the Exchange's great account board. Brass rails ran between the floor stations and the Chancellor's three-legged chair. Its carved toad mouth housed a capture shutter, not a living throat.

An archive clerk posted the rules. The Chancellor's round-end interest could replenish his bounded Balance from a separate committed vessel, at eight percent of the current stake, only while the side vessel had capacity. A Jackpot diverted a fifth of its declared output into his return path. The source and cap remained visible. No ordinary deposit entered either line.

“Your private fortune has excellent emergency exits,” Jinling said.

“I recommend them.”

The first round belonged to Nil, Jinling and Vesper, one stop each. The posted bases and available digits kept every actual strike at or below fifty; Carry would not trigger here. Maeve and Hina watched from the protected side with the receiving copies; Bai Si stood at the archive route, keeping all four lanterns clear of the apparatus.

Nil caught 3. Jinling followed with 3. Vesper took 5. A Pair lowered the control stake, but the side vessel promptly returned part of the loss as interest.

The great account board moved above them. Its shutters drove a closing action through the field rail. Vesper caught zero on defence. The load struck the stop housing and shuddered through the floor beneath their feet.

Nil looked at the side vessel. It was not bottomless. It was large enough to make a slow victory a poor plan.

Jinling had seen it too.

On the next sequence she signalled a chosen face to each of them. Nil took 7, Jinling 8, Vesper missed the intended last face and landed 6. Seven, eight, six. No ordered run, no other pattern.

The Chancellor smiled, but his hand was tight around the chair arm.

“You taught me to count the loss before the offer,” Jinling said.

“And still you came.”

The chair's mouth opened. A line of gold light ran toward Vesper's station. The posted capture marker showed her position before the shutter moved.

“Move clear,” Nil said.

She stepped back before the coupling closed. The machine took the station's scoring token into its brass mouth; Vesper remained physically outside it, alive and furious, but her attack input went dark.

“Pair releases the channel,” the clerk called. “One captured station. No second capture while it is held.”

Jinling took Vesper's place in the next sequence as the declared remaining operator. Nil would take first and last. They could not make a third person appear by pretending the shutter was merely decorative.

Nil caught 5. Jinling caught 5. Nil's last stop fell on 4.

Five, five, four. A Pair.

The shutter released Vesper's token. She caught the restored control and used the posted release strike before interest could cover the entire loss. The side vessel dropped visibly.

“Still a Pair,” she said, looking at Nil.

They nodded. A missed third match had not erased what they had actually stopped.

The Chancellor pressed a second detent. Three compound markers lit beneath the interest gauge. The posted warning raised the next three scheduled returns; a Zero-Sum would cancel the surcharge before it settled.

Nil felt the room narrowing around the easy answer: chase the largest matching pattern and hope the tax left enough. They looked instead at the digits available to the three of them.

“Three, two, five,” they said.

Jinling's wheel carried 2. Vesper's carried 5. Nil could stop 3. No one would need a face their wheel lacked.

Nil caught 3. Jinling waited a turn, then took 2. Vesper caught 5.

Ten. The Zero-Sum closed the next action and cancelled the declared compound surcharge. The three lit markers went dark before any enhanced interest was credited.

The remaining side vessel was lower now, enough for only part of another return. Jin Chan looked at it. For the first time he looked like a man making a calculation he had hoped not to need.

Jinling did not ask him to surrender because she was his daughter. The clerk had the witness terms. He could close his claim without closing the people waiting outside.

He kept his hand on the detent.

On the next round Nil stopped 7. Jinling took 7. Vesper landed 3.

A Pair, enough to press the stake close to its boundary, not enough to close it before another return.

Nil's ink fitting lay ready. One replacement after the three actual stops. Cui's trial had not made the 3 disappear. It had taught them where to put the new mark.

“I choose the third scoring slot,” they said for the witness. “Seven.”

The actual 3 remained in its lower window. The scoring line became 7,7,7. Nil's single-use ink pin dropped flat.

The chosen pattern closed through the declared channel. Overwrite had its own entry above an ordinary Jackpot in the posted priority; the Jackpot tax did not take a share of it. The main stake reached zero. Terminal closure stopped further interest; the side vessel could not reopen a completed loss.

The board above them went still.

Jin Chan sat heavily in his toad-legged chair. His Civic 88 remained above him. He was not bankrupt in the sense of having ceased to own a cup, a coat or a legal claim. His exclusive control stake had ended, and its credential moved into the clerk's witness tray.

Outside, the Exchange doors remained open. A service bell rang for the next depositor.

“You see?” he said softly to Jinling. “They still need someone behind the desk.”

“Yes,” she answered. “More than one someone gets to decide what the desk can do.”

Workers entered with the voided enforcement duplicates. Their evidence originals and certified copies had already been preserved. Under the receiving clerk's marks, Jinling burned only the duplicate seizure instructions whose authority had ended. The flames were small and needed feeding. Nobody mistook them for the true accounts.

A worker watched the last sheet blacken.

“And tomorrow you sit in his chair?”

Jinling looked toward it. The covered breakfast bowl still waited beyond the court door.

“Tomorrow,” she said, “you ask that in the hearing.”

***

Vesper spoke that night in the pawnshop's back room.

There was no ceremony to it. She put the double-ring mark from the purchased claim on the table, laid her closed revolver beside it and waited until the others had sat down.

“I know that table,” she said. “I rigged a hand there seven years ago.”

Nil felt the comfortable part of the room withdraw.

“For money?” Jinling asked.

“For money.”

Vesper kept her hands flat. “The opposing guarantee included a child's Civic allocation. Mara Vale. Nine years old. I saw it. I knew the account shouldn't be on a gambling table. I fixed the result and took my share anyway.”

Hina's umbrella eye closed.

“She died?” Nil asked.

“No. Emergency donations kept her alive. She was injured. Her family lost their home. She missed schooling while adults argued about who had lawfully won.” Vesper swallowed. “She's sixteen now. I know where the case correspondence goes. I haven't earned the right to walk into her life and ask her to tell me I'm different.”

No one offered the sentence she had been refusing to ask for.

Maeve looked at the revolver. “You should have told us when you saw the mark.”

“Yes.”

“Before we signed anything with people tied to it.”

“Yes.”

Nil thought of their own false certainty at the tea table. It did not make the two harms equal. It stopped them pretending that shame had ever been a good reason to let someone else choose without the facts.

“Why now?” they asked.

“Because you'll find more of that financing trail. Because saying it after someone else exposes it would still be making you do my work.”

She took the gun off the table and holstered it without opening the cylinder. Five rounds remained. The count had nothing to say about what kind of person held them.

“I won't ask you to answer tonight,” she said.

Hina opened her eye. “Good.”

Later, after the others had gone upstairs, Nil stayed with the copies of the protected-route accounts. On an older correction they found a small set of initials: I.M. Beside them a release order had been received, then retained without action at the district desk.

The beneficiary's name was Livia Kade.

Nil checked Gus's window note. The same full name. The same address.

An order had existed. A woman had remained behind the stair.

They went to find Jinling, carrying the page flat so the date and the initials could not be missed.

## Chapter 21: A Debt to the Living

The woman who owed eight fen wanted to know why seven was fortunate.

She stood at the public table with a folded repayment notice. On the slate beside Jinling, debts below eight fen were listed for discharge from the Exchange owner's distributable surplus.

“My seven would go,” the woman said. “My eight stays. Did your father invent the last fen?”

Several people in the queue laughed. Jinling did not.

“The first budget covers these entries,” she said. “It isn't a judgment about which debtor deserves relief.”

“It becomes one at my table.”

Jinling looked at the account again. “Then the limit stays visible, and your case stays in the review list. I can't honestly promise money this release hasn't provided.”

The woman asked for the review number, received it and remained beside the hearing rather than going away grateful for an explanation.

Nil was glad she stayed. They were beginning to distrust meetings in which only the satisfied people were still present when the agreement was read.

The hearing occupied the Exchange's public counting room. The ordinary service windows continued at the far end. A chalk line separated the waiting depositors from the records table so people could enter and leave without appearing to vote for whoever stood near the Chancellor's chair.

Jin Chan attended under a witnessed control restriction. His accounts and obligations would be examined, not made to disappear because his daughter had closed his Audit stake. He objected to several entries and supplied documents for two. One objection was upheld. The clerk changed the hearing sheet and preserved the earlier version.

“You see,” he murmured to Jinling.

“Yes,” she said. “I can survive your being right about a document.”

He looked away first.

The cooperative's witness brought its still-open loss complaint. Nil's correction remained attached. Jinling's private payment covered the immediate wage shortfall; the hearing did not call it a waiver of further claims. The worker with the healed knuckle was back at the yard. His name appeared on the injury record without requiring him to come and perform the pain for an audience.

The provisional board was argued over for most of the morning. Workers selected two temporary representatives from the people who would actually operate the draw stations. Depositors chose their own receiving witnesses. Municipal clerks held the public counter-copies. Jinling remained an owner with access and legal obligations, not an elected voice for everyone who had accepted her help.

Its first term ended after a stated service cycle unless renewed through the public process. Emergency debits needed two named approvals, a recorded purpose and an expiry. Essential wage and deposit accounts stayed outside private review stakes. The board could correct a local schedule; it could not seize the reserves of other regions or promise protection it did not fund.

A warehouse lender refused to endorse the relief release. He wanted an appeal against the source classification of some claimed surplus. The board accepted the appeal and kept the disputed portion out of the immediate distribution rather than paying it away before the question could be heard.

The remaining verified surplus funded the listed under-eight-fen discharges. Not deposits. Not wages. Not luck newly created because somebody had made a generous speech.

Jinling signed the loss of her father's sole control and her own claim to inherit it unchanged. Her pen paused at the ownership disclosure. Nil saw her look toward the quiet side door, where she could have left the room and continued buying a few debts in secret.

She kept writing.

After the board's actual service test, the exclusive office credential came out of the central receiver. Seal 8 was released under the completed review terms. The provisional local authority replaced its monopoly clearance on the participating routes; the credential remained a separate central-access key, not a crown.

The receiving clerk offered it to Jinling.

She turned to Nil. “Will you keep it with the others?”

They wrapped it separately, recorded the receiving mark and checked the three packets in order:1,9,8. The repetition was useful now. It was not an answer to what should govern the Exchange tomorrow.

The true-account extract received a protected travelling seal. Jinling would carry it; the board kept the certified archive copy and the right to challenge any use that exceeded the release. Bai Si retained the dead-count extract and Cui's independent reference. Hina's Enma finding remained in its dry sleeve, query attachment restricted until Nil chose to authorize another comparison.

Someone asked whether the witnesses intended to remain and run the new arrangement.

Jinling closed her fan.

“The records need independent measurement. I want to accompany the inquiry. The board has its own paid clerks and its own route stewards. It cannot depend on my standing in this room.”

“And your private claim purchase?” Vesper asked from the side.

Jinling brought out the bounty assignment. The suspension remained registered; its administration would pass to the named local custodian under the no-transfer condition Nil had accepted. Neither Jinling nor the board could sell it onward without Nil's permission. The national issuer could still attempt a new order. Everyone read that limit before signing.

Nil did not mistake a safer harbour for a country that could no longer hunt them.

***

Little Lantern waited outside the shop with his delivery ledger under his jacket.

He had heard Jinling was leaving. When she came through the door he asked whether that meant the rice behind the screen belonged to someone else now.

Jinling sat on the front step, making herself low enough that he did not have to look up.

“The shop will stay open. The household keeper and I have spoken about a room and regular meals, if you want them. You wouldn't pay with deliveries.”

“What would I pay with?”

“Nothing. It's a care arrangement.”

He looked down at the ledger.

“People say that before they count.”

Jinling nodded. “Then keep your own copy. Choose someone outside the shop to read it with you. The school clerk can be the second contact, if you like her.”

“She's very slow.”

“That may be a professional advantage in this conversation.”

He considered the doorway. “Can I keep the ledger?”

“Yes.”

“Do I have to keep delivering?”

“No. If you choose any work later, it needs its own safe hours and fee. Food and a bed don't depend on it.”

“What if I go out?”

“Tell the keeper where you're going. We make a safe return plan. Nobody sends you away for disliking a room.”

He sat beside her. The lantern on its stick leaned against the step.

“I want to see it first.”

Jinling stood. “Then let's look.”

Nil watched them go inside with the household keeper, who introduced herself before asking whether the boy wanted the door open. An arrangement had begun. It was not a child purchased along with a bounty, and it would need work after the visitors left.

At the back table lay a courier packet addressed through Gus's Exchange contact. Nil recognized the Wards dispatch mark. They had sent a short message on arrival; this reply had been written on Day 10 and carried by the direct cargo post. Its dates mattered. Nought knew they had reached the harbour. He knew nothing yet about the cooperative hold or the court descent.

His letter did not mysteriously answer the questions they had not sent.

He was still at the shrine. Baker had complained about the Collector standing where customers queued. Ama had delivered the repaired coat and kept both receipt copies. The laundry cart needed a new wheel. Gus had added the price and an address for contributing when Nil could afford it.

Nil read that part twice, feeling a peculiar relief at being given a concrete debt instead of a moral one with no end.

Nought had included a short statement about the release order. The Wards office acknowledged receiving it but claimed the treatment transfer still required a named receiving clinic, the patient's agreement and separately guaranteed travel coverage. A stale authorization alone had not opened Livia's stair.

Jinling compared its reference with the page bearing I.M. The order in the Exchange archive matched. She obtained a certified dated copy and sent it to Gus with a request to ask Livia what care and route she wanted. They did not write a rescue as if consent and medicine were pieces of a missing sum.

Below the order, another archive slip carried the full name behind the initials: Ivo Marr. It identified a rejected emergency-allocation proposal from TR999. Copies would be released under the existing correspondence request; the author had authorized transmission of the administrative proposal, not every classified appendix held by the Basilica.

The local clerk fetched the received copy from the public dispatch file. Ivo had not travelled to Bāfā. His handwriting had.

The proposal described regional emergency pools with shared oversight and an appeals route outside the Comptroller's sole office. It listed risks of delay and inconsistent local records. In the margin, Thale had rejected the transfer of executive control pending a level of certainty the proposal itself said no emergency system could guarantee.

Nil touched the date. “He had another option.”

“An option with costs,” Jinling said. “And people he would have had to share the decision with.”

Bai Si read the retained limits. “We should compare this with the original if we reach it.”

They copied the reference, not the conclusion that a document had solved the problem by existing.

Nought's second sheet was less official. He wrote that questions about the route papers might grow into a larger inquiry. He supplied the addresses of Open Hand contacts: a clinic, a meal room, a carrier who accepted sealed counter-copies without demanding the private account inside.

The older tradition behind the name called chance Hap. The Basilica prohibited some of its independent pledges. Nought described the tradition as people making room for an outcome they did not own, not proof of what a hidden god presently wanted.

**You do not owe the network an examination because someone gave you a chair,** he wrote. **If you need a door opened, ask for a door. We can disagree about the story above it.**

Nil looked at the notebook he had given them. They had not written the dreams into the letters. They could not tell whether his old tradition and their shifting figures belonged together, and the page did not ask them to decide it yet.

Jinling brought an invitation from the observatory's trade liaison. Her earlier request for an independent comparison had received a named instrument slot, subject to arrival and an agreement on what personal data could be used. Śūnyapura kept measurements not maintained by the Exchange or the Ten Courts. That was a reason to go, not a promise that scholars would know what Nil was.

They booked a paid place on the plateau caravan leaving before the following dawn. The board's inquiry budget covered the declared comparison journey and provisions, with personal purchases excluded. Jinling logged her own contribution separately. Nil paid the first small contribution toward the laundry cart from earned sorting wages and kept the acknowledgment.

They sent Nought a new letter with the observatory address, the public comparison references and the reason for going. Hina helped keep the restricted query attachment out of the dispatch copy. The carrier stamped it Day 13. Any answer would have to find them farther along the road; none was owed to them tonight.

Vesper stood at the room's threshold while they checked the itinerary.

“Do you still want me on the road?” she asked.

Maeve looked up. “I want you to tell us when the road goes through your past.”

“I will.”

Hina closed the sleeve around Enma's finding. “I'm not promising to be comfortable about it.”

“I know.”

Nil did not offer a verdict for Mara. “You can come. We don't decide what she owes you.”

Vesper nodded. The answer hurt; it was also more than she had been entitled to demand.

That evening Jinling went back to the hearing desk to sign her departure disclosure. Her father was still there, arguing over a warehouse assessment. When she came out, she carried the untouched little dish of preserved fruit from breakfast in a wrapped bowl.

No one asked whether she had forgiven him. She put the bowl with the road provisions.

Nil slept in the bed one last night, with three separately wrapped credentials and the paid caravan receipt beneath the notebook.

***

A little money toad sat on a counter in darkness.

It had three legs, a coin beneath its mouth and two turning dice where its eyes should have been. Separate digits drifted around it like dust caught in lamplight. The counter stretched no farther than Nil could see.

“What are you owed?” it asked.

Nil thought first of a record that would recognize them, then of the woman with the eight-fen debt and the man pinched by the capstan lever. Wanting an answer had not made their answer true.

“I don't know how to count that,” they said.

The toad waited.

“I know some things I owe. That doesn't mean everybody I help has to give me a place.”

The coin turned beneath its mouth. Nil could not tell whether it had landed on a face or merely rolled beyond the light.

When they woke, the caravan driver was knocking on the shop shutter. Maeve had already tied the tool bundle. Hina was arguing that a road breakfast could still deserve a plate.

Jinling counted five companions at the stair, then looked at Bai Si's lanterns and changed the carrying order so nobody had to force them through a narrow doorway.

Nil took the last bag without pretending it was too light to share.
