# ZERO-SUM: OMEN

# The Remainder

## Volume Four: What the Numbers Cannot Own

## Chapter 22: Room for Chai

The plain began where the river forgot itself.

For two days the caravan had followed a raised road between flooded fields, and the water on either side lay so flat and silver that Nil kept mistaking it for sky. Egrets stood in it on one leg. Men and women in rolled trousers stood in it too, setting rice in rows so straight that Maeve stopped the cart to look at them, and nobody needed to explain why.

“They're using a string,” she said. “Tied at both ends. Look.”

Nil looked. A woman at the near edge of the field pulled a line taut between two stakes, pushed seedlings into the mud along it, then moved both stakes a hand's width and did it again. Her Civic number hung over her head, a dull 03, and the rain that had stopped everywhere else was still falling on her field. It fell in a thin grey column no wider than a house, as if someone had forgotten to turn it off.

“Is that ordinary?” Nil asked.

The caravan driver did not look round. “Monsoon's ordinary. Rain that picks a field is new this year.”

“Since when?”

“Since the surveyors came.” He clicked his tongue at the bullocks. “They set up their machines on the plateau and the weather started choosing.”

Jinling wrote that down without being asked. She had been writing all morning, in the small account book with the plain cover, and every so often she showed Bai Si a line and he nodded or did not. His four lanterns rode on a pole lashed to the cart's frame. In daylight they were only paper and brass, swaying.

Hina sat at the back with her legs over the tailboard and her umbrella open against a sun that kept appearing and vanishing. The umbrella's single eye followed the egrets.

“Hina has decided,” she announced, “that she likes this country.”

“You've been here two days,” Vesper said.

“It has a great many birds and nobody has tried to buy Hina.” She considered. “Yet.”

They had paid for the caravan in Bāfā: four places, a share of the cook's rice and one corner of the cart for Maeve's tools. The provisions were running down at the rate the driver had promised, which Nil found oddly comforting. Lentils on the first evening, lentils with onion on the second, and this morning a flat bread that tasted of smoke. Their purse was lighter by exactly what they had eaten. Nothing had appeared because they needed it.

The observatory announced itself before the city did.

First there was a line on the horizon that Nil took for a ridge. Then the line acquired angles. By noon it had become a set of enormous stone shapes standing on a low rise above the roofs: a staircase that went up to nowhere, a curved wall like a slice of a bowl the size of a hill, a triangle taller than the Basilica's lower wards, all of it the colour of old bone in the wet light. Pigeons rose off it in sheets and settled again.

“Instruments,” Jinling said. “The whole hill is instruments.”

Nil had expected a building with a telescope on top. They sat up straighter on the cart and stopped counting the bullocks' steps.

***

Śūnyapura smelled of wet stone, frying mustard seed and smoke. Every doorway they passed had a lemon and seven green chillies hanging on a thread above it. Some were fresh. Some had dried to brown curls and nobody had replaced them, and Nil noticed that the doors with the dried charms belonged to the narrow houses near the drains, where the paint had come off and the steps were cracked.

Vesper saw them noticing. “Charms cost money too,” she said.

“Seven chillies?”

“Every week, if you want them fresh. Some weeks you want dinner more.”

At the observatory gate a crowd had gathered under a canvas awning. Not a mob: a dozen people sitting on the wet flagstones in a row, with their backs against the gatepost and their tools laid out in front of them in neat lines. Brass sighting rods. Plumb bobs. A coil of measured cord. A man with a grey beard had a slate on his knees with a column of figures on it, and above the column, in large careful letters: **TWELVE WEEKS.**

“Instrument crew,” Bai Si said quietly. “Those are calibration rods.”

The grey-bearded man looked up at the cart, at Jinling's bells, at the oilcloth bundles. His Civic read 05.

“If you're the guests,” he said, “you can go in. The scholars said to let the guests in.”

“And you?” Nil asked.

“We're the crew.” He tapped the slate. “We're owed twelve weeks. Until we're paid, nobody climbs the Great Dial and nobody calibrates a thing. They can look at the sky with their eyes like everyone else.”

Maeve was already crouching to look at the rods. One had a joint that had been bound with wire where a brass sleeve should have been.

“That'll throw your reading,” she said.

“It throws everything,” said a young woman further down the row. “We bought the wire ourselves.”

The gate opened before Nil could decide what they ought to say. A thin man in a damp grey shawl came out holding two umbrellas, one already open over his own head, the other furled. He handed the furled one to the grey-bearded crewman before he looked at the cart.

“Gopal,” he said. “It's going to rain on your side of the gate again. I don't know why. I'm writing it down.”

“Write down our wages,” Gopal said, but he took the umbrella.

“I have. Every day. In the same book as the rain.” The thin man turned. He had ink on both cuffs and a pencil behind each ear, as if he had lost the first and found it after acquiring the second. “You are Ba Jinling's party. I'm Vidya Rao. I keep the catalogue here, and at the moment I keep it very badly, because I can't pay the people who make the measurements true.”

Jinling climbed down from the cart. “The Exchange board sent you an instrument fee in advance.”

“It did. The Meridian office froze it at the regional desk on the day it arrived.” He showed her a folded notice. “Pending review of reporting obligations under the instrument charter.”

Nil recognised the phrase. They had read it, or something very like it, in Nought's letter about the new instructions coming out of the Comptroller's office: observatories reminded of their duties. In Bāfā it had been a line of text. Here it was a dozen people sitting in the wet.

“So the invitation,” Vesper said slowly, “came from a place that can't pay its own staff.”

“The invitation came from a place that wanted an independent comparison badly enough to invite you anyway.” Vidya looked at the row of seated people and did not pretend they were not there. “I won't ask the crew to work for promises. I also won't pretend I have an instrument without them.”

Jinling was already doing arithmetic. Nil could see it in the slight stillness of her fan.

“I can't pay twelve weeks,” she said. “Not from what's mine to spend. I can lend the observatory a bridge against the frozen fee, with the fee as the security and the crew paid first. If the fee is never released, the loss is mine, not theirs.”

Gopal looked at her for a long moment. “In writing.”

“Obviously in writing.”

“With the crew's names on it. Not 'staff'.”

“Give me the names.”

It took an hour, under the awning, with the rain falling on Gopal's side of the gate exactly as Vidya had predicted and nowhere else. Nil held the umbrella over the ledger. Maeve replaced the wire on the calibration rod with a brass sleeve from her own bundle and charged nothing, then thought better of it and charged the price of the sleeve, because Gopal insisted. Hina learned the young crewwoman's name (Lakshmi) and taught her the word for the umbrella's tongue, which as far as Nil could tell Hina had invented on the spot.

When the bridge was signed, Gopal rolled up his cord, stood, and stretched his back until it cracked.

“The Great Dial wants cleaning before anyone trusts a shadow on it,” he said. “Tomorrow, if it stops raining on me.”

***

The guest rooms were on the observatory's lower terrace, three small rooms off a courtyard with a neem tree in the middle and a brass basin under the tree that caught the leaf-drip. In one corner a woman sat on an upturned crate with a kettle on a charcoal brazier, stirring something with a long spoon and reading a sheaf of paper at the same time. She had ink on her cuffs too, worse than Vidya's, and had clearly tried to wash it out and succeeded mainly in spreading it.

“You let them in,” she said to Vidya without looking up. “Good. Sit, everyone. It's nearly ready. The trick is to boil the milk twice and not let it know you're watching.”

“Shunya,” Vidya said. “My student. She is, unfortunately, also the reason you're here.”

“Former student.” She looked up then. Her eyes went straight to the place above Nil's head, and stayed there, and she did not apologise for looking. “Oh,” she said softly. “Oh, that's worse than the report. That's wonderful.”

Nil's hand went to their cuff.

“Shunya,” Vidya said.

“I know. I know.” She poured the chai into small clay cups, steam curling off the top, and handed them round in the order people happened to be standing. Nil's came last. It was hot enough that they had to hold it by the rim. It tasted of cardamom and smoke and something sharp they couldn't name.

“Ginger,” Shunya said, watching their face. “And black pepper. My grandmother's. She believed pepper kept the monsoon out of your chest. It doesn't, but it's nice.”

She sat back down on the crate and leaned forward with her elbows on her knees.

“May I tell you what I think? Before anyone measures anything. I'd like you to hear it as a guess, not a verdict.”

Nil glanced at Vesper, who shrugged very slightly. *Your call.*

“You can tell me,” Nil said. “I'm not agreeing to anything else.”

“That's fair. That's correct, actually.” Shunya wrapped both hands round her cup. “Every morning the Basilica runs the Dawn Tally. Everyone's allocation is computed, rounded to whole units, and posted. Rounding throws away fractions. Tiny ones: a hundredth here, a thousandth there. Across the whole country, every day, for three hundred years. The fractions have to go somewhere. Accounts have to balance. I've been looking for where they go for four years, and I've found the place they *should* show up, and there's nothing there. A hole exactly the shape of a very large number.” She paused. “And then a report comes from Meridian of an account with no entry that reads as NaN. Not a number. Not zero, not missing. Something the system can't express as a whole.”

The courtyard was very quiet. The neem dripped into the basin.

“I think you might not be missing a number,” Shunya said. “I think you might be too many of them.”

Nil set the cup down on the edge of the basin, carefully, because their hand had started to shake and they did not want to spill it.

“You think,” they said.

“I think. I haven't tested it. I could be wrong in about six different ways, and I'd like to find out which.” She hesitated. “Which would mean measuring you. Your decoder, at least. Your account signature.”

Before anyone else could speak, Nil pulled their right sleeve down over the brass ring at their wrist.

“Not today,” they said. “And not without terms. I want to know what you'll measure, what you'll keep, who sees it, and how I stop it. In writing. Before anyone touches it.”

They heard their own voice and were surprised by it. Not loud. Not apologetic, either. Two weeks ago they would have held out the wrist and asked if this was the correct answer.

Shunya nodded slowly. “Yes,” she said. “Yes. That's how it should be. I'll write it tonight.”

“I'll read it tomorrow.”

“Good.”

Vesper picked up Nil's abandoned chai, sniffed it, and drank half of it. “You let it go cold,” she said. “Terrible business practice.”

Nil almost laughed.

***

Meera came at dusk.

She didn't knock. She stood at the courtyard arch with a coil of rope over one shoulder and a covered lamp in her hand, and waited until Vidya saw her, and then she walked straight past him to where Nil sat with Maeve under the neem.

She was perhaps thirty, broad-shouldered, with her hair tied up in a cloth and calluses across both palms that Nil could see from where they sat. Her Civic read 14.

“You're the one they've been writing letters about,” she said.

“Probably.”

“I don't care what you are.” She said it flatly, not cruelly. “I care that the scholars are excited, and when the scholars are excited they go into the stepwell archive, and my brother's record is in the stepwell archive, under four storeys of monsoon water, and I've asked for it back six times.”

Vidya had come up behind her. “Meera—”

“Six times.” She didn't turn round. “Kiran dived the archive for twelve years. He went down in the flood two summers ago to bring the old records up before the water reached them, and the water reached him first. His life record went in the lower cabinet with everyone else's. I want it out. I want to hold it. I don't want it in a comparison table.”

Nil looked at Shunya, who had gone pale.

“We need records from the lower archive,” Shunya said. “For the comparison. Dead records especially. They keep fractions the summaries drop—”

“Then you need *me*,” Meera said. “Because nobody else in this city can get down there and back. And I'm telling you my price before you ask. Kiran's record comes up first, it goes into my hands, and nobody reads it without my say.”

“That's not a price,” Jinling said from across the courtyard. She had been listening with her fan closed. “That's a minimum. Shunya, you wanted my true-account extract for the same table. I told you on the road what my answer was.”

“You said 'not yet'.”

“I said the depositors gave permission for a review, not for publication. If your comparison prints their entries in a scholarly appendix, every lender in the River Empire will know which dock workers are behind on rent.” Jinling's bells were very still. “I didn't burn my father's enforcement copies so you could publish a nicer version.”

Shunya opened her mouth, shut it, and looked at her chai.

“I'm not trying to hurt anyone,” she said, and her voice had lost its quick, provisional rhythm. It came out small. “I'm trying to be right about something that matters.”

“Then be right with permission,” Meera said.

Nil stood. They hadn't planned to. The words were already in their mouth, and for once they let themselves check them before they said them.

“Then let's write it all down,” they said. “Meera's terms for the dive. Jinling's terms for the extract. Mine for the decoder. Vidya's for the crew. Everybody's limits on the same page, before anyone does anything. If Shunya's guess is right, it'll still be right with the limits on it.”

Shunya looked up at them. For a moment she seemed about to argue. Then she put the cup down and laughed, short and surprised.

“A negotiated collaboration,” she said. “With consent clauses. Vidya, the Faculty's going to hate this.”

“The Faculty hasn't paid the crew either,” Vidya said. “Get some paper.”

They worked by lamplight under the neem until the brazier went out. Meera's terms were short and specific: depth limits, rope checks, a return boundary marked on the stair, and the record into her hands before anyone's eyes. Jinling's ran to a page and a half. Nil's were shortest of all. They kept crossing things out and making them simpler, until the page said only: *Measure the decoder, not me. Tell me first. I can stop. Nobody owns the result.*

Shunya read it twice.

“'Nobody owns the result',” she said. “Including me?”

“Including you.”

She took a long breath and signed under it.

Later, when the others had gone in, Nil sat alone on the basin's edge with the cold dregs of the chai and the paper. Above the observatory the clouds had cleared from one slice of sky, and the great curved wall of the Dial stood black against the stars. Somewhere up on the plateau beyond it, faint and regular, something mechanical was turning: a long grinding pulse, like a mill wheel, every few seconds.

It sounded like a wheel looking for a place to stop.

Nil folded the paper into Nought's notebook, between the ferry fee and the toll reference, and went to bed.

## Chapter 23: The Drowned Record

The stepwell went down like a book opened the wrong way.

From the top it was a long rectangular pit cut into the earth, and on three sides of the pit stairs zigzagged down in tiers, each flight crossing the one below it, hundreds of steps folding in on themselves until they disappeared into green water. On the fourth side a narrow tower of stone galleries dropped straight down, one storey under another, with carved pillars and little shelved rooms opening off each landing. The top four storeys were dry. The fifth was ankle-deep in leaves. Below that, the water had the archive.

Meera did not let anyone touch a step until she had finished talking.

“Rope one is the safety line,” she said, laying it out along the top terrace. “It goes from this ring to my harness, and Maeve holds it. Not ties it off. Holds it. If I go under and don't come up when I've said, you pull.” She looked at Maeve. “You've held a line before?”

“Forge chains,” Maeve said. “And a boat brace in a storm.”

“Good. Rope two goes to the lamp float. Bai Si, your lanterns don't need oil from the air?”

“They need oil,” Bai Si said. “Not air for burning. They will light the water from above. They will not go under.”

“That's what I want.” Meera turned to the rest of them. “Nobody else goes past the fourth landing. That's the return boundary.” She pointed at a pillar where someone, years ago, had painted a thick red ring. “Past the ring the steps are slick and some of them are gone. If I'm not back at the ring by the time the sand runs out” (she handed Jinling a small glass timer) “Maeve pulls, and you all help her. You don't come down after me.”

“And if the line snags?” Nil asked.

Meera looked at them. “Then I cut it and swim, and you lose my rope, and I'm cross about it for a week.” She tapped a knife at her belt. “I've done this four hundred times, Nil. The water isn't the problem. The problem is people at the top who think they can help by coming down.”

Nil thought of the drain gate, and the Hound on the stairs, and how often they had run toward something because staying still felt worse.

“I'll stay at the ring,” they said.

“You'll stay at the top,” Meera said. “The ring's for Maeve and the float.”

They stayed at the top.

***

Shunya had brought her instrument and set it up on the top terrace under an awning, out of everyone's way. It was a squat brass case about the size of a bread oven, with two glass tubes running out of one side and a little dial on the front, and it was not connected to anything. She had shown Nil the empty sockets that morning before she unpacked it.

“Inert,” she had said. “Nothing goes in or out of a living account. I'm testing the model, not anyone in it.”

The model was simple enough that Nil could follow it. If the missing fractions from the Dawn Tally all went into one place, then they ought to behave like one kind of thing: equal quantities, interchangeable, like coins in a jar. If you fed two equal pulses of calibration charge through two different routes, they should arrive in the same jar at the same moment, because a unit was a unit.

“And if they don't?” Vesper had asked.

“Then I'm wrong about the most important assumption in four years of work,” Shunya had said cheerfully, “and I'd like to know before I tell anyone else it's true.”

Now she knelt by the case, turning the dial while Meera rigged her harness at the edge of the pit. Nil watched both. One woman going down into dark water. One checking whether a theory would hold.

Meera stepped off the fourth landing and went into the green.

Jinling turned the timer over.

For a while there was nothing but the slow grind of the sand and the slap of water against stone far below. Bai Si's lanterns hung on their pole over the pit, four warm discs of light on the surface. Under the surface Nil could see a pale shape moving, then nothing, then the pale shape again, deeper, going sideways into the drowned gallery where the lower cabinets stood.

Hina gripped her umbrella in both hands. Its eye was fixed on the water.

“Hina doesn't like this,” she said.

“Neither does Hina's umbrella,” said Vesper.

“The umbrella *is* Hina.”

“Then you're consistent.”

Maeve sat braced at the red ring with the line around her back and both feet against a step. She let it pay out when it pulled, took up the slack when it went loose, and didn't speak at all.

Half the sand had run when the stepwell made a sound Nil had never heard a building make.

It was a long low groan, deep in the stone, and then a crack like a dropped plate. On the far side of the pit, two flights below the red ring, a whole section of stair simply let go: a dozen steps peeled off the wall in one piece and fell into the water with a slap that sent a wave across the pit. The lantern float rocked. One of Bai Si's lanterns swung so hard it nearly hit the stone.

The water level jumped. Not by much. A hand's width. Enough that the fourth landing's lowest step, which had been dry, was suddenly under.

“The fall pushed water into the galleries,” Bai Si said. “She'll have a current.”

Maeve's line went taut. Not the slow pull of someone swimming: a hard jerk, then another, sideways.

Nil was on their feet before they knew it. Their foot was on the first step.

“*Top,*” Vesper said.

Nil stopped. Every part of them wanted to keep going. They stood with one foot on the stair and their hands in fists, and they made themself look at what was actually happening instead of what they were afraid of.

The line was moving. Jerking, but moving, in a pattern: three pulls, a pause, three pulls.

“That's a signal,” Nil said. “Isn't it? Three and three.”

Maeve's face was set. “Meera said three means 'snag, wait'. Two means 'pull'.”

They waited. The sand ran. Shunya had stopped turning her dial and stood with both hands pressed against her mouth.

Then the line went slack, and for a terrible second nobody breathed, and then it pulled twice.

Maeve hauled.

Bai Si dropped his lantern pole lower so that light lay along the surface. Jinling, without a word, took the rope behind Maeve and leaned back on it. Vesper took it behind Jinling. Nil got their hands on the end, and the four of them walked it up the steps backwards, hand over hand, until a shape came up out of the green at the edge of the fourth landing and grabbed the pillar with the red ring on it.

Meera hung there for a long moment, breathing. Her headcloth had gone. There was a cut along her forearm where something had caught her, and the blood ran thin and pink in the wet.

In her other hand, held clear of the water, she had a narrow waxed tube about the length of a forearm, sealed at both ends.

“The current pulled the cabinet door shut on the line,” she said, when she could talk. “I had to cut the hinge.” She held up the tube. “Got it, though.”

Hina let out a sound that was half a laugh and half a sob. Maeve wrapped the line round the ring and went down two steps to help her up. Hina held the umbrella over both of them, though it wasn't raining.

At the top, Meera sat on the terrace with her back against a pillar and the tube across her knees. She didn't open it. She ran her thumb along the wax seal, slowly, the way Nil had once seen Ama touch a receipt.

“He always sealed them twice,” she said. “Said once was for the water, twice was for the people who'd open it.”

Nil sat down a little way off. They didn't ask anything.

***

Shunya's test had run while everyone was on the rope.

She had not stopped it. Nil found that they were glad of that; it would have been easy to blame the result on the interruption. She knelt by the brass case now with a notebook open, reading the dial and writing, then reading again.

“Well?” Jinling said.

Shunya didn't answer immediately. She tapped the glass of the left tube. Then the right.

“Equal pulses,” she said finally. “Equal quantities, both routes. Both arrived.” She turned the notebook round so they could see the figures. “Eleven seconds apart.”

“Is that a lot?”

“It's infinitely a lot. If they were interchangeable they'd arrive together. They didn't. The quantities balance and the timing doesn't.” She sat back on her heels. “Whatever the fractions are, they aren't just *amount*. They remember *when*. Which route. Which kind of claim. Maybe whose.”

“So you're wrong,” Vesper said, not unkindly.

“I'm wrong.” Shunya laughed, and it came out shakier than she meant. “Four years. I'm wrong about the thing I'd have bet my name on.”

“Then don't bet your name,” Meera said from her pillar, without opening her eyes. “Bet a page.”

Shunya looked at her.

“Write down that it failed,” Meera said. “Put it where people can see it. Kiran used to say the worst records were the ones that only kept the dives that came back.”

There was a pause. Then Shunya pulled the pencil from behind her ear, crossed nothing out, and wrote under the figures: *Equal-quantity model fails. Timing not preserved. Retained.*

***

That evening Meera opened the tube in the guest courtyard, under the neem, with only Nil and Shunya there because she had asked for them both.

Inside was a single sheet of thick paper, dry, folded twice: a life record of the kind every household in Śūnyapura kept and registered with the temple archive. Kiran's name at the top. His birth, his naming, his allocation, the years he'd dived. A column of small entries in different hands down one side: deeds witnessed, debts settled, a marriage he'd been witness to, a child he'd pulled out of a flooded drain. At the bottom, in a hand that wasn't his, the date of the flood.

Beside the final allocation total, in tiny careful figures that a summary would have dropped, someone had written a fraction and a mark that meant *carried forward*.

Meera held the sheet for a long time. Then she held it out to Nil.

“You want to compare that,” she said. “The little number at the bottom.”

Nil looked at the fraction and then at her face. “Shunya does. I want to know what it means.”

“Is there a difference?”

“Yes,” Nil said. “I think there's a difference.”

Meera considered that. She took the sheet back and laid it on her knees.

“You can copy the fraction,” she said. “And the date. And the mark. Not his name, not the deeds, not the child. Those are his. If your comparison needs more than that, you ask me again and I might say no.”

“That's enough,” Shunya said quickly. “That's more than enough. Thank you.”

“I'm not doing it for your paper.” Meera folded the record back along its old creases. “I'm doing it because if something took a piece of him every morning for thirty years, I'd like to know where it went.”

Nil made the copy themself, on a clean page, with Meera watching: one fraction, one date, one mark. When they had finished they turned the notebook so she could check it.

She checked it. Then, unexpectedly, she reached over and turned the page back to the one before, where Nil had written *Ask before promising* in Meridian.

“Who wrote that?”

“I did.”

“Hm.” She closed the notebook and handed it back. “Kiran would have liked you. He'd also have told you to eat something. Go and eat something.”

## Chapter 24: Terms of Examination

Shunya's terms ran to two pages, and she read every line aloud.

“What I'll measure,” she said. “The decoder's signature: the pattern your wrist mechanism gives off when it sits in a passive cradle. Not your Civic, because you haven't got one. Not your body. No blood, no hair, nothing taken. What I'll keep: the signature, written as figures, in two copies. One with me, one with you. What I won't do: publish it, show it to the Faculty, show it to any office, or attach your name to it, without asking you first, in writing, each time.” She turned the page. “Risks: I don't fully know. The cradle is passive. If it isn't, if anything pulls, if anything hurts, you lift your wrist and it stops. You don't need to explain why.”

She held up a small brass lever on the side of the cradle. “That's the release. It's on your side. Not mine.”

Nil read the pages after she'd finished reading them. They had learned that hearing a promise and seeing it written were different things, and that people sometimes said more than they wrote.

Shunya hadn't.

“And the result?” they asked. “If it says something.”

“Then I tell you first. Before Vidya. Before anyone.” She hesitated. “And it isn't mine. You wrote that. I signed it.”

Nil signed under her name.

Vesper, who had read both pages over Nil's shoulder, tapped the last line. “'Each time',” she said. “You'll get tired of asking.”

“Probably,” said Shunya. “I'll ask anyway.”

***

The cradle sat in a small stone room under the curved wall of the Dial, where the light came in through a single slot cut to catch the sun at noon. Vidya was there, writing. Bai Si had come to watch, and stood by the door with his lanterns turned low.

Nil laid their wrist in the cradle.

For a while nothing happened. Then the decoder warmed, the way it had in Ama's lane and at Nought's terminal, and the little orange light in the crack of the brass came on. On the cradle's face a needle moved and began to draw on a slowly turning drum of paper: a line that wobbled, steadied, broke into a ripple, steadied again.

It didn't hurt. Nil had expected it to hurt, and the absence of pain was strange enough that they kept waiting for it.

“It looks like handwriting,” Bai Si said quietly.

Vidya leaned over the drum. “It looks like a great deal of handwriting,” he said. “All at once.”

Shunya didn't say anything. She was writing figures as fast as the needle moved. Her face had gone very still.

After a quarter of an hour she sat back.

“That's the signature,” she said. “I can't tell you what it means yet. I can tell you it isn't one account. It's thousands of very small ones, layered. Like—” She stopped herself. “No. I'm not going to say what it's like. I'm going to compare it with something independent first.”

Nil lifted their wrist out of the cradle. The light in the decoder faded. Their hand was steady, which surprised them.

“Independent,” they said. “Like the harbour records? Wen's return? Kiran's fraction?”

“Like those. And one more.” Shunya looked at Vidya, and Vidya nodded slowly. “There's a keeper in this city who holds the records of deeds. Not allocations. Not Civic numbers. What people *did*. If the fractions in your signature come from people's lives, the deed records should have traces of the same losses, written down for a completely different reason by people who didn't care about allocations at all.”

“Chitragupta,” Bai Si said.

“You know him?”

“I know of him. The Ten Courts send him copies, sometimes.” He considered. “He is careful. He will not like being asked for an answer.”

“Good,” said Vidya. “Neither do I.”

***

Chitragupta's hall stood at the edge of the old city, past the temple tanks, in a courtyard where pigeons walked over geometric patterns of coloured chalk that somebody redrew every morning. Inside it was cool and smelled of palm leaf and lamp oil. Tall wooden cabinets lined the walls. Clerks worked at low desks, writing on long narrow leaves with styluses, and nobody looked up when the party came in.

The keeper sat at the end of the hall on a plain mat with a writing board across his knees. He was older than Nil had expected and smaller, a lean man with a white beard and a stylus behind one ear, and in front of him lay two palm-leaf ledgers side by side.

He finished the line he was writing before he looked up.

“Vidya Rao,” he said. “And a student who wants an answer. And a person the student wants an answer about.” His gaze rested on Nil's head for exactly as long as it took to read the flickering characters there, and then moved to their face and stayed. “Sit. What is your question?”

Shunya opened her mouth. Nil got there first.

“Whether the things people lost are written down anywhere,” they said. “Not the amounts. What they were.”

Chitragupta looked at them for a while. Then he set the stylus down.

“That is a better question than the one your scholar brought,” he said. “Hers is whether her figures are right. Yours is whether anyone remembered.” He laid one hand on each ledger. “I keep deeds. A deed is not a number. A man carries water to a neighbour; a woman lies under oath; a child returns a coin she found. I write what was done. I do not decide what it was worth.”

“But fractions turn up in your records,” Shunya said. “Carried-forward marks. I've seen copies.”

“They do. When a deed was rounded away elsewhere, my clerks note it. That is all.” He studied her. “You want to compare my marks with this person's signature. I can let you. But my records will not answer your question in your language. They answer in theirs. If you want my source, you must prove that you can tell the difference.”

“Prove how?”

He smiled very slightly and turned the two ledgers so they faced the party.

“A trial,” he said. “Nobody is harmed by it. Nothing is staked but your access to my cabinets.”

***

The trial was laid out on the floor between them: three wheels of polished wood in a frame, each turning slowly on its own, and on either side of the frame an open ledger. One ledger was headed in red: **WHAT WAS GIVEN.** The other in black: **WHAT WAS TAKEN.**

“Each digit you stop is a deed,” Chitragupta said. “Before it stops you do not know what it is. When it stops, my clerk reads you the entry it stands for, and you choose which ledger it belongs in. If you choose by the size of the number, you will be wrong more often than not. If you choose by what was done, you will be right.”

“And scoring?” Vesper asked, crouching to look at the wheels.

“Three entries in the same ledger make a pattern, the way your patterns are made: matches, runs, sums. A pattern in the correct ledger opens a cabinet. A pattern in the wrong one closes it.” He shrugged. “You have five rounds. There is no other cost.”

Nil and Shunya took the first round together, Shunya stopping the first wheel because she asked to and Nil the other two.

Shunya stopped a 7. The clerk, a girl of about fifteen with ink on her chin, read from a leaf without inflection: “Seven. A merchant gave seven measures of grain to a famine kitchen, and recorded it in his own tax ledger as a loss.”

“Given,” Shunya said at once.

“Given,” the girl agreed, and laid the leaf in the red ledger.

Nil stopped a 2.

“Two. A clerk took two days' wages from a widow's settlement and returned them a year later with an apology.”

Nil hesitated. *Taken*, said the first part of the sentence. *Given back*, said the second.

“What do you think?” Shunya asked.

“It was taken,” Nil said slowly. “Giving it back didn't make it not taken. It just means he also did something else later.”

Chitragupta's eyebrow moved a fraction of an inch.

“Taken,” said the girl, and laid the leaf in black.

The third wheel stopped on 1: “One. A boy gave one coin he'd found back to the woman who dropped it, though she was rich and he was not.”

“Given.”

Seven, two, one: no pattern in either ledger. A Bust. Nothing opened. Nothing closed.

“You were right each time,” Chitragupta said mildly. “Being right is not enough. You must also be right in a way that adds up.”

Shunya let out a breath that was almost a laugh. “That's my entire life,” she said.

The second round was worse. Nil stopped a 4 and called it *given*, because the entry said a woman had paid a debt, and only when the clerk set it down did Nil realise the debt had been her neighbour's and the money her own, and they'd guessed right by accident. Shunya stopped a 4 too: a man had *given* evidence against a friend. She hesitated a long time and said *taken*, because he'd taken his friend's trust, and Chitragupta's clerk laid it in black. Two fours in different ledgers. Nothing. The third wheel came round on 6, a deed about a fisherman lying to save his brother, and they argued about it until the sand ran out and the clerk set it aside unplaced.

Bai Si, watching from the door, said nothing. But when Nil glanced up at him, he gave a very small nod, as if to say: *That is the right argument to be having.*

In the third round Nil stopped looking at the numbers at all.

They watched the wheels until each one stopped, and then they listened to the clerk and thought only about what had been done and to whom. A 5: a midwife gave her night to a stranger's child. *Given.* A 5: a landlord gave a family a week's grace before eviction, and charged them for it. They both stared at that one.

“He gave them a week,” Shunya said.

“He sold them a week,” Nil said. “Taken.”

“Taken,” said the girl.

Shunya stopped the third: a 5. *A grandmother gave her grandson the last five pieces of bread in the house and said she wasn't hungry.*

“Given,” said Nil and Shunya together.

Two fives in red. One five in black. The red ledger had a Pair, and the clerk smiled for the first time and slid a small brass key across the floor.

“One cabinet,” said Chitragupta. “Two more rounds.”

They opened a second cabinet in the fifth round with a run in black: three, four, five, a Cascade of things taken from the same street over three years by the same office. The clerk read them in a flat voice. Nobody in the hall was smiling when she finished.

***

The cabinets held what Shunya had hoped for and also something she had not.

In the margins of the deed records, beside entries written decades apart in different hands, were the same small carried-forward marks Nil had copied from Kiran's sheet. Not on every entry. On the ones where something had been done that the official allocation hadn't counted: a night given, a week sold, a debt quietly paid. As if the system had noticed those moments and rounded them away.

Shunya copied the marks and dates into her notebook, one column per record, and her hand got slower as she went.

“These don't match my model,” she said at last.

“Your model failed yesterday,” Vidya said gently.

“I know. I mean they don't match the *corrected* one either.” She tapped the page. “The fractions aren't random losses. They cluster around particular kinds of deeds. Kindness. Debt. Sacrifice. Things done for other people.” She put the pencil down. “I need to ask a different question. And if I ask a different question, I need to measure Nil's signature against *this*, not against what I measured this morning.”

She turned to Nil.

“That's new,” she said. “It's not what you agreed to. I'm asking again.”

Nil looked at the column of little marks. At the deeds beside them. A night given. A week sold. Five pieces of bread.

“Measure the signature against these,” they said. “The same cradle. The same release. And Shunya, keep this morning's model too. The wrong one.”

“I was going to throw it out.”

“Don't. Write it down beside the new one.” Nil thought of Bai Si's travelling book, the old acceptance left visible beside the correction. “So people can see what you thought before you knew better.”

Chitragupta had been listening without expression. Now he picked up his stylus and wrote one line on a fresh leaf, then held it out to Shunya.

“A copy of what you were permitted,” he said. “Marks and dates. Not the deeds themselves; those belong to the people who did them. Bring me your comparison when it is finished. Not your conclusion. Your comparison.”

“What's the difference?” Shunya asked, and then, hearing herself, flushed.

The keeper's beard moved with something that might have been amusement.

“About four years,” he said. “In your case.”

## Chapter 25: Everyone's Handwriting

They laid it out on the floor of the comparison room because no table was long enough.

The room was a gallery at the top of the observatory's east tower, long and narrow, with tall unglazed windows on one side that looked out over the instruments and the wet roofs of the city. Shunya had swept it and chalked a grid on the boards: columns for the sources, rows for the dates. Into the grid, one sheet at a time, went every record they had.

From Bāfā: Jinling's extract, the entries masked except for the fractions the summaries had dropped and the dates the drops had happened. From the Ten Courts: Bai Si's dead-count extract, Wen's continuation mark beside his accepted subtotal. From the stepwell: Nil's one copied line from Kiran's sheet. From Chitragupta: a column of carried-forward marks beside deeds nobody had counted. And down the middle, unrolled from its drum and weighted at the corners with Maeve's spare washers, the trace from the cradle: Nil's signature, a long ragged line full of small ripples.

“Everyone's handwriting,” Hina said, standing at the end of the room with her umbrella furled. “In one place.”

Nobody had told her what the comparison was for. She had worked it out anyway.

Shunya knelt at the head of the grid with Vidya beside her. She didn't start by talking. She started by giving Bai Si a pencil.

“You read the dead-count dates aloud,” she said. “Jinling reads the harbour dates. Nil, Kiran's and Chitragupta's. I'll mark the trace. Vidya checks me. If anyone thinks I'm marking something because I want to see it there, say so.”

“I'll say so,” said Vidya. “Loudly.”

***

It took the whole morning.

Bai Si read a date from Wen's return: the day the subtotal had been posted and the continuation had vanished. Shunya ran her finger down the trace to the matching mark on the drum's time scale and looked for a ripple.

There was one. Small, distinct. A little notch in the line.

She marked it. Vidya bent and checked the scale, and checked it again, and nodded.

Jinling read a harbour date: a dock worker's allocation rounded down on a morning two winters ago. Ripple. Marked. Checked.

Nil read Kiran's date. The trace jumped, a sharp spike that made Shunya draw in her breath, and then settled.

“The flood,” Nil said quietly. “He died that day. The whole allocation released at once. Except the fraction.”

Meera wasn't there. She'd given permission for the fraction and the date and nothing else, and she'd said she didn't want to watch strangers look at it. Nil was glad. It was easier to say the date aloud without her face in the room.

Chitragupta's marks were the last and the strangest. They didn't line up with single ripples. They lined up with clusters: short bursts of small notches on the dates when a deed had been done and rounded away. A night given. A week sold.

By noon the grid was full of chalk ticks and the trace was dotted with pencil marks. Shunya sat back on her heels and looked at it. Nil had expected her to look triumphant. She didn't. She looked frightened.

“Well?” said Vesper from the window.

“Every independent source,” Shunya said slowly, “in four different kinds of record, written for four different reasons by people who'd never met, has a mark on a date where the Tally dropped something. And on every one of those dates, Nil's signature has a ripple.” She swallowed. “I can't find a source that doesn't. I've been trying to find one all morning, so that I could say this was a coincidence.”

Nil looked at the trace. At the ripples. Each one a morning, somewhere, when something small had been subtracted from someone and put somewhere else.

They felt the familiar pull. The room was waiting. Shunya had done careful work. Everyone was looking at Nil as if Nil ought to know what it meant, and the easiest thing in the world would have been to say *Yes, that's me, I'm made of those*, and give everyone the answer the work had earned.

They had done that once before. At a tea table in Bāfā, with a blurred suffix.

“Is it enough?” Nil asked instead. “The evidence. Is it enough to say what it is? Or is it only enough to say it lines up?”

Shunya stared at them. Then, slowly, she smiled, and for once it wasn't the quick, excited smile from the courtyard.

“That's the right question,” she said. “Vidya?”

“It lines up,” said Vidya. “Across independent sources, with the dates fixed before we looked. That's stronger than I expected. It does not tell us *why*.”

“It tells us the fractions went into one account that answers to all of them,” Shunya said. “It doesn't tell us how that account came to—” she glanced at Nil “—to talk. It doesn't tell us what's at the other end of the Tally, or who designed it, or whether anything else is involved. It doesn't tell us anything about gods. It's an accumulation. That's all I can show.”

“That's a lot,” said Jinling.

“It's a lot,” Shunya agreed. “It's not everything.”

Nil heard themself say it before they could soften it.

“Then tell me plainly. Not today, if you need to check. But plainly. Without any words I'll have to ask you to explain.”

Shunya held their gaze.

“Tomorrow,” she said. “I want to run it once more from the raw figures with Vidya, with the result covered, so neither of us can steer it. Then I'll tell you in one sentence.”

***

The surveyors came in the afternoon.

Nil heard them before they saw them: the creak of a heavy cart on the observatory road, and a sound that took a moment to place, the double clink of glass jars in brass cases, two of them, in step. They went to the gallery window.

Below, at the gate, Gopal and his crew had stopped work on the Great Dial and stood in a loose line across the path. Facing them was a cart painted in Basilica white, with a canvas cover and a brass instrument mounted on its back like a small telescope pointed at the ground. Four people in pale grey surveyors' coats climbed down from it. Behind them came two Collectors in the familiar rust-red coats, walking side by side, and between their belts, binding them together at the waist, ran a short length of brass chain.

In front of all of them walked a woman Nil had never seen before.

She was tall and square-built, in a Collector's coat cut longer than the others and faded almost pink at the shoulders by sun. Her hair was cropped short and grey at the temples. She carried no jar and no case. She had a clipboard, of all things, and a ring of keys on her belt, and the way the two linked Collectors kept half a step behind her and to either side made it obvious who was in charge.

Her Civic read 72.

“Captain,” Vesper said under her breath. “That's a captain's cut.”

“You know her?”

“I know the coat. Captains run linked pairs. You hit one Collector, the chain shares it with the other. They wear each other down slower.” Vesper's hand had gone to her holster without touching it. “And captains don't come out for one debt.”

At the gate the captain was reading something to Gopal off her clipboard. Nil couldn't hear the words. They could see Gopal's face, and the way the crew behind him shifted closer together.

“Reporting order,” said Vidya. He had come up behind them and was already pulling on his damp shawl. “They've come for the measurement logs. Excuse me. I'm going to be extremely slow and extremely polite.”

He went down. Through the window they watched him greet the captain with a small bow, accept her clipboard, read the first page, and begin to ask questions. Every so often he pointed at a line. Every so often he consulted a document of his own. The captain answered him with a patience that looked practised.

“She's not here for Nil,” Jinling said slowly. “Or she'd have come with a warrant, not surveyors.”

“The national claim's suspended in my name,” she added. “She can't execute it here without a new order. If she tries, she'll have to say so on paper.”

“She's looking up,” Hina said.

She was. While Vidya talked, the captain's eyes had left the clipboard and travelled up the face of the east tower, window by window, until they reached the gallery. Nil didn't step back. They didn't know why. They stood in the window with the flickering NaN over their head, and the captain looked at it for a long, level moment.

Then she touched two fingers to the brim of a cap she wasn't wearing and went back to her clipboard.

“That,” said Vesper, “was a professional saying *later*.”

***

Vidya came back an hour later, wet through and pleased with himself.

“The order covers measurements touching TR999,” he said. “I've given them the weather logs, which are voluminous and touch nothing, and told them the remainder are under active calibration and cannot be copied until the crew has finished cleaning the Dial, which, because of the frozen fee, has been delayed.” He sat down heavily on the gallery floor. “They'll be back. Their name is Brask. Captain Odile Brask, Meridian Collections, attached to the survey on the plateau. She has two linked officers, four surveyors and a great deal of patience. She asked after my health.”

“Is that a threat?” Maeve asked.

“I don't think so. I think she meant it.” He took the pencil from behind his left ear, found another behind his right, and looked at them both in mild confusion. “She also asked whether we'd noticed the rain.”

Nil turned. “The rain that picks fields?”

“She asked if it had got worse since the plateau machines started. I said yes. She wrote that down too.” Vidya frowned at the pencils. “I don't know what to make of a Collector who writes down what the weather is doing to poor people.”

Nil went back to the window. Down on the road the white cart was trundling away toward the plateau, the two linked Collectors walking beside it, the captain on the box with her clipboard on her knee. Beyond the city, on the high ground, Nil could just make out shapes against the clouds: a great tilted disc on a framework of brass, and beside it a long jointed arm like a crane, both turning so slowly that you could only see the movement if you looked away and back.

The grinding pulse they'd heard the first night drifted down over the roofs.

It was louder now.

In the gallery, Shunya had covered the trace with a sheet of paper and was laying out fresh pages for tomorrow's blind run. She looked up.

“Tomorrow,” she said again. “One sentence.”

Nil nodded and didn't trust themself to say anything.

## Chapter 26: But You Are Yours

Shunya asked to tell them in the quiet room, and asked who they wanted there.

Nil thought about it longer than they expected to. Then they said: Vesper, Maeve, Hina, Bai Si. Jinling, if she wanted. Not Vidya, not yet. Not anyone from the Faculty.

The quiet room was a small whitewashed cell off the courtyard where the observatory's night-watch slept in the hot season. It had a rope bed, a water jar and one window full of neem leaves. Shunya sat on the floor with her back against the wall and the covered sheets of the blind run on her knees. She had not slept. The ink on her cuffs had reached her wrists.

“We ran it twice,” she said. “Vidya marked one, I marked the other, with the dates covered. The results are the same. I'm going to say it in one sentence and then I'm going to stop talking, because I'll want to qualify it, and I promised you plainly.”

Nil nodded. Their mouth was dry.

“For three hundred years, the Dawn Tally has rounded away the fractions of people's allocations, and those fractions went into one unlisted account, and that account is you.”

She stopped.

It was very quiet. A pigeon walked along the window ledge, looked in, decided against it.

Nil had thought, during the night, about every way the sentence might come. They had imagined it hurting. They had imagined it being a relief. Neither was quite right. It felt like being told the name of a street they had already been living on.

“From everyone,” they said.

“From everyone the Tally rounded. Which is nearly everyone.”

“Ama. Kiran. Wen.”

“Their fractions. Not them.” Shunya's voice cracked on it, and she pressed her lips together and kept her promise and said nothing else.

Nil looked at their hands. The healed scar across the right palm. The brass ring at the wrist with the crack in it. They had always assumed the decoder was something done to them. Now they wondered if it was the only part of them that had been made on purpose.

“Does it say *why*?” Vesper asked. Her voice was rough. “Why it woke up. Why it walked out of wherever it was and asked for a job.”

“No,” said Shunya. “It doesn't say anything about that. Or about where the Tally sends things next, or who built it, or what else might be down there. I have no evidence about gods. I won't invent any.”

Maeve was sitting on the edge of the rope bed with her hands clasped between her knees, very still. Nil could see her thinking the way she thought about a broken hinge: what is it made of, what holds it, what could be done. They saw her catch herself doing it, and stop.

“I don't know what to say,” Maeve said finally. “I keep wanting to say something useful. I don't think there is anything useful.”

“That's all right,” said Nil.

Jinling had stood by the door the whole time with her fan closed. “Thousands of debts,” she said quietly. “Every one too small to bother collecting.” She looked at Nil, and something in her face was raw in a way Nil had not seen before. “My father would have called you the largest unclaimed balance in the country.”

“Would you?”

“No,” Jinling said. “I'd call you a person who was owed a great deal of change.”

Bai Si said nothing at all. He had his travelling book open on his knee, and after a moment Nil realised he was writing.

Hina was the last.

She had sat cross-legged at Nil's feet with her umbrella across her lap. The eye on the canopy was closed. When she looked up, her face was very serious.

“Hina was made from an umbrella,” she said. “Somebody's umbrella. Used every day for a hundred years. Hina is made of every time somebody held Hina over their head in the rain.” She considered. “Nobody asked the umbrella if it wanted to become Hina. And Hina doesn't belong to the people who held it.”

She reached up and put one small hand flat against Nil's knee.

“You're made of everyone,” she said. “But you're yours.”

Nil found that they couldn't speak. They put their hand over hers and kept it there.

After a while, Bai Si closed his book.

“I have written something,” he said. “I would like to show you, if you want to see it.”

He turned it round. On the page, under the dead-count extract's reference and the continuation he had added in the Ten Courts, he had written a single line in his careful hand:

*Wen, ferry-rope maker. Part of his remainder was carried forward. It went to someone who is still alive.*

“I was careful,” he said. “For a long time, I was careful. I thought a clean count would leave less room to lose someone.” He closed the book again. “I was wrong about where the room was.”

***

The Faculty's letter arrived at noon.

Vidya brought it himself, and he brought it unopened, and he held it out to Nil before he held it out to Shunya.

“It's addressed to her,” he said. “But it's about you. I think you should decide whether she reads it first.”

Nil opened it. It was short, on heavy paper with a seal.

The Faculty of the Great Observatory had received word of Scholar Shunya's comparison. Given the reporting order, the surveyors' presence and the extraordinary nature of the claim, the Faculty could only defend publication if the result were demonstrated publicly: the subject to appear before the full assembly, the cradle to be run in open session, the signature traced before witnesses including the surveyors, so that no one could later claim the figures were invented.

The demonstration was set for tomorrow, the day of the eclipse, when the city would be watching the observatory in any case.

Nil read it twice and handed it to Shunya. She read it, and went white, and then red.

“No,” she said. “Absolutely not. I didn't— that isn't what I asked them for. I asked for leave to circulate a bounded result.”

“It's what they want.”

“It's not what *you* agreed to.” She turned on Vidya. “You can tell them no.”

“I can tell them Nil said no,” said Vidya. “That's not the same. If Nil says no, the Faculty won't defend the result, and the surveyors will report that the observatory refused to demonstrate its claim. In Meridian that becomes 'the claim was false'.”

The quiet room was quiet again.

Nil sat with the letter in their lap. They could feel the old shape of it: the room waiting, everyone's careful work balanced on whether Nil would offer themself as the answer. A wrist on a cradle in front of the whole city. It wouldn't hurt. It hadn't hurt yesterday.

“No,” they said. “Not today. Not in front of an assembly. I'm not doing any more testing this afternoon.”

Shunya let out a breath.

“I'll tell them,” she said. “And Nil, you can still stay here. Nothing about the room changes. Nothing about the food. That isn't part of the bargain and it never was.”

“I know,” said Nil, and found that they did know. “Thank you.”

They got up and went out.

***

They climbed the Great Dial.

Gopal's crew had cleaned it that morning, and the great stone staircase that ran up its spine was still dark with wash-water. From the top Nil could see the whole city: the wet roofs, the chilli charms on the doors, the temple tanks, the stepwell's long pit. And on the plateau to the east, much closer from up here, the surveyors' machines.

The tilted disc was enormous: a plate of dark brass as wide as a house, mounted on a frame of girders and turning, with its face angled toward the sky. Beside it the jointed arm swung slowly back and forth, and at its tip something glinted like a lens. Thin cables ran down from both machines to a cluster of white tents. Even from here Nil could hear the grind.

Gopal had come up behind them, slow on the wet steps.

“Eclipse engines,” he said. “That's what the surveyors call them. The disc's meant to measure the sun. The arm follows it.” He spat over the edge, neatly. “Measure. Since they came, the Dial reads wrong at noon, my calibration rods drift, and the rain picks the fields of people with low numbers. Lakshmi's mother's field has been under a cloud for three weeks.”

“They're suppressing the readings,” Nil said slowly. “So the observatory can't measure anything independent.”

“That's what Vidya thinks. And the rain's the same machine doing something else, like a pump leaking.” Gopal shrugged. “The scholars argue about it. My crew just gets wet.”

Nil looked at the disc for a long time.

The Faculty wanted Nil's body on a cradle in front of the city, so that the result couldn't be denied. But the result wasn't really in Nil. It was in the ledgers, the stepwell, the deeds, the dead counts. What stopped anyone believing it was that the one place in the country with instruments independent of the Basilica couldn't take a clean reading, because two brass machines on a hill were leaning on the sky.

If the observatory could measure the eclipse tomorrow, unsuppressed, Vidya could publish his own figures, signed by his own crew. Not about Nil. About the sun, and the Tally's interference with it, and the rain.

Nobody would need Nil's wrist at all.

“Gopal,” Nil said, “who maintains those machines? Not the surveyors. Who actually climbs them?”

Gopal looked at them sidelong.

“Surveyors don't climb,” he said. “They hired local riggers. Two of them are my cousins.” He paused. “Why?”

“Because I don't want to be the demonstration,” Nil said. “I want to switch off the thing that makes one necessary.”

***

They told the others that evening, in the courtyard, and laid it out the way Meera had laid out the dive: what, who, how far, how they stop.

Maintenance access through Gopal's cousins. The suppressors were linked, so they had to come down together; one alone would just pass the load to the other. The surveyors would be there, and so would Captain Brask and her linked pair. Nobody would agree to a bounded review this time. If it came to a fight, it would be a real one.

“I'm not asking anyone to come,” Nil said. “I'm telling you what I'm going to do.”

Vesper laughed, short and incredulous. “Kid, you've been asking permission to breathe since Meridian. You're really going to make a plan without us?”

“I made it with Gopal.”

“Then make it with us too.” She held out her hand for the sketch. “Show me where the captain stands.”

Maeve looked at the drawing of the linked suppressors and frowned. “If they're linked by a load cable, you don't need to break both. You need to take the cable's load off at the same moment on both sides, so neither end has anywhere to dump it.”

“Like the bridge plates in the Ten Courts,” said Bai Si.

“Like the bridge plates.”

Jinling said she would go to the Faculty with Vidya and argue for time. Hina said she would go wherever Nil went, and that this was not a negotiation.

Shunya was the last to speak.

“I'll come,” she said. “The suppressors are instruments. I know instruments.” She hesitated. “And I owe you something that isn't a measurement.”

***

Later, when the courtyard had emptied, Nil went to the crew's workroom under the Dial, because Gopal had said something on the steps that afternoon that wouldn't leave them alone.

The crew kept a training frame there: three practice wheels on a wooden stand, connected to nothing, with a little scoring board and a crate of brass weights. Gopal used it to test new riggers' hands. Lakshmi was oiling it when Nil came in.

“He said you'd come,” she said. “He said you wanted to learn the column trick.”

“He said there was a trick.”

“There's always a trick.” She grinned and spun the first wheel. “Stop one. Any number.”

Nil stopped a 4.

“Now don't use it,” said Lakshmi. “Pretend the round's over. That four's spent. Except—” she reached over and slid a little brass marker along the scoring board, from the ones column into the tens “—you can carry it over. Not as four. As forty. Onto the first strike of the next round. Once. Then it's gone.”

Nil looked at the board. A four, moved one column to the left.

“Place value,” Lakshmi said. “Zero was born here, you know. That's what it's for. The same digit's worth more depending on where you put it.”

“Does it cost anything?”

“The digit you move doesn't count where it was. That's the cost.” She shrugged. “And you've got to choose before the next round starts. People always want to choose after.”

Nil spun the wheels again and stopped a 7. They looked at it. Then they slid the marker over, and watched the 7 become seventy on the board, waiting for the next first strike.

Nobody had measured anything. Nobody had asked for Nil's wrist. It was just a trick of where to put a number, learned on a wooden frame from someone who oiled it every night.

They practised until Lakshmi wanted to go home. Then they sat alone in the workroom for a while longer, moving the little marker back and forth between the columns.

Made of everyone. But theirs.

## Chapter 27: Brightness: Varies

They went up the plateau before dawn, in a rigger's cart, under a tarpaulin that smelled of tar and goats.

Gopal's cousins were brothers called Ravi and Suresh, and they had made it very clear they were not doing this for the scholars. “The disc pays us,” Ravi had said in the workroom. “It also puts a cloud over our aunt's field every afternoon. If you can switch it off without dropping it on anyone, we'll show you the maintenance ladders. If you drop it on anyone, we never met you.”

The cart creaked to a stop behind a stack of girders. Nil lifted the edge of the tarpaulin.

Up close the engines were bigger than anything Nil had ever stood under. The disc rose out of its scaffold like a second moon made of dark brass, tilted toward the paling sky, and as it turned it made the slow grinding pulse they'd heard from the city. Every revolution, a shiver of pressure passed over the plateau: not wind, something under the wind, a pull in the ears and the stomach like the moment before a stair you hadn't counted. The arm stood beside it on a jointed column, its long lens tracking a point in the sky where the sun would rise.

Between the two machines ran a single thick cable in a brass sleeve, humming.

“The load line,” Shunya whispered. She was lying flat under the tarpaulin beside Nil with her instrument case in her arms. “Whatever one does, the other takes up. If you break the disc, the arm pulls the whole charge and probably throws it somewhere you don't want.”

“Down the valley,” said Suresh, “onto Lakshmi's mother. We know.”

Maeve was already studying the cable's anchor points through a gap in the canvas. “Two junction boxes,” she murmured. “One at the foot of each machine. If both boxes are opened at the same moment and the load's taken into a dead sink, it has nowhere to go.”

“Same cycle,” said Bai Si. “Like the plates.”

“Same cycle.”

Before any of that, though, Vidya had insisted on one thing, and he'd made Nil promise it in front of everyone.

“Rubbings,” he'd said. “Of every maker's stamp on every frame. When the report goes out, I want it to say *machines built by a survey division and placed here by people*. Not *demons*, not *eclipse spirits*, not anything from a temple wall. People in this city will hear 'Rahu and Ketu' and think of something holy. I won't have our names attached to anyone killing a god.”

So the first thing they did, while the surveyors' camp was still asleep, was crawl along the foot of the disc's scaffold with Shunya's charcoal and thin paper and take rubbings. The stamps were everywhere, on every girder, every bolt plate: **BASILICA FOUNDRY · SURVEY DIVISION · OCCLUDER MK II · TR1001.** On the arm's column: **TRACKER MK II.** Someone had painted *RAHU* on the disc in white letters and *KETU* on the arm, crudely, as a joke or a nickname. Under the paint the foundry stamp was very clear.

Nil folded the rubbings into the oilcloth beside the Seals.

“People,” Shunya said softly. “Built by people. Nicknamed by people.”

“That's the point,” said Nil.

***

The surveyors' camp woke an hour after sunrise. The eclipse was due a little before noon.

The plan was simple, which Vesper said was the only kind of plan worth having. Ravi and Suresh would take Maeve and Nil up to the junction boxes as a maintenance crew, in borrowed riggers' smocks. Shunya would go with Maeve to the disc's box, Nil with Bai Si to the arm's. Vesper and Hina would stay low by the girder stack and watch the camp. Each box had a release lever and a load gauge. When both gauges read the same and the sun was at the right height, they'd pull together, the load would drop into the sink Maeve had rigged from a spare coil, and the engines would stop.

It went wrong in the usual way: not all at once, but in one small place first.

The arm's junction box was locked.

Not with a padlock. With a credential socket, a round brass mouth in the box's face exactly the size of the little office Seals in Nil's oilcloth. Above it, in neat engraving: **SURVEY CONTROL · EXCLUSIVE · DIGIT 0.**

“It's the office credential,” Bai Si said. “The region's monopoly key sits in the box. Nobody local can open it.”

“Then how do the riggers—”

“We don't,” said Suresh flatly. “The surveyors open it. With a key the captain carries.”

Somebody coughed politely behind them.

Captain Odile Brask stood at the foot of the arm's column with her clipboard under one arm and a ring of keys at her belt. Her two linked Collectors flanked her, the short brass chain swinging between their belts. In daylight, close to, she looked tired. There was mud on her boots up to the ankle.

“Good morning,” she said. “You're not my riggers.”

Nobody answered. Nil found they had put themself between the captain and Bai Si without deciding to.

Brask looked at the oilcloth bundle under Nil's arm, at the flickering characters above their head, and then, briefly, at the disc.

“I have a suspended national claim with your description on it,” she said. “I can't execute it without a fresh order, and I haven't got one. I'm not here for you.” She tapped the clipboard. “I'm here because the Comptroller's office pays for those machines, and I'm assigned to keep them running through the eclipse. If you're about to stop them, I have to stop you. That's all it is.”

“The rain,” Nil said. “You asked Vidya about the rain.”

Something moved in her face.

“I did.”

“It's these. They're suppressing the readings, and the load's leaking into the weather. It picks the poorest fields.”

“I've been told it's coincidence.”

“Have you believed it?”

Brask was quiet for a moment. Down the valley, a column of grey cloud was standing over a single field as it had every afternoon for three weeks.

“My orders don't ask me what I believe,” she said. She put her clipboard down very carefully on a girder. “I'm sorry. Bai Si, isn't it? Put your lanterns somewhere safe. This won't be a review.”

The linked Collectors stepped apart, as far as the chain allowed, and opened their cases.

***

There was no boundary rising out of the ground this time. No printed stake, no release bar, no witness copy. The first Collector's jar flared and a hooked blade of pressure came across the scaffold at knee height, and Nil threw themself flat and felt it go over their back like a door slammed shut an inch away. It took a girder strut clean off behind them. The strut fell twenty feet and rang on the rock below like a bell.

The decoder at Nil's wrist was already hot.

They stopped the wheel that rose in front of them on instinct: a 6. The second: a 6. The third went past fast and they missed and stopped a 2.

Pair. Their strike went into the nearer Collector, and he staggered. Then the chain between the two officers lit, and the second Collector staggered too, and half the force bled away between them.

“Linked,” Vesper had said. *They wear each other down slower.*

The second Collector answered. This time Bai Si was there, his great blade unslung for the first time since Nil had met him, the flat of it raised; the hooked pressure struck the steel and slid off with a shriek. He went back two steps and his heel found the scaffold edge and held.

“They share everything,” he said through his teeth. “Damage. Recovery. Like the plates.”

Below them, by the girder stack, a gun went off.

Vesper's shot didn't touch either Collector. It hit the chain between them, square in the middle, with a sound like a struck anvil. The chain held. But it jumped, and for a moment the light along it flickered out.

“Again!” Nil shouted, and stopped a 3, and a 4, and the third wheel came round and they forced their hand to wait, wait—

5.

Three, four, five. A Cascade. The strike went into the first Collector while the chain was dark, and nothing shared it. He went down on one knee with a grunt. The Cascade gave Nil another round before anyone could answer, and they used it, and for the first time in a fight they did the thing Lakshmi had taught them on the wooden frame: they took the 5 from the last stop and slid it one column over in their mind, not as five but as fifty, onto the first strike of the next round.

The next round's first wheel stopped on 7. The decoder added the carried fifty to it, and the strike that left Nil's hand was so much heavier than anything they'd thrown before that it knocked the second Collector off his feet entirely and dragged the first after him by the chain.

Vesper fired a second time. This shot hit the chain's clasp at the second officer's belt, and the clasp sprang.

The chain fell slack between two men lying on the scaffold, panting, unhurt, out of the fight.

Brask had not moved.

She stood by the girder with her hands empty at her sides. She looked at her officers, and then at Nil, and then at Vesper down by the stack reloading nothing, because there was nothing in her pocket to reload with.

“Three left,” Vesper called up. “In case anyone's counting.”

“I'm counting,” said Brask. She picked up her clipboard.

***

The eclipse began at eleven.

On the scaffold nobody said anything. Brask stood with her clipboard; her officers sat against a girder, rubbing their wrists. Nobody had decided anything, but nobody was fighting.

The sky went the colour of tin. Shadows sharpened. The pigeons that had followed the riggers' cart settled on the girders all at once, as though somebody had told them it was evening.

And the disc began to turn faster.

It was tracking the sun now, swinging its great face into line, and the grinding pulse rose to a shriek. The pressure in Nil's ears became pain. Down the valley the grey column over Lakshmi's mother's field thickened and darkened and began to spread, sideways, onto the next field and the one after, and Nil understood that this was what the machine did at the moment it mattered most: it leaned on the sky with all its weight, and the weight had to go somewhere.

“Box!” Maeve shouted from the disc's foot. “Now or never, Nil. Same cycle!”

The arm's box was still sealed. Credential 0, exclusive.

Nil looked at Brask.

She looked back at them for a long moment. Then she unhooked a small brass key ring from her belt, chose a key, and held it out over the edge of the girder: a round office credential, stamped 0.

“My orders say keep them running through the eclipse,” she said. “The eclipse is now. I've written down what they're doing to the valley. If I hand you this, it's because the machines are faulty, and I'm reporting that.” Her voice didn't change. “Be quick.”

Nil took it. The credential seated in the socket with a soft click, and the box swung open.

Inside: a lever and a gauge. The gauge needle sat at the top of its arc, quivering.

Across the plateau, at the disc's foot, Shunya held up one hand.

“Watch the needle,” Bai Si said quietly, beside Nil. “Not her. Not me. The needle.”

The sun narrowed to a crescent. The light went grey-green. On both gauges, the needles swung, and for one moment they came level.

Nil pulled.

So did Maeve.

There was a sound like a vast breath let out. The cable between the machines went dark along its whole length, and the load dropped into Maeve's coil at the far end with a flash that left spots in Nil's eyes. The disc's shriek wound down into a groan. The arm stopped swinging and hung, lens pointed at nothing.

Down the valley, the grey column over Lakshmi's mother's field thinned, and frayed at the edges, and broke apart into ordinary clouds that went on drifting east with all the others.

In the arm's junction box, the credential socket clicked again, and the round office Seal slid out into Nil's palm, warm. Below it the engraving had changed. Someone, long ago, had built it to do that: **SURVEY CONTROL · RELEASED.**

***

Vidya and Gopal's crew measured the rest of the eclipse from the Great Dial, unsuppressed, with every rod freshly calibrated.

Nil watched from the scaffold as the moon slid off the face of the sun, and the light came back, and the pigeons lifted off the girders and went home. They found that they were shaking and couldn't stop.

Shunya climbed up from the disc's foot. She sat down beside Nil without asking and put her instrument case between her feet.

“Vidya's going to publish the eclipse,” she said. “Under his name and the crew's. Timings, brightness, the suppression before and after. He's already written the first line.” She laughed weakly. “'Brightness: varies.' He says that's the most honest sentence in astronomy.”

“What about your paper?”

“Mine waits.” She looked at the case. “I want to ask you something, and you can say no, and it won't change anything.”

“Go on.”

“The equation. The comparison. The failed model and the corrected one.” She took a breath. “I'd like to publish it with you as a co-author. Restricted. Your name only if you want it, the decoder signature only in the form you approve, withdrawable if you change your mind. And I want to put the failure first. The timing test. And the part we can't explain yet: the scaling. My corrected model works for small, consenting, defined claims. I have no idea if it works for a country. I want that written on the first page.”

Nil thought about it. About the cradle, the ripples, the dates. About Kiran's fraction and Wen's knot.

“Not yet,” they said. “Ask me again when it's finished. With the first page on top.”

“Each time,” Shunya said, and smiled.

***

Captain Brask came over last, when the camp was packing.

She had her clipboard and her two officers, the chain coiled over one of their shoulders. She stopped a respectful distance away.

“I'll be filing my report tonight,” she said. “Survey equipment fault during eclipse. Load leakage into local weather. Credential surrendered to prevent harm. Observatory measurements now independent.” She paused. “It'll go to Meridian. It'll be read.”

“By the Comptroller?”

“Everything about you is read by the Comptroller.” She looked at the NaN above Nil's head with an expression Nil couldn't place. “You hit hard, for a debt.”

“I'm not a debt.”

“No,” said Brask, after a moment. “I'm beginning to see that's the problem.”

She touched two fingers to the brim of the cap she wasn't wearing, and went down to her cart.

Nil sat on the scaffold until the shaking stopped, with four Seals in the oilcloth now, each wrapped separately: one, nine, eight, and zero.

## Chapter 28: An Order Accelerated

Shunya made the protected copy in the dispatch room, with the door open, so that anyone passing could see what went into it.

The dispatch room was where the observatory sent its catalogues into the world: a long bench, a press for sealing wax, racks of oilcloth sleeves and a window onto the courtyard where couriers waited with their mules. Vidya had cleared the bench. Shunya laid the pages out in order and read the order aloud before she bound them.

“First page,” she said. “The failure. Equal-quantity model, stepwell test, two equal pulses, eleven seconds apart. Rejected. Retained.” She set it down. “Second: the corrected model, for a defined class of claims, with its uncertainty stated in figures. Third: the comparison across four independent sources, with the source limits written beside each column. Fourth.” She paused over it. “The warning.”

She read it slowly.

*This model has been tested only on inert instruments and small, bounded, consenting claims. It has not been tested at scale. It must not be used to redistribute allocations across a population, or to force any account into another, or to treat any person as a reservoir. Anyone who uses it as though it were proven at scale is using it wrongly.*

“That's strong,” said Jinling, reading over her shoulder.

“It's meant to be.” Shunya picked up the pen. “Last page. Signatures. Mine. Vidya's as supervising observer. Nil's, if Nil wants, as the subject of the decoder signature, consenting to this copy and only this copy.”

Nil read all five pages before they signed. Then they signed the last one, under Shunya's name, and wrote beside it in small letters: *for this copy*.

Shunya sealed the sleeve twice.

“Once for the weather,” she said, “and once for the people who'll open it.” She glanced at Nil. “Meera told me that. I asked if I could use it.”

“What did she say?”

“She said Kiran would want royalties.”

The equation went into Shunya's own pack, not the dispatch rack. It would travel with her. That had been decided the night before, at length, with several of Jinling's clauses: Shunya was its keeper, Nil's consent rode with it, and neither of them could hand it to anyone else without the other.

***

Vidya's eclipse report went out in the dispatch rack, in twelve copies.

It was short. It gave the timings of the eclipse to the second, the brightness of the corona at five points, the readings of the Great Dial before and after the plateau engines stopped, and the weather in the valley for three weeks, field by field, with the names of the farmers whose fields had been under the cloud. At the bottom, under Vidya's signature, every member of Gopal's crew had signed or made a mark, and under that was a single line in Gopal's square handwriting: *Calibrated by hand. Paid.*

Attached to each copy was a set of charcoal rubbings: **BASILICA FOUNDRY · SURVEY DIVISION.** Built by people. Nicknamed by people.

“Where are they going?” Nil asked.

“Everywhere that will take them,” said Vidya. “The River board. The Hyakki court. Ofuku. Enma's archive. Judge Cui. Two to Meridian, one to the Wards desk and one to the Comptroller, because if he's going to read about it, I'd rather he read my version first.” He tied the last sleeve. “And one to the Kroton academy, because they have instruments too, and someone there should know theirs can be leaned on.”

He held the Kroton sleeve a moment longer than the others.

“The Kroton academy relayed something this morning,” he said. “You should see it.”

***

It was a relay transcript of a printed public notice, the kind posted on walls. A colleague at the Kroton academy had sent it with a note at the bottom: *Vidya, is this connected to your surveyors?*

**PUBLIC ORDER: KROTON NOVA, ACCELERATED SCHEDULE.**

**Under the Comptroller's stabilisation programme (Even Hand), the annual Sieve of the city's residents is brought forward. The Sieve of residents under sixteen years will take place in the Terraces on the third day after this order is posted. Families are reminded that ranking is final for the year and determines terrace residence, school placement and protected-route access.**

Nil read it twice. The second time the words *under sixteen* stood out from the page as if they had been printed in red.

“The Sieve,” Bai Si said. “I have heard of it. They rank everyone. The lowest are moved down the terraces.”

“Children,” said Hina.

“Children,” Vidya agreed quietly. “Brought forward by eleven days. The academy says the order arrived the morning after our engines went quiet.”

Jinling took the notice and read the bottom line, where the issuing office was printed.

“The Comptroller,” she said. “Not the Kroton council. The Comptroller's own programme. Even Hand.” She looked up. “That's the name from Ivo's copies. The stabilisation schedule.”

Nil took out Nought's notebook and found the page where they had copied the archive reference from Bāfā: the rejected TR999 proposal, Ivo Marr's handwriting, *regional emergency pools with shared oversight*.

“He had an option,” they said. “Thale did. Ivo wrote it down twenty-odd years ago. Regional pools. Shared oversight. Appeals outside his office.” They looked at the notice. “And instead he's making it faster.”

“Because we stopped his machines,” said Shunya. Her voice was very small. “Because of my comparison.”

“Because he decided to,” Nil said. “We stopped two machines that were pouring rain on people. He decided what to do next.”

Shunya didn't answer, but after a moment she nodded.

***

That night Jinling spread a map across the guest courtyard's flagstones, weighted with the clay chai cups, and they decided where to go next.

There were other routes. The road north led back toward the River and the board, where Jinling had work waiting. The road west led to the Crossroads, where Vesper's past was waiting whether she liked it or not. The road east led over the hills to Kroton Nova, which was the furthest, and the most expensive, and had a date printed on it.

“Three days,” said Maeve. “From this morning.”

“A day and a night by the paid caravan if the passes are clear,” said Jinling. “Two if not.”

“So we'd have a day there. Maybe two.”

“Maybe one.”

Nil looked at the map for a long time. Nobody told them what to choose. They noticed that, and noticed that it didn't frighten them.

“Kroton,” they said. “Not because it's where the next Seal is. Because there's a date on it, and children, and we can still get there before it.”

Vesper nodded. “Kroton.”

“Kroton,” said Hina, and Maeve, and Bai Si.

Shunya hesitated, and then said, “I'm coming. The academy has instruments. If Even Hand is leaning on them the way it leaned on ours, someone needs to measure it.”

Jinling was already writing. Not a plan; a letter. Several letters. “The councils,” she said, when Nil looked over. “Bāfā's board, the Hyakki court, the River witnesses, Vidya's Faculty if they'll listen. I'm asking them to consider something. Not to agree to it. To consider it.” She read aloud as she wrote. “*Given the evidence enclosed, and the Comptroller's acceleration of his programme, we ask each council to consider whether it would support a bounded, witnessed, revocable transition of emergency allocation to regional pools with shared oversight, along the lines of a proposal the Comptroller's own office drafted in TR999 and rejected.*” She looked up. “They'll reply by courier. Ten days, maybe. Some will say no.”

“Some should say no,” said Nil. “If they all say yes, we haven't asked properly.”

Jinling looked at them, startled, and then laughed.

They paid the caravan master that night: seven places to Kroton, provisions for two days, an extra charge for the passes. Meera came to the courtyard while they were counting it out, with her rope over her shoulder and Kiran's record in its tube on a cord round her neck.

She didn't say goodbye. She looked at Nil, and at the NaN, and said, “Eat something on the road,” and gave Hina a bag of roasted chickpeas, and left.

Lakshmi came later with a small wooden frame no bigger than a hand, three tiny wheels and a sliding brass marker, and gave it to Nil without a word, and ran.

***

In Meridian, Ivo Marr brought the Śūnyapura report up with the evening post.

There were two versions in the satchel. Captain Brask's came first, on Collections paper, in a square, careful hand: *Survey equipment fault during eclipse. Load leakage into local weather. Credential surrendered to prevent harm.* Under it, Brask had attached a page that nobody had asked for: a list of the fields in the valley that had been under the cloud, and for how long, and the Civic numbers of the people who farmed them.

The second version was Vidya Rao's, with the rubbings.

Thale read Brask's first. He read it standing at the window, where the pigeons had gone in for the night, and when he reached the list of fields he stopped for a long time.

“She surrendered the credential,” he said.

“To prevent harm, sir. She says.”

“She's probably right.” Thale turned the page. “The occluder was leaking. I was told it wasn't.” He set the report down very square to the edge of the desk, the way he set everything down. “Send the valley a relief allocation from the protected reserve. Quietly. Name the fields she lists. Nobody needs to know where it came from.”

Ivo wrote that down. His pen hesitated over *quietly*, and he wrote it anyway.

Then Thale picked up Vidya's report, and the rubbings, and the twelve signatures of the instrument crew, and *Calibrated by hand. Paid.*

He looked at that for a while too.

“They're publishing,” he said. “Independent measurements. Signed. Circulated to every council.” He didn't sound angry. He sounded like a man reading a weather forecast. “Within a month, every region will have figures that don't come from this office. Within two, someone will say those figures prove the Tally is leaking. Within three, a storm will come and every council will want to manage its own reserve, and the reserves will not be where they need to be, and a street somewhere will flood.”

“Sir, the proposal from TR999—”

“Would have taken years, Ivo. I don't have years. Neither do they.” Thale drew the Even Hand binder toward him and opened it at the schedule. “Kroton is the furthest along. Bring their Sieve forward. If the terraces are ranked and settled before the councils start arguing, at least one city will be stable when it starts.”

Ivo's pen didn't move.

“The Sieve of minors, sir.”

“The Sieve of residents. It's always included minors.” Thale didn't look up. “Draft the order. I'll sign it tonight.”

Ivo drafted it. He wrote it in the clearest, most exact language he knew, every date and clause where it belonged, and it came out of him as smoothly as everything he had drafted for twenty years. When he had finished, Thale read it, corrected one comma and signed.

Ivo took the signed order down to the printing office himself. He waited while they set it.

Then he went back to his own desk, took out a fresh sheet and wrote out the whole order again, word for word, in his own hand. At the bottom he added the date, the hour, the name of the man who had signed it, and his own name as the man who had drafted it.

He put the copy in the same drawer as the others. It was getting full.
