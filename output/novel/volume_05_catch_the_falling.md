# ZERO-SUM: OMEN

# The Remainder

## Volume Five: Catch the Falling

## Chapter 29: The Even Prime

Kroton Nova looked like a city someone had poured through a strainer and kept only the parts that didn't fall.

It stood on a white headland over a sea so blue that Nil kept looking away from it and back again to check. The city went up in terraces, ring above ring, each one a little narrower than the last, all of them cut from the same pale stone and pierced with holes. Round holes, square holes, holes in neat repeating patterns, so that from the road the whole hill looked like lace. Through the holes in the upper terraces you could see the terraces below. Through the holes in the lowest terraces you could see nothing at all, only darkness, and a smell came up out of them of smoke and hot metal and people living close together.

“The Sieve,” Shunya said quietly. “It's an instrument too. It sorts.”

“Sorts what?”

“Everyone.”

They had come over the pass in the dark and down the coast road at dawn, seven of them in a paid caravan that smelled of olives. At the city gate a woman in white was waiting for them.

She was tall and very straight, in a white tunic and greaves of pale bronze, with a round shield on her back so polished that Nil could see their own face in it, small and curved. Her hair was cut short and square. On the shield's face, inlaid in blue enamel, was a single numeral: 2. Above her head her Civic read 02.

Behind her stood six Synod guards in white, and behind them a goat.

“You are the travellers from Śūnyapura,” the woman said. She had the voice of someone reading aloud from a text she knew by heart. “I am Prima of the Synod. By order of the Archon under the Comptroller's stabilisation programme, persons without a ranked Civic number, and persons travelling with them, are excluded from the upper terraces until the Sieve has completed. You will be escorted to a holding residence. You will be fed. You will not be harmed.” She paused. “The exclusion is temporary.”

Vesper laughed, not kindly. “They always are.”

Prima's eyes went to her, then back to Nil, then up to the flickering characters over Nil's head. Something moved behind her composure, and was put away.

“The exclusion is temporary,” she said again. “Please follow me.”

***

They were prisoners. Nobody pretended otherwise, but Prima ran their captivity with a thoroughness that was almost courteous. The guards walked on either side, not behind. Their packs stayed with them. Maeve's tool bundle was counted, listed and returned. When Hina's umbrella opened its eye at a guard who reached for it, Prima said, “Leave that,” before anyone else could speak, and the guard left it.

The road climbed through the terraces by long ramps. Nil looked through the holes in the walls as they passed.

On the upper terraces there were olive trees in tubs, children in white tunics doing sums on slates in a shaded courtyard, a fountain that ran into a basin carved with numbers. Every door had a blue glass bead hanging above it, like an eye. Each household had its Civic number painted on the lintel, and every one was prime: 31, 37, 41, 53. Nil couldn't find a 4 or a 6 anywhere.

“Composites live lower,” Prima said, seeing where they were looking. She said it the way someone would say *the bakery is on the next street*. “Each ring holds those whose numbers can be divided by fewer factors. The Sieve does not judge character. Only structure.”

“Structure,” said Jinling.

“A prime cannot be broken into smaller whole parts. A composite can. The Synod holds that a city is stronger when its upper rings rest on what cannot be broken.”

“And the people whose numbers can be broken?”

“Are housed. Fed. Employed.” Prima's jaw tightened very slightly. “Lower.”

Nil looked through the next hole. Below this terrace was another, smaller and darker, with washing strung across it. Below that, another. Below that, nothing but the dark and the smell of hot metal.

They didn't ask about the bottom. Not yet.

The holding residence was a whitewashed house on the fourth terrace with seven beds, a jug of water, a bowl of figs, and a guard at the door. Prima showed them each room as though they were guests. In the kitchen she checked that the stove drew properly. In the yard she pointed out the cistern and how much it held.

“There is a shelter under this terrace,” she said, “for storms. The steps are behind the yard door. In a storm you go down whether or not you are excluded.” She looked at them. “Everyone goes down.”

“That's decent of you,” said Maeve, meaning it.

“It is the Synod's,” said Prima. “I did not build it.” She hesitated. “I maintain it. I know the roster of everyone who sleeps on this terrace. I count them down the steps when the sea comes in.”

It was the first thing she had said that didn't sound recited.

***

The goat came back in the afternoon.

Nil was sitting in the yard with the little wooden frame Lakshmi had given them, sliding its brass marker between the columns, when a scrabbling on the terrace wall made them look up. A brown goat with one white ear was standing on the top of the wall, which was not wide enough for a goat, chewing a fig it had clearly stolen from somewhere. It looked at Nil with the calm of an animal that had never once considered consequences.

A moment later a boy appeared on the wall behind it, scrambling, out of breath.

“Don't move,” he said. “If you move she'll think it's a game.”

Nil didn't move.

The boy was perhaps ten, thin and brown with a crop of black curls and a sling of rope over one shoulder. His Civic read 15. He edged along the wall toward the goat with his arms out.

“Persephone,” he said firmly. “Come here. Now. I mean it.”

Persephone finished the fig and stepped off the far side of the wall.

Nil was on their feet. The far side of the wall was not the yard. It was the edge of the terrace, and below the edge, through the pierced stone, the next terrace was a long way down.

There was a scrape of hooves and a bleat and then nothing.

The boy went white. He was already climbing over after her, and Nil reached him just in time to catch the back of his tunic.

“Wait,” Nil said. “Let me see.”

They looked over together.

The goat was standing on a narrow ledge about two body-lengths down, where a section of the terrace's outer face had sheared away and left a lip of stone. She looked entirely unconcerned. Below her, the lip ended in a jagged edge, and below that was a drop through the Sieve's holes into the dark.

The terrace face, Nil saw now, was cracked all along that section. Old cracks, filled with mortar that had dried and shrunk. One of the pierced holes had split into the next one, making a single long gap.

“That's new,” said the boy. His voice wobbled. “That wasn't there in spring. I take her down that way to the low pasture and it wasn't there.”

“What's your name?”

“Niko.”

“Niko, don't climb.” Nil turned. “Maeve!”

It was Maeve and the guard and, in the end, Prima, who came at a run when the guard called her. Maeve looked at the crack for about four seconds and said a word Nil had never heard her use. Prima looked at it for longer.

“This section was inspected in the spring,” she said.

“By who?” asked Maeve.

Prima opened her mouth and shut it. Then she unslung the shield from her back.

She didn't climb. She lay flat on the terrace and lowered the shield, held by its strap, until its rim was level with the goat's ledge. Persephone looked at it with interest. Niko, lying flat beside Prima, held out the remains of a fig. The goat stepped onto the shield, and Prima lifted her up with a single long pull of her arms, and set her down in the yard, and stood up and brushed the dust off her tunic.

Niko flung his arms round the goat's neck. The goat ate his collar.

“Thank you,” he said into her fur. Then, to Prima, “It was cracked. I *told* them it was cracked. At the spring inspection. They said composite children don't report structural defects, they report to their terrace warden.”

Prima's face did something complicated.

“Who is your terrace warden?”

“There isn't one any more. He got moved down.”

Prima looked at the crack again. Then she took a stub of chalk from her belt and drew a line along it, and a date beside it, and her own mark: a small square 2.

“This will be reported,” she said.

“By you?”

“By me.”

***

That evening Nil asked her about appeal.

She had come to check the guard and the water and to count them, which she did twice, under her breath. Nil waited until she had finished.

“If the exclusion's temporary,” they said, “is there a way to ask for it to end sooner? An appeal. Like the review desks in the River.”

Prima considered. She sat down on the edge of the yard's low wall, her shield across her knees, and for a moment she looked very young.

“There is an appeal,” she said. “To the Archon's access court. It sits after the Sieve has completed. You submit a petition through a terrace warden, who submits it through the temple, which schedules it.”

“After the Sieve.”

“After.”

“And if the person appealing hasn't got a terrace warden?”

Prima was quiet.

“Then they submit through the undercity liaison office,” she said finally. “Which no longer sits. Its last liaison was reassigned.”

“Reassigned where?”

“Down.” She looked at her hands on the shield's rim. “There is another route. Through the undercity itself. The workers' council down there keeps its own petitions and sends them up the maintenance shafts. The temple does not recognise them, but the Archon has to receive them, by an old rule. If you went down there, they might carry yours.”

“You could take us.”

“I could escort prisoners to another holding place for their own safety, if this terrace were found to be structurally unsound.” She glanced at the chalk line on the wall. “Which, as of this afternoon, I am obliged to report that it is.”

Nil stared at her. She didn't look up.

“You know the route,” they said. “You know the people down there.”

“I know the route.” Her voice was very level. “The people know me. That is not the same thing.” She stood up and slung the shield on her back. “There are people in the undercity I have turned away. At the stairs. With an exclusion order in my hand that I believed was temporary. If you go down with me, you will meet them, and they will tell you what I did, and they will be right.”

She went to the gate.

“We go at first light,” she said without turning round. “Bring the goat. Niko will not leave without her, and he knows the way better than I do.”

## Chapter 30: The City Beneath

The undercity began where the stairs stopped being white.

Prima led them down a maintenance shaft at the back of the fourth terrace, a spiral of narrow steps cut into the stone, and for the first few turns everything was the same pale marble as the city above. Then the steps were patched with grey. Then the grey was patched with iron. Then the walls were brick, and the brick was hung with pipes, and the pipes were sweating, and there was light coming up from below that wasn't daylight. Lamplight. Forge-light. The warm orange light of a great many small fires.

Niko went first with the goat on a rope. He took the steps two at a time and knew without looking which ones were loose.

“Seventeen, skip,” he called back. “Twenty-three, skip. Thirty-one is fine but it wobbles.”

“Primes,” said Shunya behind Nil. “He's skipping the primes.”

“Not on purpose,” said Niko. “They just break first. People up top stand on them for luck.”

The shaft opened into a cavern, and Nil stopped on the last step and forgot to breathe.

It was enormous. The whole underside of the Sieve hung overhead like the inside of a vast stone honeycomb, the holes letting down thin columns of daylight from the terraces above. Under it, filling every space between the great pillars that held the city up, was another city. Houses built from scrap. Workshops in arches. Walkways strung between pillars on cables. Markets in the hollows. Washing. Smoke. Children. And everywhere, on every pillar, every girder, every support, people were working: bracing, bolting, patching, oiling, hauling.

The city above was standing on this.

“Composite,” said Prima quietly. “That's what they call it up there. The undercity of the composite.”

“And down here?” asked Nil.

Prima didn't answer.

***

Kallisto met them in a workshop under the third great pillar.

She was a broad grey-haired woman in a leather apron, with forearms like hawsers and a pencil behind each ear, and when they came in she was standing on a scaffold with her hand flat against a crack in the pillar's base, feeling it the way Maeve felt a hinge. She didn't come down.

“Niko,” she said. “You brought Persephone back. Good. Who are the others?”

“Travellers,” said Niko. “From the River. And Śūnyapura. And one of them's got a broken number.”

Kallisto looked at Nil, and at the NaN, and then she looked past them all at Prima.

She came down the scaffold then, slowly, and stood in front of Prima with her arms folded. She was shorter than Prima by a head. It didn't seem to matter.

“Shield-bearer,” she said.

“Kallisto.”

“You've been down here before.”

“Twice. To inspect the shelters.”

“Three times,” Kallisto said. “The third time was the stairs at the eighth pillar, two winters ago, in the storm. You stood at the top with an order. A family came up from the flooded lower galleries. Andreas and Ione Kostas and their two girls. Eleven and seven. You read them the order and you didn't let them through.”

The workshop had gone quiet. Two men at a lathe had stopped turning it.

Prima's face didn't move. Nil watched her hands. They had closed on the strap of her shield.

“They were composite residents of the eleventh ring,” Prima said. “The order excluded unranked passage during storm evacuations, to prevent crowding on the upper shelters. It was temporary.”

“It was a storm,” said Kallisto. “The younger girl got pneumonia in the eighth-pillar galleries waiting for the water to drop. She lived. She coughs every winter. Ione lost the use of her left hand from the cold.”

Prima said nothing.

“I'm not telling you so you'll say sorry,” Kallisto said. “I'm telling you so these travellers know who's leading them.” She looked at Nil. “You understand? She's not your friend because she brought you down the stairs. She's an officer of the Synod who's decided, today, to do something different. Maybe she'll keep deciding. Maybe she won't.”

“I understand,” said Nil.

Prima spoke then, in a voice Nil had not heard her use. Not recited. Not level. It came out rough, as if the words had to be dragged.

“I believed it was temporary,” she said. “I believed the order would end when the storm ended, and that the family would be housed after. They were. I checked the roster after.” She stopped. “I checked the roster. I didn't go and see them.”

“No,” said Kallisto. “You didn't.”

“I can't mend that by having believed it.”

“No.” Kallisto unfolded her arms. “But you can carry a crate. There's a support at the fourteenth pillar that wants bracing before the next tide, and we're three people short.” She turned away. “The rest of you, if you want something from the council, you can carry crates too. We'll talk while we work.”

***

They carried crates.

The undercity's supports were not the clean marble columns of the city above. They were the original pillars, ancient and cracked, wrapped and braced and buttressed with every kind of material the undercity could scavenge: iron bands, timber shores, brass collars, cable. The workers kept a ledger for each pillar, chalked on boards hung at its base: what had been added, when, by whom, and how much load it took.

Nil carried bolts for a crew under the fourteenth pillar, and listened.

The Sieve above held its shape because the undercity held it up. Every ring of the official city, every prime household on its terrace, every olive tree in a tub, rested on supports that had been maintained, for generations, by people whose numbers could be divided.

“The Synod's engineers come down once a year,” a woman with a bad leg told Nil, tightening a collar. “They measure the pillars. They write the measurements in the temple archive. Then they go back up and the archive says the city is structurally sound. They don't write down who made it sound.”

“Does the Archon know?”

“The Archon,” she said, “gives lectures about it.”

Across the fourteenth pillar Maeve had stopped work entirely. She was crouching by an old brace at the pillar's foot, a great toothed wheel of black iron half-buried in the stone, turning it a quarter-turn this way and that and frowning.

“This is a gear,” she said when Nil came over. “It's not a brace. Someone's used it as a brace, but it's a gear. From a machine.” She ran her thumb along the teeth. “Look at the cut. That's the same tooth profile as the old ward frames in Ofuku's drawer. As my grandmother's supplier mark.” She looked up. “This came out of whatever the Sieve was before it was a city.”

“The workers use parts like that everywhere,” said Kallisto, who had come up behind them. “There's a whole gallery of them under the temple. The upper city threw them down when they rebuilt the rings. We use them because they're good iron.”

“Can I see?”

“After the brace.”

***

They finished the brace an hour after noon.

On the way back along the walkway, a woman was waiting at the foot of the fourteenth pillar with a basket of bread on her right arm. Her left hand hung at her side in a knitted glove, and she held it a little away from her body, the way people hold something that doesn't work properly and still hurts.

Kallisto saw her and stopped. So did Prima.

“Ione,” said Kallisto.

The woman looked at Prima for a long time. Prima didn't move. Nil saw her straighten, the way she had at the city gate, as if bracing for a strike.

“I heard you were down here,” Ione Kostas said. “Carrying crates.”

“Yes.”

“My daughter's at the school. The little one.” Ione's voice was quite level. “She coughs. Every winter. You'll hear her, if you're down here long enough.”

“I—” Prima began.

“Don't.” Ione shifted the basket on her arm. “I don't want your apology today. I don't know if I'll want it ever. I wanted to see your face, that's all. I've seen it.” She turned to go, and then turned back. “The brace on the fourteenth. Did you hold the collar or turn the bolts?”

Prima blinked. “Held the collar.”

“Hm.” Ione looked at the brace, at the new iron, as critically as Maeve would have. “It's straight,” she said, and left.

Prima stood looking after her until Kallisto touched her arm.

“That's more than she's said to anyone from the Synod in two years,” Kallisto said. “Don't make it into anything.”

“I won't.”

Then Kallisto brought them to the council.

It met in a disused cistern under the ninth pillar, a round brick room with benches cut into the walls and a single lamp hanging in the middle. Twenty people sat around it: forge workers, cable riggers, a schoolteacher, a goatherd who turned out to be Niko's uncle, three old women who ran the market. They listened to Nil's petition: the exclusion, the appeal, the request to be heard by the Archon before the Sieve rather than after.

Then the schoolteacher, a thin young man called Demetrios with chalk on his sleeves, said: “We have a bigger problem than your petition.”

He laid a paper on the bench. It was an order, sealed, in the temple's white wax, and it had been delivered to his school that morning.

Nil read it.

**SCHOOL TRANSFER ORDER. Under the accelerated schedule, pupils of the undercity schools of the ninth to fourteenth pillars will be transferred to the Sieve examination hall on the upper terrace on the morning of the Sieve, for ranking. Transfer route: the eleventh-ring span. Escort: Synod guard. Families need not attend.**

Nil read it again.

“The eleventh-ring span,” Prima said slowly. “That's the bridge across the inner Sieve.”

“It's the bridge I told you about,” said Niko, very quietly. “By the crack. The low pasture way. It's under the crack.”

Kallisto had gone very still.

“That span hasn't been braced since spring,” she said. “We've been asking the temple to close it since spring. The engineers measured it and said it was sound.”

“How many children?” Nil asked.

“Forty-one,” said Demetrios. “From six to fifteen. All the pupils I have.”

Prima stood up.

She stood in the middle of the cistern with her shield on her back and her face very white, and Nil saw her look at the order, and at Kallisto, and at the lamp, and at Niko, and they understood that she was reading the order in two ways at once. As the officer who might have to carry it out. And as someone who had counted families up the shelter steps in a storm.

“The Sieve is tomorrow,” she said.

“Tomorrow,” said Demetrios.

“An appeal to the access court sits *after* the Sieve,” Prima said. “If the petition goes up tonight, it won't be heard until the children have already crossed.”

Nobody spoke.

“Then we don't let them cross,” said Nil.

“It's an order of the Archon.” Prima's voice cracked on it. “I carry orders of the Archon. If I am on that span tomorrow, I am the escort.”

Kallisto looked at her for a long moment. She didn't soften.

“Then you'll have to decide,” she said, “who you are tomorrow.”

## Chapter 31: The School Above the Sieve

The children came up the ninth-pillar stairs at first light in two lines, holding hands, each with a slate tied round their neck on a string. On each slate, in chalk, was a name and a number. Some of the numbers were very small.

Demetrios walked at the front. He had argued half the night with the Synod guard sergeant who'd come down with the order, and lost, and his face was grey with it. At the back walked six guards in white. And in the middle of the escort, where the order said the senior officer should walk, was Prima.

She had not said a word since the cistern.

Nil, Kallisto and the others waited on the eleventh-ring terrace by the near end of the span. They had no right to stop anything. They were excluded persons in a place they were forbidden to stand, and Kallisto had argued, and won, that if they were going to be arrested anyway they might as well be arrested somewhere useful.

The span ran across the inner Sieve: a stone bridge, narrow, pierced with the same holes as everything else, from the eleventh-ring terrace to the examination hall on the far side. Below it the city's honeycomb dropped away through ring after ring into the dark of the undercity. Two-thirds of the way across, on the downstream side, the bridge's parapet was cracked. Niko's crack. In the morning light it was visibly wider than it had been on the terrace wall.

Maeve had been lying on her stomach at the span's foot for an hour, with her ear against the stone.

“It's moving,” she said when Nil crouched beside her. “Not a lot. When the sea comes in, the whole ring flexes, and that bit flexes more. It's been doing it for months. The mortar's gone.”

“Will it hold forty children?”

“It held Persephone. It held the guards coming down this morning.” Maeve sat up. Her face was very calm, which was how Nil knew she was frightened. “It'll hold until it doesn't. That's what that sort of crack does.”

The two lines of children reached the span. The sergeant at the front, a lean man called Lysander with a sergeant's stripe on his sleeve, held up a hand and the lines stopped.

Prima stepped out of the escort.

“Sergeant,” she said. “Halt the transfer. The eleventh-ring span is unsound. I reported it on the fourth terrace yesterday afternoon with chalk and date. The undercity council has reported it since spring.”

Lysander looked at her as if she had spoken in another language. “The span was inspected.”

“The span is cracked.”

“The order specifies this route.” He held it up, the temple's white wax glinting. “By the Archon's seal. You're the senior escort, Shield. If you're countermanding the Archon's order, I'll need it in writing, and the temple will need to know why.”

Prima stood very straight. Nil could see her hand on the shield's strap.

“I am ordering a halt,” she said. “On my authority as senior escort. Until the span is braced.”

“And I'm refusing it,” said Lysander, not unkindly, “on the Archon's authority, which is higher. I'm sorry, Shield. The Sieve begins at the second bell.” He turned. “Line one. Walk.”

The first line of children stepped onto the span.

***

Afterward, nobody could agree exactly when it started. Maeve said the first sound was the mortar going, a dry trickle like sand in a glass. Niko said Persephone, who had been tethered at the terrace's edge, started bleating before anything happened at all. Kallisto said she saw the parapet lean before she heard it.

Nil remembered the children. The first line was about halfway across, a dozen of them in single file holding hands, Demetrios at the front, when the span gave a groan so deep that Nil felt it in their teeth, and the cracked section of parapet tilted outward like a door swinging open, and the walkway beneath it sagged a hand's width and stopped.

Twelve children stood on a piece of bridge that was no longer level, over a drop of eleven rings.

Nobody screamed. That was the worst part. The children were too frightened to scream. They stood absolutely still, holding hands, staring down through the holes at their feet.

Lysander had gone white. “Back,” he said, and then louder, “Back! Line one, back!”

“*Don't*,” Maeve shouted. “Don't let them move. If they all move at once on that section, it'll go.”

The children stood frozen. The span groaned again. The sagging section dropped another finger's width.

And Prima walked onto the bridge.

She didn't run. She walked, quickly and evenly, the way you walk on ice, along the near half of the span to where the sag began. She unslung her shield as she went. When she reached the edge of the broken section she knelt, set the shield's rim against the walkway, and did something Nil had never seen anyone do with a shield.

She opened it.

The bronze face split along lines Nil hadn't known were there, and unfolded: once, twice, three times, the segments locking edge to edge, until what had been a round shield on her arm was a wide flat plate of bronze laid across the walkway like a section of floor. Its inner face glowed with a faint blue light, the colour of the enamel 2 on its front, and where it touched the sagging stone the stone stopped moving.

Lysander stared at her.

“That's the temple's shield,” he said. “You're not authorised to deploy it for—”

“I am deploying it,” said Prima, without looking round. Her arms were braced against the shield's back edge. They were already shaking. “Demetrios. Bring them across the plate. One at a time. Slowly.”

***

It was not enough.

Nil saw that within a minute, and so did everyone else. The shield held the sagging section level. It did not hold it up. Prima was holding it up, with her arms and her own channel. The blue light pulsed in time with her breathing. Every child who stepped onto the plate made the light flicker and her arms shake harder.

Three children across. Four. The span groaned.

“She can't hold it for twelve,” Maeve said hoarsely. “She'll be able to hold it for maybe six.”

“Then she needs more weight behind it,” said Nil.

They looked at Shunya.

Shunya had her pack open on the terrace and her hands inside it, on the sealed sleeve of the protected equation. She looked back at Nil, and Nil saw her understand.

“It's not tested at this scale,” she said.

“It's twelve children,” said Nil. “Not a country. Twelve, and one shield, and a few of us. You said small, consenting, defined claims.”

Shunya took a breath and let it out.

“Named sources,” she said. “Named target. Everyone who stakes says so out loud, says how much, and can stop. The target is Prima's shield channel. Nothing goes anywhere else. Nothing goes to the children's accounts. It's just—” She was already pulling the brass instrument case out of the pack. “It's just holding.”

She looked up at the people on the terrace.

“I need volunteers,” she said. “Small amounts. Ten units each. You won't feel it much. It comes back when the claim closes, if it closes. If it doesn't close, you've lost ten units.”

“I'll stake,” said Kallisto immediately. “Kallisto of the ninth pillar, ten units, to the shield.”

“Jinling,” said Jinling. “Ten.”

“Vesper Sette,” said Vesper. “Ten.” Her voice was steady and her hands weren't.

Maeve. Bai Si. Hina, who said *Hina* and then, after a pause, *of the Ninety-Nine*, and *ten*, and gripped her umbrella. Two forge workers from the undercity who had followed Kallisto up. Niko's uncle. Niko tried to volunteer and three adults said no at once.

Nil didn't stake. They couldn't. They had no allocation to give; NaN didn't have ten units to name. What they had was the decoder, and the instrument, and the corrected model that Shunya had built from their signature.

“The decoder can carry it,” Shunya said. “It's what the comparison showed. You're an account that answers to everyone's fractions. You can route between them. With consent.”

“With consent,” said Nil, and put their wrist into the instrument's cradle.

It didn't feel like the stepwell test, or the cradle under the Dial. It felt like holding many small hands at once. Ten units from Kallisto, rough and warm. Ten from Jinling, precise as a column of figures. Vesper's, quick and nervous. Hina's, which felt like rain on paper. Each one named, each one counted, each one passing through the crack in the brass ring at Nil's wrist and out along a line Shunya had chalked on the stone, to the bronze plate under Prima's hands.

The blue light steadied.

Prima looked up, startled, as her arms stopped shaking.

“Bring them,” she said.

Demetrios brought them. One at a time, holding hands with the next child back so that nobody was ever alone on the plate. Five. Six. Seven. The span groaned and the light held. Eight. Nine. A girl of about seven stopped in the middle of the plate and couldn't move, and Prima, without taking her hands off the shield, began to recite something in a low steady voice, the primes, two three five seven eleven thirteen, and the girl started saying them too, and walked, step by step, a prime per step, to the far side.

Ten. Eleven. Twelve.

Demetrios came last.

When his foot left the plate, Prima let go.

The shield folded itself back into a round with a sound like a closing book. The cracked section of the span, with nothing holding it, groaned one last time and dropped away into the Sieve. It fell for a long time. When it hit something far below, the sound came up through the holes like distant thunder.

On the terrace, the instrument's needle swung back. Nil felt the small hands let go, one by one, and the units go home to their names. All of them. The claim had closed.

Shunya was crying, quite openly, with her hands still on the case.

“It worked,” she said. “It worked for twelve.”

***

It did not work for anything else. They found that out within the hour.

The second line of children was still on the near terrace. Kallisto wanted the inner span closed and braced, and the other spans across the inner Sieve inspected before any of them were used. The undercity's materials were down in the undercity. The upper city's materials were in the temple stores, behind the temple's locks.

“Can you do it again?” Kallisto asked Shunya. “Bigger? Hold the inner ring up while we brace the other spans?”

Shunya tried. She set up the instrument with the same volunteers and a wider target, the inner ring's main support channel, and Nil put their wrist in the cradle. The small hands came. The units went out along the chalk line.

And stopped.

There was a wall. Nil could feel it, at the end of the line, as plainly as a locked door: a channel that wouldn't accept anything that didn't come through the temple. A single round socket of authority, engraved, exclusive.

“It's the monopoly,” Shunya said. She pulled the needle back before anyone's units could be lost against it. “The ring's support channel goes through the Synod's control. Through the credential. You can hold a shield, because the shield is Prima's. You can't hold a city, because the city is locked.”

“So it's a toy,” said Lysander. He was standing on the terrace with his guards, looking at the place where the span had been.

“It saved twelve children,” said Kallisto, without turning round. “Don't call it a toy in front of me, Sergeant.”

He was quiet.

***

Prima sat on the edge of the terrace with her folded shield across her knees and her arms trembling. Nobody had told her to sit. She had simply stopped standing.

Kallisto came and stood over her.

“You disobeyed your sergeant,” she said.

“I disobeyed the Archon.”

“You did.”

Prima looked up. “Tell me what to do next,” she said. “Not as an order. I don't— I can't tell any more which of my orders are mine.”

Kallisto considered her for a long moment.

“The second line goes back down to the school,” she said. “You walk them. The span's closed: you post a guard on it, one you trust, and a sign. Then you come down to the ninth pillar and you give the council your access logs. Every storm. Every stair. Every family you turned away. Written down.”

Prima nodded slowly. “For a hearing.”

“For a record. Whether there's a hearing depends on the Archon.” Kallisto didn't soften. “I'm not forgiving you for the eighth-pillar stairs because you held a bridge. Ione's hand doesn't work any better this morning.”

“I know.”

“Good.” Kallisto held out her hand. After a moment Prima took it and let herself be pulled up. “Then go and walk the children home, Shield-bearer. We'll talk about what you are later.”

Prima went. She didn't look back. At the head of the second line, she knelt and spoke to each child in turn, by name, reading the names off their slates. When she reached the end of the line she stood, and took her own place at the back, behind the smallest one, and walked them down.

## Chapter 32: A Thread That Measures

“The Archon won't hear the council,” Kallisto said, “unless the council brings him something he can't call composite.”

They were in the ninth-pillar cistern again, the morning after the span. Prima's access logs lay on the bench in a stack as high as a hand, written out overnight in her square, exact writing: every storm, every stair, every name. She had not slept. Nobody had asked her to read them aloud, and she hadn't offered.

“Such as?” asked Nil.

“The founding thread.” Kallisto unrolled a map of the headland drawn on the back of an old pillar ledger. Below the city, below the undercity, below the waterline, a cave was marked with three small circles. “When Kroton was built, before the Synod, before the rings, the city's first compact was spun, measured and cut by the keepers in the sea cave. Everyone's share of the city's protection, measured against everyone else's. Not ranked. Measured. The Synod says the Sieve is the compact made visible. The keepers have never said so.”

“And if they said otherwise,” said Jinling slowly, “the Archon would have to hear it.”

“By the oldest rule in the city.” Kallisto rolled the map. “But the keepers don't give anything to anyone who asks. You go down, you do their trial, and they decide what you've earned.”

Prima looked up from the logs.

“I've never been,” she said. “Shield-bearers aren't permitted. The Synod says the cave is superstition.”

“Then you'll be the first,” said Kallisto, “to find out if it is.”

***

The sea cave lay at the bottom of a long tunnel the undercity used for nothing but this. The walls were older than any stone in the city above. They were smooth and wet and lined, at intervals, with niches holding small clay lamps that somebody kept lit. The sound of the sea came up the tunnel in long slow breaths.

At the bottom the tunnel opened into a round chamber half-flooded with green water. A ledge ran around it, and on the ledge sat three women.

The first was young, or seemed young, with her hair loose and a spinning wheel in front of her. The wheel turned on its own, slowly. The thread it drew out was pale and fine and ran up into the dark of the ceiling.

The second was middle-aged and sharp-faced, with a measuring rod across her knees marked in a system Nil didn't recognise.

The third was old, very old, with a pair of iron shears in her lap and a basket beside her full of short lengths of red ribbon.

“Clotho,” Kallisto said, very quietly. “Lachesis. Atropos.”

The young woman looked up from her wheel.

“You've come for a measurement,” she said. Her voice was mild and precise. “Before we do anything, I'll tell you how this works, and then I'll show you one example. If you don't understand the example, say so, and I'll show you another. People get hurt here when they nod at instructions they haven't understood.”

“That's sensible,” said Maeve.

“It took us a long time to learn,” said Clotho.

***

The trial was a sequence.

Clotho's wheel had three spindles. Each one, when it turned, drew out a length of thread marked with a digit. The digits had to be stopped in order, by different people, each person stopping one spindle, so that the thread passed from hand to hand without slack and without breaking.

“If one of you stops too early, the thread tangles,” Clotho said. “Too late, it snaps. It is not a test of luck. It is a test of whether you can act together.” She showed them: she stopped the first spindle herself, and Lachesis the second, and Atropos the third, and the thread ran from one to the next smooth and taut, three digits marked on it in a row. “Like that. Questions?”

“What happens if it snaps?” asked Vesper.

“We spin another,” said Clotho. “Nobody is cut for a snapped thread.”

Atropos, who hadn't spoken, lifted her shears an inch and set them down again.

“Not here,” she said. Her voice was dry and plain. “I cut what is meant to be cut. Not people for mistakes.”

Nil and Prima took the first attempt, with Hina. It snapped. Hina stopped her spindle early in her eagerness, Prima stopped hers exactly on the count she'd been taught for shield drills, and Nil, caught between them, stopped late. The thread pulled, frayed, parted.

Clotho spun another, unhurried.

They tried again with Bai Si instead of Hina. It tangled. Prima and Bai Si were both too careful, each waiting for the other.

“You're each doing your part correctly,” Clotho observed, “and the thread doesn't care.”

The third time Nil stopped thinking about their own spindle and watched Prima's hands. Prima, after a moment, watched theirs. When she stopped hers, Nil was already moving, and when Nil stopped theirs, Vesper's hand came down on the third without a word, and the thread ran out smooth and taut between them: 6, 7, 42.

Forty-two. The third spindle had marked two digits.

“That's not a digit,” said Nil.

“No,” said Clotho, pleased. “Sometimes a sequence produces something larger than a single place can hold. Watch my sister.”

Atropos lifted the length of thread marked 42, held it up between her fingers, and with one snip of the shears cut it neatly in two. Not in half. At a point she seemed to measure by eye. On one piece, the mark now read 6. On the other, 7.

“Six times seven,” she said. “Forty-two is made of them. When a number won't fit where you need it, you can break it into what made it. Not into anything. Into its factors.” She laid the two pieces down side by side. “Then each fits a place.”

Nil looked at the two pieces of thread. Six and seven. They'd stopped a six and a seven themselves, a moment ago. The forty-two had come from them, and now went back into them.

“You can do that with any number?” they asked.

“With any number that is made of smaller ones. You cannot break a prime.” Atropos glanced at Prima's shield, at the blue 2. “That is not a judgment on primes. It is only what they are.”

Prima looked at the 2 on her own shield for a long moment.

***

Lachesis spoke for the first time when the sequence trial was done.

“The second trial is a measurement,” she said. “I measure what holds between two people. I do not rank it. I do not say it is good or bad. And I do not do it unless both of them agree, and I tell them first exactly what the thread will record, and where the record goes.”

She held up her rod.

“It records the tension between you. Where you pull together and where you pull apart. Nothing else. The record stays here, witnessed by my sisters, unless the two of you agree together to take it out.” She looked from Nil to Prima. “The thread for this city's compact is one of these, measured long ago between everyone who built it. If you want me to tell your Archon what that thread says, I need to know you can be measured honestly yourselves. Will you?”

Nil looked at Prima.

Prima looked at the floor. “I'm not sure what it will say,” she said.

“Neither am I,” said Nil. “I'll agree if you will.”

Prima was quiet for a long time. Then she nodded.

Lachesis drew out a length of Clotho's pale thread and gave one end to Nil and the other to Prima.

“Hold it,” she said. “And talk. About something you disagree on.”

They looked at each other.

“The shelters,” Nil said, after a moment. “The ones the Synod built. On the terraces. You said everyone goes down in a storm.”

“Everyone does.”

“Except the people on the stairs. Except the Kostas family.”

Prima's hands tightened. The thread went taut between them.

“The shelters work,” she said. “They're real. I've counted three hundred people into the fourth-terrace shelter in a single night and every one of them came out in the morning. The Synod built them. Good engineers built them. They're not a lie because some of the orders around them were wrong.”

“I didn't say they were a lie.”

“You implied—”

“I said they excluded people. Both things are true.” Nil heard their own voice rising and stopped. They thought of Bāfā. Of how easy it was to say the clean thing. “I'm not saying the Synod is all bad. I'm saying you can't use the good shelters to make the stairs not have happened.”

Prima stared at them. The thread between them hummed.

“No,” she said finally, very low. “I can't.” She took a breath. “And you can't use the stairs to make the shelters not work. The people on the fourth terrace need them. Whatever comes after the Synod needs to keep them.”

“Yes,” said Nil. “It does.”

The thread went slack, just slightly. Not loose. Easy.

Lachesis made a mark on her rod.

“Disagreement,” she said, “held without breaking.” She looked up. “That's what the city's compact thread says too, if you're wondering. Everyone who built Kroton pulled in different directions. It held because none of them let go. Nothing in it ranks anyone above anyone.” She coiled the measured thread neatly. “I will witness that to your Archon. And I will keep this thread, yours, here, until the day you both decide what it's for.”

***

Atropos's trial was the shortest.

“A measurement must end in a cut,” she said, “or it never closes. I am required to cut something, to complete what you've done here.” She held up the shears. “People often think the cut must hurt to be honest. They offer me something precious. A proof. A memory. A promise. Once, a young man offered me his own thread, because he thought it would make his petition stronger.” She shook her head. “It did not. It only made him dead.”

She reached into the basket and lifted out a short length of plain red ribbon.

“This,” she said, “is what I cut. It is a ribbon. It stands for the trial. It has no other value. You need not give me anything you'd mourn.”

Nil looked at the oilcloth under their arm, at the four Seals, at the notebook. At the thread Lachesis had coiled. They had half expected, coming down here, to be asked for something unbearable.

“That's all?” they said.

“That's all. The trial is real. The cost is declared. Nobody's life is the price of a hearing.” Atropos held the ribbon out between two fingers. “Witness it, all of you.”

They witnessed. She cut. The two halves of the ribbon fell into the basket with the others.

“Done,” she said. “Your trial record is with Lachesis. Go and tell your Archon.”

***

On the long climb back up the tunnel, Prima walked beside Nil without speaking for a long time.

“The 2,” she said at last. “On my shield. The Synod gave it to me. They said it was an honour. The only even prime. The one that holds the others up.”

“Is it?”

“It's a number.” She touched the enamel. “Atropos is right. You can't break it. That's all it is.” She was quiet for a few steps. “I folded my old uniform last night. Before I wrote the logs. I didn't throw it away. I folded it and put it on the shelf.”

“Why?”

“Because I was it.” Prima looked straight ahead up the tunnel. “I don't get to pretend I wasn't.”

Behind them, in the cave, the wheel went on turning, drawing pale thread up into the dark.

## Chapter 33: The First Gear

The gallery of old parts was under the temple, and it smelled of rust and lamp oil and a hundred years of patient work.

It had been a storeroom once. Now it was the undercity's archive of everything the upper city had thrown away when it rebuilt the rings: toothed wheels taller than a person, lengths of shaft, brass collars, plates of iron with rows of holes punched in patterns. The workers had sorted all of it. Every piece had a tag, and every tag had a number, and the numbers referred to a row of ledgers on a shelf by the door. The ledgers went back further than anyone in the gallery had been alive.

Maeve walked in and stopped.

Nil had seen her stop like that once before, at Ofuku's, over her grandmother's supplier mark. She stood in the doorway with her hands at her sides and looked at the great black gears ranked along the walls, and her face was the face of someone in a church.

“These are all the same family,” she said. “Look at the teeth.”

Nil looked. To them every tooth was a tooth. To Maeve, apparently, they were handwriting.

She went straight to the far end of the gallery, past the newer pieces, to a gear so large it had been laid on its side on the floor and used as a table. Someone had put a lamp and a pot of grease on it. Maeve moved the lamp and the pot very carefully to the floor, then knelt and ran both hands round the rim.

“This one's the oldest,” she said. “It's the first.”

“How can you tell?” asked Prima.

“Wear.” Maeve pressed her thumb into a tooth. “Every gear after this was cut to match this one. You can see where the later ones were trued to it. This is the master.” She looked up at Kallisto. “Is there a tag?”

Kallisto checked. “Forty-one. Ledger one.”

The first ledger was so old its cover had been replaced twice. The entry for item forty-one was in a cramped brown hand Nil could barely read, and Kallisto read it aloud slowly.

“*Item forty-one. Master drive gear, first engine. Removed from service in the rebuilding of the third ring. Repaired: teeth eleven through fourteen re-cut by Theron of the Smithy, as the record shows.*” She turned the page. There was a date, in the old reckoning. “TR seven hundred and two.”

Shunya made a small sound. “Seven hundred and two. The Comptroller's office was founded—”

“In nine eighty-five,” said Jinling. “When Thale's predecessor died. I read the dates in my father's archive.”

“So this machine's three hundred years older than Thale's office,” said Nil.

“At least.” Maeve was still kneeling at the gear. “And it's not a Kroton machine. It's not Synod. Look at the stamp.” She wiped a patch of grease away with her sleeve. Under it, small and clean, was a maker's mark: three linked spirals.

Nil had seen that mark. On a rubbing in Ofuku's workshop. On a supplier slip pinned to a forge door in the Meridian Bazaar.

“That's your grandmother's mark,” they said.

“That's my family's mark,” said Maeve. “My grandmother inherited it. So did her grandmother. All the way back.” Her voice was very quiet. “Somebody in my family cut this gear in TR seven hundred and two, for an engine that was already old enough to need repairs. In Kroton. Not Meridian.”

“For what engine?”

Nobody knew.

***

Bai Si found the other thing.

He had wandered down the row of ledgers while the others crowded round the gear, reading the spines, and somewhere in the middle of the row he'd stopped beside an old worker who was oiling a shaft. Nil saw him bend his head to listen. A minute later he was smiling.

Nil had never seen Bai Si smile like that: openly, without any of the careful stillness he wore like his white robes.

“What is it?”

“This man's grandfather came from the River,” Bai Si said. “A rope-walk family from the docks at Bāfā. He tells me his grandfather used to say the best rope-maker on the river was a man who left the working end too long, because someone after him would need it for a repair.” He looked at the old worker, who nodded, pleased to be listened to. “And who always tucked one extra turn under the standing part, so it would spread the load.”

Nil felt something catch in their chest.

“Wen,” they said.

“His grandfather never knew the name. Only the knot.” Bai Si took out his travelling book and opened it beside the man's shaft. “And the knot is wrong, in the telling. He says two turns. Wen used one.” He was still smiling. “It doesn't matter. He remembered the reason.”

He wrote it down: the worker's name, his grandfather's story, the knot with two turns. Beside it, in brackets, he wrote *(one turn, per Wen's work sample)*, and didn't cross anything out.

“You're not going to correct him?” asked Nil.

“I've written down both,” said Bai Si. “That's the correction.”

***

The testimony took most of the afternoon.

The undercity council meant to present it at the access court the next morning, and Kallisto wanted everything in order: Prima's access logs, the failed school order, the span's collapse, Lachesis's witness of the founding thread, the gear and its dated repair log. She wanted each piece presented by the person who had a right to present it.

“Not by the travellers,” she said firmly. “You'll leave. We'll still be here.”

Prima helped. Nil watched her do it and saw how hard it was for her. She knew the temple's forms, its procedures, its order of precedence, and she kept starting to say *it should be done like this*, and stopping herself, and saying *the temple usually expects it like this. Do you want to do it that way?*

Usually the council said no. Once Demetrios said yes, and Prima looked so relieved that Kallisto laughed at her.

“You can tell us how the temple thinks,” Kallisto said. “That's useful. You don't get to tell us what to say.”

“I know.” Prima folded her hands. “I'm learning.”

It was Jinling who found the financing reference.

She was reading the first ledger's later pages, because she couldn't leave a ledger alone any more than Maeve could leave a hinge, and she came to an entry from much later in the old reckoning: a payment for maintenance of the drive train, made to the undercity smithy through an intermediary. The intermediary's mark was a double ring. One line thicker than the other, where a printing plate had slipped.

Jinling went very still. Then she held the page out to Vesper without a word.

Vesper took it. Nil watched her face, and saw it close.

“That's the House's mark,” Vesper said. “The Crossroads. It's on every contract they've ever written. Including the one on the table where I rigged that hand.” She handed the ledger back carefully. “Somebody at the House has been paying for the maintenance of this machine. For a long time.”

“The same machine as the Sieve?” asked Shunya.

“The same family,” said Maeve. “The Sieve's drive train is made of these.” She touched the first gear. “And the Basilica's, probably. And whatever else they built.”

There was a long silence in the gallery.

“Which means the trail goes to the Crossroads,” Jinling said.

“Which means the trail goes to my table,” said Vesper.

She stood for a moment with her hands in her coat pockets. Nil saw her right hand close round something: the empty cartridge cases she still carried, or the coin she turned when she was thinking.

“Before we leave Kroton,” she said, “I'm going to tell you all of it. Mara, the table, who I owed and what it cost and what might be waiting for me out there. Not now. When the hearing's done. So that you can decide whether you still want to come.”

“We'll still want to come,” said Hina at once.

“You don't know that yet.” Vesper smiled, a little crookedly. “That's the point of telling you.”

***

Shunya had left the gallery once the financing reference turned up. She spent the rest of the afternoon at the Kroton academy, at the top of the city, with an astronomer who had read Vidya's eclipse report and wanted to know whether his own instruments had been leaned on.

She came back down at dusk, white-faced, with her brass case under her arm and a sheaf of figures.

“They have,” she said, before anyone asked. “For months. Not like the eclipse engines. Quieter.” She knelt on the gallery floor beside the first gear and spread the figures out. “Every reading the academy has taken this year has a narrower spread than last year's. The tides, the wind, the market prices, the births. Everything varies less. Not because the world changed. Because something is pressing it toward the middle.”

“Even Hand,” said Jinling.

“That's the name on the order.” Shunya touched the figures. “It doesn't stop things happening. It makes the good and bad outcomes closer together. Fewer storms that miss you. Fewer storms that hit you hard. Everything a little more like everything else.” She looked up. “On the terraces, that feels like safety. Down here—” she glanced at the pillars, the braces, the cracked supports “—it means nothing gets better by surprise either. Nobody gets lucky.”

“Is that so bad?” asked Prima, quietly. “Fewer disasters?”

Shunya considered that seriously, which Nil was grateful for.

“Not on its own,” she said. “If everyone chose it. If it were shared. But it isn't shared. The academy's protected routes have narrowed from the bottom. The undercity's have narrowed from the top.” She tapped the two columns. “It cuts off the best that could happen to them and keeps the worst. It's a ceiling. Not a floor.”

Nobody said anything for a while.

“I'm writing this down,” Shunya said at last. “With the uncertainty. And I'm sending it to Vidya.” She looked at Nil. “Not your signature. Just the academy's tides.”

***

Niko came down the gallery stairs an hour later, two at a time, with Persephone dragging behind him on her rope.

“There's a captain,” he said, out of breath. “At the upper gate. A Collector. With a paper. She came on the coast road this afternoon with two men chained together. She asked the gate guard for the shield-bearer and the travellers with the broken number.”

Nil looked at Vesper. Vesper looked at the stairs.

“Brask,” said Nil.

“She's got a paper this time,” said Vesper. “Not a clipboard. That's the fresh order.”

Prima stood up.

“She will not execute it tonight,” she said. “Not in Kroton, not without presenting it to the Archon's court. That's the law here. A Meridian order is received by the Synod first.” She paused. “The court sits tomorrow. For the council's petition.”

“So she'll be there,” said Kallisto.

“She'll be there.” Prima looked at Nil. “With a warrant. At the same hearing where you're a witness for the undercity.”

Nil looked down at the first gear on the gallery floor. The old cut teeth. The three linked spirals. The repair that someone had made three hundred years ago, so that the machine would keep turning for people they'd never meet.

“Then I'll be a witness first,” they said. “And find out what I am after.”

## Chapter 34: What We Refuse to Cut

The Archon's access court sat at the very centre of the Sieve, on a round marble dais at the top of the city, where every ring could see it.

From the dais, Nil could look straight down through the honeycomb: terrace below terrace, hole below hole, olive trees and washing and the dark of the undercity at the bottom. The dais itself was pierced like everything else. Under its floor, through a ring of round openings, Nil could see the top of a great iron shaft turning slowly, and the teeth of gears engaging, and they knew without asking whose family had cut them.

“The drive,” Maeve murmured. “The whole city sits on it.”

The Archon sat on it too.

He was older than Nil had expected, and smaller: a thin, upright man with a white beard and white robes embroidered with mosaic numerals at the hem. Over his head his Civic read 17, clear and steady. He had a scholar's hands, long-fingered and ink-stained, and when the court assembled he was reading a slate of figures and did not look up until he had finished.

Then he looked at Prima.

“My student,” he said. “You've brought me a petition from the undercity.”

“The council has brought it, Archon.” Prima stood with the residents, not in front of them. Her shield was on her back. She wasn't wearing her white tunic; she wore a plain grey one Kallisto had found her. “I'm a witness.”

“Ah.” The Archon set his slate down. “A witness. Very well.”

Behind him, at the edge of the dais, stood Captain Brask with her two linked officers and a sealed paper in her hand. She caught Nil's eye and nodded, very slightly, as one professional to another.

***

The council presented its case the way Kallisto had planned it, each piece by the person who had the right.

Demetrios read the school transfer order and then described the span: the crack, the reports, the collapse. Forty-one children named. Twelve carried across a folded shield. Niko's uncle read the spring inspection request that had been refused. Three market women read, in turn, the pillar ledgers: decades of supports, braces and repairs, every one carried out by undercity hands and recorded by undercity clerks.

Prima read her own access logs.

She read every entry. Every storm. Every stair. Every name she had turned away. When she reached the eighth-pillar stairs, two winters ago, and the names Andreas and Ione Kostas and their two daughters, eleven and seven, her voice didn't change. Kallisto, standing beside her, did not look at her and did not move away either.

Then Lachesis's witness was read, by Kallisto, from a sealed leaf the keeper had sent up: *The founding compact of Kroton was measured as tension held between all who built it. It records no rank. It records no sieve.*

The Archon listened to all of it. He did not interrupt. When it was finished, he folded his hands.

“This is a moving account,” he said. “I don't doubt its sincerity. Let me explain why it changes nothing.”

He gestured downward, through the floor, at the turning shaft.

“The Sieve works,” he said. “Look. Every ring stands. Every shelter holds. In forty years no household on the upper terraces has been lost to a storm. That is not doctrine. That is engineering. You can measure it.”

“The undercity built it,” said Kallisto.

“The undercity maintains it,” said the Archon calmly. “Under supervision. And the Sieve arranges the city so that what cannot be broken carries what can. That is also engineering. A structure must rest on its strongest members.” He looked at the residents with something almost like kindness. “The composite are not lesser people. They are differently constituted. They are housed according to their structure. When they are moved up, the city weakens. When the span failed, it failed in the composite ring.”

“It failed,” said Maeve, “because nobody braced it.”

“It failed because it carried a load it was not ranked for.” The Archon's voice didn't rise. “The petition is denied. The Sieve proceeds tomorrow. The school order will be reissued by a safer route. The undercity's maintenance will be commended in the record.” He turned. “Captain. You have a Meridian order to present?”

Brask stepped forward. She did not unseal the paper.

“I have, Archon. A warrant for the person called Nil. I'm required to present it to the Synod before execution.” She held it out. “I'll note that the person is currently a witness in a petition before your court.”

The Archon took the warrant and laid it on his slate without opening it.

“Then the Synod receives it,” he said. “After the court is closed.”

Nil stepped forward.

“The council asks for a review,” they said. “Under the old rule. If the court denies a petition from the undercity, the undercity can ask for a review by Audit. A bounded one. On the Archon's control of the Sieve's access.” They held up a leaf: the rule, copied by Prima from the temple's own book the night before. “Kallisto gave me leave to ask on the council's behalf. I'm not asking for myself.”

The Archon looked at the leaf. Then at Prima.

“You copied this.”

“From your own shelf, Archon.” Her voice was steady. “You taught me where to find it.”

For a moment something passed over the old man's face that was not doctrine at all. Then it was gone.

“Very well,” he said. “A review. Under the old rule. Bounded to the court's access stake.” He rose. “But you should know what you're asking for. The Sieve doesn't only sort the city.”

***

Three wheels rose out of the dais floor in front of Nil, polished white marble with mosaic numerals on their faces. Behind them, Prima stood with her shield. Vesper and Jinling took the flanks.

The Archon raised one hand, and the wheels changed.

Nil watched it happen. Tiles slid out of the marble faces, *click, click, click*, and fell away into the floor below. The 4 went. The 6. The 8. The 9. Every face that could be divided by something smaller dropped out of the wheels, and what was left spun on: 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7.

“The Sieve,” said the Archon, “removes what can be broken. You'll find your options narrower.”

He wasn't lying. Nil stopped a 3 and a 5 and a 7 on the first round, an honest Bust, and his answering strike came down like a dropped slab. Nil caught it on a 0 and felt the parry jar every bone in their arm. On the second round they tried for a Pair, stopped a 5, watched the next wheel come round and saw how few faces there were to choose from, and missed, and stopped a 2.

“Fewer faces makes it easier,” said Vesper through her teeth, at their shoulder. “Doesn't it? Less to aim at.”

“It makes it easier to aim,” said Jinling. “It makes the patterns harder. No four, no six, no eight. No runs past three. No sums to ten without a seven.”

“Seven, three, zero,” said Nil.

“If you can get all three.”

They got a seven and a three. The third wheel came round with its gaps and its six faces, and Nil stopped the zero, and the Zero-Sum closed the Archon's next action before it began. For a moment he looked surprised.

Then he sieved again. The 0 dropped out. Then the 1.

“What's left?” Hina called from the edge of the dais.

“Two, three, five, seven,” said Shunya, very quietly. “He's taken everything but the primes.”

The Archon's next strike came. Nil had no zero to parry with. They stopped a 7, Fortune, their own lucky number, and the strike went back along its path and staggered him. But he steadied, and sieved nothing more, because there was nothing more to sieve.

“Primes,” he said, breathing a little hard. “You see? When everything else is taken away, what remains is what cannot be broken. That's what holds.”

Nil looked at the four faces left on the wheels. Two, three, five, seven. You could make a Jackpot with them. You could make a Pair. No Cascade past three, no Zero-Sum except seven-three-and-nothing.

Not enough. Not against his stake.

Then they thought of the cave. Of Atropos holding up a thread marked forty-two, and cutting it.

*When a number won't fit where you need it, you can break it into what made it.*

The next round, Nil stopped a 5 on the first wheel. Vesper stopped a 7 on the second. And Nil, on the third, stopped a 5 again, and before the scoring frame could close they did what Atropos had shown them: they took the strike as a whole, not the digit. Their base and the 5 together made thirty-five. Thirty-five could be broken.

Five times seven.

They broke it.

The third scoring slot, which had held a single 5, split under their hand into two: a 5 and a 7. The strike's value didn't change. The pattern did. Five, seven, five, seven: four places where there had been three, and two pairs where there had been one. The frame found a pattern it hadn't been able to make a moment before.

The Archon's stake dropped like a stone.

He stared at the wheels. At the 5 and the 7 that had come out of a 35.

“You broke a composite,” he said, very slowly, “into primes.”

“It was made of them,” said Nil. “That's all it ever was.”

***

The rest went quickly. Factor was a single-use trick each round, and Nil spent it carefully, and Vesper and Jinling and Prima did the rest with the faces they had. The Archon's strikes kept coming, and Prima took two of them on her shield and stood. Each round the old man's stake fell further, and each round he sieved the wheels again out of habit, though there was nothing left to sieve.

On the last round, Prima stopped the third wheel herself.

She stopped a 2. The only even prime. It closed a Pair with Nil's 2 on the first wheel, and the Archon's court stake reached zero, and the marble wheels stopped turning and sank back into the floor.

It should have been over.

Nil heard it before anyone else: a change in the sound of the shaft under the dais. The steady grind of the drive faltered. Something under the floor went *clunk*, heavily, like a dropped anchor. Then a long screech of iron on iron.

“The court stake was holding the drive's access,” Maeve shouted. “When it closed, it let go of the—”

The dais tilted.

Not much. A few degrees. The marble floor tipped toward the eastern edge, where the Sieve dropped away in rings below, and everything that wasn't fixed began to slide: slates, papers, a jug of water, the Archon's chair.

The Archon himself had been standing at the eastern edge, where he had stood for the whole Audit, between his chair and the drop.

He took one step back to steady himself. His foot found the edge of the dais and the edge was not where it had been. Nil saw his face change from surprise to understanding in less than a heartbeat.

Then he was gone over the edge.

## Chapter 35: Catch the Falling

Prima was already moving.

Nil saw it in pieces afterward. Prima's shield coming off her back. Prima sliding down the tilted dais on one hip, not running, because you couldn't run on that slope. Prima's arm going over the edge after him, the shield's strap wrapped twice round her wrist, the shield itself unfolding as it went: once, twice, three times.

Then a jolt that dragged her to the very lip of the dais and stopped her there, flat on her stomach, one arm over the edge and the shield's strap cutting white into her wrist.

“I have him,” she said. Her voice was perfectly flat. “I have him. I can't lift him.”

Nil reached her first, because they were closest, and threw themself down beside her and looked over.

The Archon hung below the edge of the dais by one hand clamped on the rim of Prima's unfolded shield. Below him the Sieve dropped away ring under ring, the holes in the terraces looking up at him like eyes. His other hand was scrabbling at nothing. His face had gone grey. Over his head, quite steady, his Civic still read 17.

His court stake was empty. His number wasn't. Nil had time to think, absurdly, *Varro. Same thing. The stake isn't the man.*

“I can't—” said the Archon. His fingers were slipping on the bronze.

“Hold on,” said Prima. “That's an instruction. Hold on.”

The dais groaned. The drive beneath it was still grinding, still wrong. Every few seconds the tilt shifted a little, and Prima and Nil slid a finger's width toward the edge.

“Anchor!” Maeve was shouting somewhere behind. “Someone get a line to the chair base. It's bolted. Bai Si!”

Bai Si was there. He ran a cord from his lantern harness round the bolted base of the Archon's chair and threw the end to Nil, and Nil looped it round Prima's waist and their own, and the sliding stopped.

But the Archon was still hanging, and the shield's strap was still cutting into Prima's wrist, and nobody could reach far enough over the edge to take his weight off her.

“The equation,” Nil said. “Shunya. Like the span.”

Shunya was already on her knees with the case open. Her hands were shaking.

“Named stakes. Named target.” She swallowed. “The target's the shield. Same as before. It's Prima's channel.”

“Then name them.”

They named them. Kallisto, first, without a pause: *Kallisto of the ninth pillar, ten units, to the shield.* Then Demetrios. Then two market women. Then Jinling, Vesper, Hina, Maeve. Then the three undercity riggers who had come up behind Kallisto. Then, after a long moment in which nobody spoke, a guard in white, Lysander, the sergeant from the span, who stepped forward and said, *Lysander of the Synod guard, ten units*, and didn't look at anyone.

And then a voice Nil didn't expect.

“Brask,” said the captain. “Meridian Collections. Ten units. To the shield.”

She was standing at the edge of the tilted dais with her linked officers behind her and the Archon's unopened warrant still lying on his fallen slate at her feet. She didn't look at it.

Nil put their wrist in the cradle and felt the hands arrive: rough, careful, nervous, wet as rain on paper, procedural, fifteen of them, each one named, each one counted through the crack in the brass. The blue light on the shield's inner face came up strong and steady.

Prima's arm stopped shaking.

“Now,” said Kallisto. “Everyone on the line. Pull.”

They pulled. Bai Si and Maeve and the riggers on Bai Si's cord, Vesper and Hina on Nil's belt, Prima's shield arm locked straight. Slowly, a hand's width at a time, the Archon came up over the edge of the dais and lay on the tilted marble on his back, gasping, staring at the sky through the holes in his own city.

Nil felt the fifteen claims close and the units go home, one by one. All of them.

***

Kallisto was the first to stand over him.

“Archon,” she said. “You're in the custody of the witnesses of this court. All of them. Not the Synod. Not me. The witnesses. Until a hearing decides.”

He looked up at her. He looked older than he had an hour ago, and smaller.

“The Sieve—” he began.

“The Sieve's drive is broken,” said Maeve. She was lying on her stomach at the dais's edge, looking down through the floor openings at the shaft. “Not destroyed. Broken. Your court stake was what kept the drive's access exclusive. When it closed, the lock let go, and the drive slipped. Kallisto's people can fix it.” She sat up. “*Theirs*. Not yours. And when they fix it, it won't sort anyone.”

The Archon closed his eyes.

His number still read 17. Nobody had touched it. Nobody could.

“You caught me,” he said to Prima, without opening his eyes.

“Yes.”

“After what I— the order. The span.”

“Yes.” Prima was sitting on the marble cradling her wrist, which had gone purple where the strap had cut. “I caught you because you were falling. That's all. It doesn't mean anything else.” She looked at him. “You taught me to count people down the shelter stairs. Every one. I'm still doing that. I've just started counting the ones you told me to leave out.”

***

The Seal came out of the drive housing an hour later, when Maeve's riggers had shored the dais level and opened the access hatch. It was small and round like the others, and engraved, and it slid out into Kallisto's palm with a soft click: **SIEVE CONTROL · RELEASED · 2.**

Kallisto held it for a moment. Then she held it out to Nil.

“Take it,” she said. “It's a key to a door in Meridian. It's not a key to anything here. The routes stay with us.”

“The routes stay with you,” Nil agreed, and wrapped it, and put it in the oilcloth beside the others: one, nine, eight, zero, two.

Lachesis's witness came up from the cave that evening, carried by the young clerk with ink on her chin. It was the coiled length of pale thread the keeper had measured between Nil and Prima, sealed in a reed tube, and with it a leaf in Lachesis's sharp hand: *Released to Prima of Kroton, with Nil's agreement, for a use they decide together. Record of tension held without breaking.*

Prima took it and held it for a long time.

“What's it for?” she asked.

“I don't know yet,” said Nil. “You keep it. You'll know when.”

***

Maeve asked Nil what they needed at the forge in the ninth pillar, with the first gear cooling in the corner where the riggers had brought it up.

She didn't start with the gear. She sat on an upturned crate across from Nil with her hands on her knees, empty, and said: “I want to make you something. From a piece of that gear. A tooth, maybe. I could make you a lot of things. A brace for the decoder. A guard for your wrist. A charm.” She stopped. “I'm not going to. Not unless you tell me what you want. I did that to Hina's umbrella. I'm not doing it to you.”

Nil thought about it for a long time.

They thought about the horseshoe Gus had given them in the Bazaar, repaired across one end without hiding the join. About the washer in their pocket. About Maeve's grandmother's mark, three spirals, on a gear older than every office in the country.

“A horseshoe,” they said. “Like Gus's. But from your family's gear. Not to make me luckier. Just so that something your family made is with me when we get to the gate.” They hesitated. “And so that whatever happens there, someone can see where it came from.”

Maeve's face did something complicated.

“A horseshoe,” she said. “From the first gear. With the join showing.”

“With the join showing.”

She forged it that night. Nil sat on the crate and watched. It was small, black iron with a single bright line of brass across the bend where she'd welded the cut tooth, and on the inside, very small, three linked spirals.

***

Vesper told them everything on the road out of Kroton, in the back of a paid westbound cart, with the sea going grey behind them.

She told it in order. The table at the Crossroads, the House's mark on the contract. A child's allocation staked by a family that had nothing else to stake. Mara Vale, nine years old, at the next table with her mother. The rigged deck. Vesper's cut of the winnings. The emergency donations. The injury. The school Mara missed. The home her family lost. Seven years.

Then she told them the rest: what she still owed, and to whom. The House's clauses. The people at the Crossroads who knew her face. And Mara, sixteen now, who worked in a mutual-aid office on the main road and had written, once, a single letter through the case correspondence: *You can make restitution. You can't make me meet you.*

“So that's what's out there,” Vesper said when she'd finished. “The House, which is paying to keep these machines running. And people who'd have reason to see me hanged. And a girl I nearly killed, who has every right never to see me again.” She turned the empty cartridge case in her fingers. “Three rounds left. In case anyone's counting.”

Nobody said anything for a while. The cart rattled.

“We're still coming,” said Hina.

“You heard it all?”

“Hina heard it all.” Hina opened her umbrella against a wind that wasn't there. “We're still coming. You don't decide what Mara owes you. And you don't decide what we owe you either.”

Vesper laughed, short and shaky, and looked away at the sea.

***

In Meridian, Ivo brought Captain Brask's Kroton report up with the morning post.

It was short. *Warrant presented to Synod as required. Archon fell from the access court during a bounded review and was caught alive by the party and residents, including this officer, ten units staked. Archon in witness custody. Synod receiving authority suspended pending hearing. Warrant cannot be executed in Kroton; awaiting instruction.* At the bottom, as always, she had added a list nobody had asked for: the forty-one children of the undercity school, by name.

Thale read it at the window. When he reached *including this officer, ten units staked*, he read it again.

“She staked her own allocation,” he said. “On a prisoner's rescue. In front of witnesses.”

“Yes, sir.”

“She'll be asked why at her review.” Thale set the report down square to the edge of the desk. “I imagine she'll tell them.”

He was quiet for a long time. Ivo waited.

“Where are they going?”

“West, sir. The Crossroads. Paid passage.”

“The House.” Thale's face didn't change. “Send a letter to Octavian Nine. Tell him what's coming, and how fast. Tell him the warrant follows by a different hand.” He paused. “Not Brask's.”

Ivo wrote it down. At his own desk, afterwards, he wrote it down again, word for word, with the date and the hour and both their names. Then he took out the copy of the Kroton order: the Sieve of minors, brought forward, the order he had drafted himself. He read it slowly, all the way through.

At the bottom, under his own name as drafter, he added one more line in his own hand: *Forty-one children were on the span this order specified. Twelve were carried across a shield. None were lost.*

He put it in the drawer. The drawer didn't close properly any more. He left it open.
